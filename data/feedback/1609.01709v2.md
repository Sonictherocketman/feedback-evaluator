## [1609.01709v2-general-review] General Review


# Review of "Thermometry for Laughlin States of Ultracold Atoms"

**P.T. Raums and V.W. Scarola, Virginia Tech, arXiv:1609.01709v2 (2021)**

---

## Summary of the paper's argument

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck for ultracold-atom experiments aiming to realize bosonic Laughlin states at filling ν = 1/2: **what entropy does the system need to be cooled to, before correlations characteristic of the Laughlin state can be observed?**

The authors use a heat-capacity peak (the Schottky-type peak at temperature $T_L$) as the thermodynamic marker for "cooled below the relevant scale." Their central claim is that the naïve entropy estimate—treating the system as a non-interacting particle-hole pair continuum with $N$ degenerate modes below the gap, giving a threshold of $S/N \approx 0.29\,k_B$—substantially underestimates the entropy budget. By constructing a three-term ansatz partition function $Z_A$ (Eq. 2) with a gapped low-temperature structure and a cumulant-matched high-temperature tail, and validating it against exact diagonalization on small systems, they find

$$S_L/N \approx 0.799(2),$$

roughly a factor of 3 larger than the naïve estimate. Comparing this to the best publicly reported bosonic entropies ($S/N \approx 0.35\,k_B$ from Ref. [8]), they conclude that cooling below $T_L$ is within current experimental reach.

---

## Detailed critique

### What the paper does well

1. **The question is the right one.** Thermometry in strongly correlated atomic gases is a genuine and unresolved experimental obstacle. Establishing a quantitative entropy budget for the FQH regime is timely and fills a real gap.

2. **The methodology is transparent and verifiable.** The ansatz has only a handful of free parameters ($g_1, g_2, g_3$ plus the cumulant coefficients), and the authors show the high-temperature side reproduces the cumulant expansion to $O(T^{-6})$. The stochastic trace method (Appendix) and finite-size scaling functions are standard and well-implemented.

3. **The honest scope.** The authors are explicit about what is not covered: fermions (deferred), edges, inhomogeneous geometries ($\omega \neq \Omega$), and fillings away from 1. The "Intermediate Temperature Trends" discussion (supplementary, Section on Fig. 6/7) acknowledges that the finite-size scaling fits fail in the intermediate regime and that the validation chain has a gap.

4. **The key number (0.799) is clearly stated** and the comparison with the naïve value (0.29) is the paper's most actionable result.

---

### Major concerns

**1. The "within reach" claim is fragile and under-specified.**

The abstract's headline—that required entropies "are within reach of current cooling capabilities"—rests on comparing the naïve best-atomic-gas entropy $S/N \approx 0.35\,k_B$ (Ref. [8]) to $S_L/N \approx 0.799(2)\,k_B$. The factor of ~2.3 margin sounds encouraging, but:

- The comparison uses the entropy *at the peak* of the heat capacity, which is only a proxy for "below $T_L$." Whether experiments need to be at, below, or well below the peak to resolve Laughlin correlations (e.g., in the pair-correlation function of Ref. [33]) is not quantified. The entropy threshold may be *higher* than $S_L/N$ for any meaningful signal.
- The quoted $S/N \approx 0.35\,k_B$ comes from a *different* geometry (conventional evaporative cooling without flux loading). It is not established that this entropy is achievable in the specific rotation-plus-flux geometry needed for the FQH state—edge state occupancy, inhomogeneous density, and finite trap effects all change the entropy budget.
- No discussion of the time scale: evaporative cooling in a FQH geometry with flux and rotation may not reach the steady-state entropy that the equilibrium calculation assumes.

**2. The ansatz is validated only at low and high temperature; the intermediate regime is not closed.**

The finite-size scaling fits (Eqs. 9–13 in the supplementary) are used to extract the cumulants and to verify $Z_A$ against exact diagonalization. But the authors explicitly state (and Fig. 6 illustrates) that the peak region—the very region of physical interest—shows strong finite-size effects and is *not* accessible to the scaling fits. The argument that the entropy inflection point $S_L'$ (Fig. 7) tracks the entropy at the peak $S_L$ is a reasonable workaround, but it is itself a construct without direct experimental interpretation. The experimentalist asks "at what temperature does the heat capacity peak pass?" while the paper's number tracks "at what entropy does the entropy curve inflect?" These are related but not identical, and the relationship is not tight for gapped systems with broad Schottky peaks.

**3. The continuum approximation is the weakest link and is only qualitatively motivated.**

The core physical assumption—that the high-energy tail of the spectrum (beyond the CF particle-hole pairs) is well modeled by three gaussian-like energy levels with rapidly falling degeneracies ($g_m$)—is motivated by Fig. 1 histograms from boson exact diagonalization at $N=8$ and a reference to large-vortex behavior (Ref. [70]). There is no derivation or quantitative estimate of when $g_m$ "falls off rapidly enough" to make the three-level ansatz complete. The statement "small $g_m$ (but large $E_m$) imply diminishing impact on the thermodynamics" (p. 3) is intuitive but unquantified. For $N=8$, the continuum is barely visible in the spectrum; the claim that the ansatz extrapolates correctly to the thermodynamic limit is a significant extrapolation.

**4. The fermions are not just deferred—they are the more experimentally motivated system.**

The bosonic Laughlin state at $\nu=1/2$ requires an $s$-wave $p$-wave interaction tuned to the pseudopotential regime. Real atomic proposals for FQH physics have historically focused on *fermions* at $\nu=1/3$, where the $p$-wave scattering is stronger and experimentally demonstrated systems exist (e.g., Refs. [5], [17]). Deferring the fermionic calculation to "future work" because it requires a Gröbner basis approach means the paper addresses the less experimentally mature of the two systems. A rough upper bound on $S_L/N$ for fermions—even if not from exact numerics—would make the paper's experimental conclusion more useful.

**5. Edge states are not a footnote issue for trapped gases.**

The entire analysis is for $\omega = \Omega$ (bulk-dominated, edge field cancels trap). In a real trap at $\omega \neq \Omega$, the authors claim edge modes increase $S_L$. But for *neutral* atoms in a harmonic trap as used in most experiments, the edge states are part of the same many-body system—they are not a separate reservoir of additional degrees of freedom that add entropy on top. The relevant question is whether the single-particle edge gap (which is $\omega$-dependent) closes at a lower temperature than the bulk Schottky peak, changing the low-temperature entropy behavior. This is not modeled and could substantially alter the threshold.

**6. The gap used is the transport gap; the thermodynamic gap may differ.**

The paper uses $\Delta_T = 0.627(3)$ for bosons from exact numerics Table I. However, the relevant energy scale for thermal activation is the energy of the lowest single-particle excitation above the ground state, which for contact-interaction Laughlin states may not be identical to the transport gap (which involves two-particle processes). Refs [72–74] are cited for the contact-interaction gap but not compared to the $\Delta_T$ value used in the ansatz. This is a small but potentially important systematic.

**7. The stochastic trace method is applied at $N \leq 12$; the thermodynamic limit is an extrapolation.**

The finite-size scaling fits use $N$ values that are small enough that the log-linear and exponential scaling forms are well-defined. At $N=9$ (the max shown in Fig. 6 top panel), finite-size corrections at the peak are still evident. The claim that "the low-temperature agreement by construction but the low-temperature [sic—high-temperature] agreement demonstrates the accuracy of the ansatz" (Fig. 2 caption) is only as good as the cumulant precision, which is O($T^{-6})$ and depends on the cumulant extraction being correct to that order at finite $N$.

---

### Minor issues

- **Abstract wording**: "stable viability of low temperature FQH states" is ambiguous—does it mean "the FQH state is stable at those temperatures" or "the thermometry is stable/viable"? The sentence is hard to parse.
- **Fig. 1**: The four-panel exact-diagonalization spectrum is useful but the "nearly Gaussian state counting" claim in the caption is only visually supported for $N=8$ bosons; no histogram is shown.
- **The $S_d = N_Q \log(d)$ term** for fillings away from 1: this is taken from Ref. [75] without discussion of whether $\log(d) = \log d$ with $d = \sqrt{p}$ (the degeneracy of the additional quasiparticles causing departure from filling) is the correct expression at finite $T$, or whether there are additional $T$-dependent entropy terms from the quasiparticle dispersion.
- **The supplementary "Intermediate Temperature Trends" section** is valuable but could have been in the main text. The failure of the scaling fits in the intermediate range is precisely where the ansatz is doing its most important work (bridging low-T and high-T limits), and it deserves more prominence.
- **Ref. [48]** (supplementary material) is cited for the naive-model result; the fact that the main text's naïve-estimate of 0.29 comes entirely from that supplementary material makes the comparison somewhat self-referential.

---

### Overall verdict

This is a useful and honest technical contribution to a specific subproblem in the atomic FQH program. The central number ($S_L/N \approx 0.8$ vs. the naïve $0.29$) is physically reasonable and well-motivated even if the full precision of "(2)" error bar overstates confidence. The ansatz partition function is a sensible practical construction, the validation against exact diagonalization is done in good faith, and the scope limitations are stated.

However, the paper would be significantly stronger with: (i) at least a qualitative discussion for fermions; (ii) a more quantitative treatment of the intermediate-T regime where the ansatz is doing its bridging work; (iii) a more careful argument for why the entropy inflection point tracks the experimentally relevant threshold; and (iv) a discussion of how the $S/N \approx 0.35\,k_B$ reference entropy was achieved in what geometry, and whether it transfers to the FQH geometry.

As written, the paper's "within reach" conclusion is the right *direction* but not yet the *precision* that experimentalists need to plan cooling targets.

---

**Originality**: The method (cumulant-matched ansatz + stochastic trace + finite-size scaling) is a standard toolkit applied to a new physical system. The key novelty is the *quantitative finding* (S_L/N ≈ 0.8) for the bosonic FQH thermodynamics, which is genuinely new for this system but is not a conceptual advance.

**Soundness / overall quality**: The physics is correct. The main caveats (intermediate-T gap, small-N validation, single filling, no edges, no fermions) are stated but not resolved. The "within reach" claim is too strong given the caveats.

**Impact**: The paper will be cited by the atomic FQH group (Raums, Scarola and collaborators) in future proposals. It is not field-shaping because it addresses a subproblem (entropy budget at peak) rather than a central question (how to actually *demonstrate* topological order at achievable temperatures), and because the fermion case—the more experimentally prominent FQH system—is absent.

## [1609.01709v2-expert-collegue-review] Expert Collegue Review


# Review

**Paper:** Raum & Scarola, "Thermometry for Laughlin States of Ultracold Atoms" (arXiv:1609.01709v2), 9 pp.

## What the paper does

The authors address a concrete and experimentally urgent question: how low a specific entropy must an ultracold bosonic gas be cooled to in order to reach the regime where Laughlin (fractional quantum Hall) correlations become visible? The proposed metric is the entropy per particle at the heat-capacity peak temperature $T_L$ (the "Schottky" peak from gap activation). Because the relevant thermodynamics live in a temperature regime that is out of reach of exact numerics at useful system sizes (the continuum of high-energy states is only partially resolved at $N\lesssim 12$), the authors construct a small ansatz partition function

$$Z_A = \Bigl(1 + \sum_{m=1}^{3} g_m e^{-E_m/T}\Bigr)^{N}$$

whose parameters are fixed by the first three cumulants $\kappa_l/N$ obtained from an RMT (stochastic trace) evaluation of $\mathrm{Tr}\,H^l$ against a high-temperature $\log Z$ expansion. Validation is against exact diagonalization of $N=8$ bosons in the spherical geometry. The headline number is $S_L/N \approx 0.799(2)$, compared to $\approx 0.29$ from a naive two-level (gap + $N$-fold ground-state degeneracy) model, so the excited continuum raises the entropy budget by roughly a factor of three. The claim is therefore a relaxation of the cooling requirement, i.e., the Laughlin regime becomes reachable with existing evaporative cooling for bosons.

## Strengths

1. **Clear, well-scoped experimental motivation.** The question of whether the experimental entropy budget for bosonic FQH states is sufficient had been open. Reducing it to $S_L/N$ in units of $k_B$ is a clean, decision-relevant deliverable.
2. **Sensible methodology that is transparent.** The combination (high-$T$ cumulant expansion, low-$T$ activated asymptotics, compact ansatz, RMT cumulants, finite-size scaling) is a natural toolkit, and each piece is documented. The paper honestly reports the parameter errors (5% on $\kappa_0$ → 5% on $S_L$, etc.).
3. **Real validation, not just a fit.** Comparing the ansatz against exact diagonalization of $N=8$ across the full $T$ range (Figs. 2, 3, 6) is the right check, and the N→∞ inflection-point trend (Fig. 7) is a nice independent confirmation that the ansatz is not merely interpolating.
4. **Quantification of the naive-model shortfall.** Fig. 3 explicitly shows $s_0$ (naive) diverging from $S$ around $T_L$ by ~$0.4\,k_B$, which is the physically meaningful comparison.
5. **Modest and honest scope** — the authors acknowledge in the main text that intermediate $T$ is where the ansatz does real work, and that edges/quasiparticles are not included.

## Weaknesses and points to press

1. **The factorized ansatz has no microscopic justification.** $Z_A$ is literally a product of $N$ identical single-excitation partition functions, i.e., it assumes the low-lying quasiparticles behave as *non-interacting* excitations of a single-particle spectrum. Low-energy CF particle–hole pairs are strongly correlated and composite; there is no argument (and no demonstration at $N\to\infty$) that the many-body partition function of a composite-fermion Laughlin state factorizes in this way. The fit is also remarkably flexible: six free parameters ($g_1..g_3$, $E_1..E_3$) pinned to four constraints leaves residual room to match any smooth $C(T)$ at $N=8$. The "agreement" in Fig. 2 is therefore not an independent prediction — the model is being asked to interpolate, and the validation is essentially to the right of the fit.
2. **Small validation set.** The only full-$T$ comparison is $N=8$ (one system size). Figs. 5 and 7 lean on finite-size scaling forms (Eqs. 12, 13) that are physically plausible but not established; the $\log(1/N)$ and exponential $e^{\tilde c(T)/N}$ pieces are chosen to make it work. The error bar $0.799(2)$ reflects only the fit-parameter uncertainty — model-form and finite-size uncertainty are not propagated, which makes the headline number look far more precise than it is.
3. **The gapped-limit degeneracy mismatch.** The paper notes $g_1\ge1$ is expected from CF theory but the fit gives $g_1=5.53$. The authors wave through this ("$g_1$ approximately degeneracy of first excitation") without reconciling the factor of ~5×. Similarly $g_3=8.76$ is large relative to a single high-energy shell.
4. **The "reachable with existing cooling" claim mixes systems.** The cooling budget $S/N \approx 0.35\,k_B$ cited for the boson problem comes from Ref. [8], which is a review of *fermionic* evaporative cooling in the Fermi-Hubbard context. Transferring that number to a neutral bosonic gas with a different cooling mechanism is a non-trivial step that is not argued for. The paper also never anchors $\Delta_T/k_B$ to any physical temperature in any proposed geometry, so the final statement is really "$S_{\rm cooling}^{\rm fermion}/k_B < S_L^{\rm boson}/k_B$" compared across systems.
5. **No in-situ entropy- or temperature-measurement protocol.** The title is "Thermometry" but the deliverable is a budget number, not a measurement method. For a paper aimed at an experimental audience, the absence of even a sketch (e.g., how $T_L$ or $S/N$ would be inferred from a pair-correlation / momentum distribution observable) limits direct usefulness.
6. **Spherical vs. disk geometry.** The numerics are in the spherical geometry, but all atomic proposals use a disk. Bulk thermodynamics should converge in the thermodynamic limit, but at $N=8$ the difference is not airtight, and the paper does not quantify it.
7. **Reachability ≠ observability.** Even if $S<T_L$ is achievable, the actual diagnostic of a Laughlin state (entanglement, fractional statistics, edge transport, structure factor) depends on more than entropy. Worth stating more explicitly as a limitation.

## Minor points

- Table I: $\kappa_4/N$ for bosons ($6.8(2)\times10^4$) and $\kappa_5/N$ ($3.3(1)\times10^4$) are suspiciously similar in magnitude and in error bar for cumulants that should grow rapidly with $l$ at high $T$; worth re-checking the RMT convergence at those orders ($R=100$, $N=12$ may be marginal for $\kappa_5$).
- Eq. (11) uses $\kappa_{l\ge 1}$ — the ellipsis with an inequality as a subscript should be $\kappa_{l\ge 1}\to$ or a different notation.
- Fig. 3 caption mentions a horizontal dotted line in the main panel that isn't clearly visible in the figure provided.
- The paper could briefly discuss whether $S_L/N\approx 0.8$ is consistent with the value quoted in the companion Fermi-Hubbard literature once you convert units.

## Bottom line

A competent, well-documented applied-theory paper that answers a real, timely question for the ultracold-atom FQH community, with the right instinct (compare a full-temperature ansatz against small-system exact numerics rather than trusting either the naive gap model or a low-$T$ extrapolation). The methodology is honest and the numerics appear careful. The main limitation is structural: the factorized ansatz has no first-principles justification for the composite-fermion system being modeled, the validation is one system size deep, and the final "we can reach $T_L$" claim leans on a cooling budget borrowed from a different system. The internal logic of the paper supports $S_L/N\approx 0.8\pm(\text{something larger than }0.02)$; the practical reachability conclusion is suggestive but not settled.

## Critique: "Thermometry for Laughlin States of Ultracold Atoms" (Raum & Scarola, PRL 124, 24061, 2021)

### Problem and contribution

The paper tackles the thermometry of fractional quantum Hall (FQH) states in ultracold atoms — specifically, *what entropy (and hence temperature) is required for low-T Laughlin correlations to become visible*, given that atomic gases at their "low" temperature retain a large entropy. The gap to the many-body spectrum sets a Schottky-type heat-capacity peak, and the authors use the entropy at (or below) that peak as a *necessary* criterion. Their main move is an **ansatz partition function** (Eq. 2) that is exact at high T (a cumulant/truncated-free-energy expansion) and gapped-exponential at low T, with a small set of parameters `{g_m, E_m}` fixed by cumulants from exact diagonalization. The headline result: for bosons (ν=1/2), the entropy per particle needed to go below the peak is ≈ 0.35 k_B/N — *within reach* of contemporary evaporative cooling — in contrast to the naive non-interacting two-level model's ≈ 0.29 k_B/N, with the continuum of excited states *lowering* the required peak temperature.

### Strengths

- **Genuine, open gap addressed.** Large-N Laughlin thermometry over the full temperature range did not exist; this is a real need for guiding experiments.
- **Sound and honest validation pipeline.** High-T expansion is reproduced to O(T⁻⁶); the thermodynamic functions (heat capacity, Fig. 2/3) agree with exact diagonalization across the accessible range; a stochastic-trace + finite-size-scaling procedure (Supp. material, Figs. 4–7) is used to carry the N→∞ limit rigorously on the *extensive* cumulants. The model-independence is checked (dropping the m=3 level shifts entropy by <1%; 5% in κ₀ → 5% in S_L).
- **Counterintuitive and useful direction.** Finding that the continuum makes the target *less* extreme than the naive estimate is a genuinely constructive result, not just another "it's hard" bound.
- Clear writing, appropriate caveats about finite size and the T~Δ⊤ breakdown of N-scaling.

### Weaknesses and points to press

1. **The large-N prediction rests on an extrapolation that is only qualitatively validated.** The actual physical claim (S_L/N ≈ 0.8 per particle, and "we're in reach") is a thermodynamic-limit quantity inferred from cumulants fit at N=8. Fig. 7 shows the exact finite-N inflection entropy *trending* toward the ansatz — but it's a modest, qualitative trend, not a hard closure. This is the crux of the paper and its weakest link.
2. **The single-particle gap is an uncontrolled external input.** Δ⊤ is taken from a 2014 contact-interaction result and never varied. Since the entire entropy estimate scales with this gap, a **sensitivity analysis over Δ⊤** (and over sub-pseudopotential interactions, which the authors note change the gap) would substantially strengthen the conclusion. They error-propagate κ₀ but treat the gap as a fixed point.
3. **Only the boson case is fully treated; the experimentally leading case is deferred.** The most competitive FQH-in-atoms proposals are fermionic (ν=1/3, n=1, Fermi-Hubbard lineage), and the paper explicitly reserves fermions for future work because of scattering issues. The "in reach of current cooling" verdict is therefore demonstrated only for the *less* dominant experimental target.
4. **Loose link between diagnostic and observable.** "Entropy below the heat-capacity peak" is used as a proxy for "Laughlin correlations observable." The authors state this is necessary, not sufficient, but the connection is never established. So the headline number attaches to a *diagnostic feature*, not to the actual correlations/fractionalized vortices/edge states one wants to see — which softens the physical punch of the number.
5. **Minors.** The fitted level weights are non-monotonic (g₃≈8.76 > g₁≈5.53 with E₃≈19Δ⊤), and the g₁≈"one CF excitation" is called a first approximation — whether the low-lying quasi-particle manifold's size should grow with N (and thus push S up) is left implicit; the "in reach of evaporative cooling" equation to McKay–DeMarco's 0.35 (a different, non-FQH fermionic system) is asserted with edge and 1/(1+p)-filling corrections discussed but not quantified.

### Verdict

A competent, well-executed benchmark that fills a real thermometry gap with an appropriate (if off-the-shelf) combination of exact diagonalization, stochastic trace, finite-size scaling, and a reasonable high-T/gapped continuum ansatz. It is honest about its limits and its method is standard rather than inventive. Its impact is limited by three self-acknowledged gaps: the N→∞ extrapolation is only qualitatively corroborated, the gap is an unexamined input, the boson-only treatment sidesteps the experimentally central fermionic case, and the diagnostic criterion is a proxy rather than an established observable. A solid stepping stone rather than a field-moving result.

## Stated contribution (in my own words)

The paper is a short thermometry feasibility study for fractional quantum Hall (Laughlin) states of ultracold atomic gases. The central problem: atomic experiments are essentially closed systems, so temperature can only be inferred from the entropy, but in a strongly correlated regime the S–T relation is a many-body question that no existing tool answers over the full temperature range — exact diagonalization (ED) works only for tiny systems; high-temperature cumulant/series expansions only for T far above the gap; composite-fermion (CF) ansatz theory only for T far below it.

The authors' answer is an **ansatz partition function** for neutral bulk excitations of the Laughlin state in spherical geometry,

Z_A = (1 + Σ_{m=1}³ g_m e^{−E_m/T})^N ,

i.e., a product of N three-level Schottky-type systems. It is constructed to be (i) *exact at high T* — the (g_m, E_m) parameters are a Gröbner-basis fit to the first six cumulants of an exact high-temperature expansion, themselves computed with the stochastic trace method (STM) and finite-size scaled to N → ∞; (ii) *correct at low T* — the m=1 term encodes a gap E_1 ~ Δ_T to N CF particle-hole pairs; and (iii) simple enough to use at *all* temperatures. The key physical output: including the observed nearly-Gaussian continuum of high-energy excitations pushes the entropy needed to cool below the Schottky heat-capacity peak up to S_L/N ≈ 0.799 (vs. ≈ 0.29 for a naive two-level gapped model, their supplemental ref. [48]). They also offer a validation protocol: direct low-T/high-T comparison of the ansatz against N → ∞ finite-size-scaled ED (Figs. 2, 3), and a trend of the finite-N entropy-inflexion point toward S'_L (Fig. 7).

## Is it novel given the cited prior work?

**What is *not* new** (all explicitly cited and reused as building blocks):
- The model Hamiltonian and spherical-geometric ED framework (Haldane [59], Trugman–Kivelson [60], Fano–Ortolani [68]).
- High-temperature cumulant series expansions for FQH thermodynamics — done long before for electrons in the 2DEG (Zheng–MacDonald [51], Tevosyan–MacDonald [52], Sawatdiaree–Apel [53]).
- ED heat capacities of small FQH systems ([49], [50]) and low-T CF ansatz thermodynamics (Murthy & Shankar [54], Jain [55], [57]).
- The stochastic trace method itself (generic; Weisse [58]), the contact-interaction gap ([71]), the fermionic entropy-of-reach program it parallels (Fuchs, Paiva, Kozik–Scarola [9]–[12]), and the quasiparticle-entropy cooling argument (Gervais–Yang [75]).
- The "naive gapped model" (two-level Schottky, S/N | peak ≈ 0.29) is a textbook construction presented in the authors' own supplement.

**What *is* genuinely new:**
1. **A closed-form, all-temperature-range Laughlin partition function.** No cited work bridges the high-T cumulant expansion and the low-T CF asymptotics in one simple expression; the multi-level Schottky form matched cumulatively to six STM/finite-size-scaled cumulants is, to the best of my check of the literature, a new construction for this model family. (One should be clear that the m≥2 "levels" are not physical CF excitations but a phenomenological Gaussian-continuum surrogate — the ansatz is an interpolating fit, not a derived partition function.)
2. **The STM-cumulant + finite-size-scaling validation protocol** applied to check an ansatz against N → ∞ ED in both limits — a sensible, genuinely useful methodological contribution for this community.
3. **The physical conclusion itself**: that the excited-state continuum raises the entropy at the Schottky peak to S_L/N ≈ 0.8, which flips the viability judgment for bosonic FQH thermometry relative to the naive estimate. This specific entropy budget, and its comparison to the 0.35 k_B floor of evaporative cooling [8], is a new claim with direct experimental guidance.

**Reservations that temper the novelty:** the result is for one specific interaction (unit pseudopotential contact/p-wave, bosons at ν = 1/2, no edge, disk-sphere bulk). They acknowledge the gap (and hence the peak location) is interaction-dependent [72]–[74] but do not demonstrate robustness; the T ~ Δ_T accuracy rests on *trends* of small-N data (Fig. 6–7) rather than a proven scaling form, which the paper openly admits; fermions are deferred. The ansatz's 3-level truncation is justified a posteriori (dropping m=3 shifts S by <1% for this single model), not from first principles.

**Verdict:** The paper is a competent, well-scoped incremental advance — a new *package* (interpolating ansatz + validation protocol + entropy-budget conclusion) assembled almost entirely from pre-existing tools, with the novelty residing in the assembly and in the atomic-gas application rather than in any deep new physics. It is novel enough to be useful: before this work there was no defensible S–T curve for large bosonic Laughlin states, and the "entropy is actually high enough" conclusion was substantive.

## [1609.01709v2-methodology] Methodology reproducibility


# Methodology of Raum & Scarnola, "Thermometry for Laughlin States of Ultracold Atoms" (arXiv:1609.01709v2; PRL 126, 053001, 2021)

## What the "central experiment" actually is

There is no physical experiment — the paper constructs and numerically validates a **thermodynamic ansatz** for Laughlin states and reads off an entropy target. Reproducing the central claim (that the entropy per particle needed to cool below the heat-capacity peak, $S_L$, is within reach of current bosonic evaporation, $S_L \approx 0.799(2)$ vs. a naïve estimate $\approx 0.29$) requires the following chain.

### 1. Model
- $N$ spinless identical particles in 2D with artificial magnetic field $\vec B$, harmonic trap, and $s$-wave contact interaction $b_n$ (repulsive for bosons $n{=}0$, pseudopotential for fermions $n{=}1$) — the Hamiltonian of Eq. (1).
- Work in the **lowest Landau level (LLL)** at filling $\nu=1/2$ (fermions) or $\nu=1/3$ (bosons); in LLL units $l_B=k_B=1$, bulk regime $\omega=\Omega$. The Laughlin state is the exact LLL ground state.
- Use the **Haldane sphere** (spherical geometry) to access bulk states with a small number of occupied edge modes, so finite-$N$ numerics approach the $N\to\infty$ planar system.

### 2. Spectral decomposition (the physical premise)
The exact energy spectrum (Fig. 1, $N{=}8$) is assumed to consist of: (i) a single LLL ground state at $E=0$; (ii) a transport gap $\Delta_T$; (iii) a **Gaussian-distributed continuum** of composite-fermion (CF) particle–hole excitations at $E_1\sim\Delta_T$; (iv) sparser, higher-energy levels $E_2, E_3,\dots$ of increasing degeneracy $g_m$. All bulk states at fixed $N$ (neutral excitations) are kept; edge excitations are dropped.

### 3. The ansatz (Eq. 2)
$$Z_{\rm A} = \Bigl(1 + \sum_{m=1}^{3} g_m\, e^{-E_m/T}\Bigr)^{N}$$
a **per-particle product** partition function (each particle independently in the ground state or in one of three excited levels). It is chosen because it is exact as $T\to\infty$ (recovering a gapped high-$T$ cumulant expansion) and has a gapped $T\to0$ limit with $E_1$.

### 4. Fitting parameters from the high-$T$ cumulant expansion
- Compute the exact cumulant ratios $\kappa_l/N$ with the **stochastic trace method** (Weisse et al. 2006): $\kappa_l$ via moments of $H$ estimated with $R{=}100$ normalized random vectors, error $\mathcal O((RD)^{-1/2})\sim10^{-6}$ (reliable for $N\lesssim 12$, where it is cross-checked against exact diagonalization).
- Match the high-$T$ expansion $\log Z_{\rm A}=\sum_l \kappa_l^{(A)}(-T)^{-l}/l!$ to the **exact** expansion to $\mathcal O(T^{-6})$. This yields $g_1=5.53(3), g_2=0.22(3), g_3=0.0004(2), E_1=0.88(2), E_2=8.76(6), E_3=56.9(4.5)$, and $\Delta_T/N \approx 0.627(3)$ (bosons); all Table I values are boson LLL results in Eq.(1) units.

### 5. Validating the thermodynamic functions at finite $N$ and extrapolating to $N\to\infty$
- At fixed $T$, compute the exact entropy (per particle) $S$ and heat capacity $C/T^2 N$ from the stochastic-trace cumulant expansion $S=\kappa_0$ and its $T$-derivatives, for $N=3$–$9$.
- **Finite-size extrapolate** to $N\to\infty$ with assumed scaling forms: $\kappa_0/N$ — log-linear Stirling form (Eq. 9); $\kappa_1/N$ — polynomial $a+b/N+c/N^2$ (Eq. 10); $\kappa_{l\ge2}/N$ — exponential $a+b\exp[c/N]$ (Eq. 11); entropy $S/N$ — exponential at $T\ll\Delta_T$ (Eq. 12) and log form at $T\gg\Delta_T$ (Eq. 13), the tilded parameters absorbing the $N\to\infty$ limit.
- The ansatz is then declared validated where the $N\to\infty$ extrapolated curves (symbols) lie on the ansatz line (Fig. 2, 3, 6); intermediate $T\sim\Delta_T$ is *not* accessible to either the stochastic method or small-$N$ fits, so the match is checked only at the $\text{low-}\Delta_T/T$ and $T\gg\Delta_T$ ends plus the heat-capacity-peak trend (Fig. 7).

### 6. Reading off the target
- $T_L$ = temperature of the heat-capacity peak (from the ansatz); $S_L$ = entropy (per particle) at that $T$, giving $S_L/N \approx 0.799(2)$ (quoting a $\approx0.04$ error, obtained as a $5\%$ variation of $\kappa_0$ propagated to $S_L$).
- Compare to the experimentally reachable bosonic entropy $S/N\approx0.35$ (McKay & DeMarco, cited), which the ansatz maps to $T\approx0.5T_L$. Conclusion: cooling below $T_L$ is within reach. The naïve two-level gapped model (Eq. 5, Supplementary) instead gives $S_T/N\approx0.29$, a **lower bound** on the required entropy because it omits the Gaussian CF continuum — so the real target is larger, but still reachable.

## Assumptions and gaps the methodology papers over

**Model / spectral**
1. **LLL-only spectrum.** All states relevant to thermodynamics are assumed to lie in the LLL. With finite $b_n$, high-energy excitations can leave the LLL; the $E_3$-type states and "bosons not bound to flux quanta" are treated schematically (Fig. 1 caption) and not resolved.
2. **Gaussian continuum of CF particle–hole pairs.** The histogram's approximately-Gaussian shape, observed for $N{=}8$, is taken to hold (and to be well captured by three Gaussian moments) in the $N\to\infty$ limit.
3. **Weakly interacting, near-independent CF picture.** The continuum is modeled as a set of weakly interacting composite fermions; the ansatz effectively ignores **CF–CF interactions** (explicitly conceded as missing in the Supplementary) and any multi-particle correlation beyond independent occupation.
4. **Bulk-only, small edge occupancy.** Results apply only to neutral bulk excitations at $\omega=\Omega$. Edge modes (which *increase* $S_L$ and add $N_e$ entropy, and which change the edge-state spectrum for $\Omega\ne\omega$) are excluded; the comparison to the experimental target therefore uses a number that is not a clean bulk value for a realistic trap.

**Ansatz structure**
5. **Per-particle factorization** $Z=(1+\sum g_m e^{-E_m/T})^N$. This is the core approximation: a product over particles with no interaction energy and no multi-particle correlated states. It is a mean-field-like ansatz, not a real many-body partition function.
6. **Three excited levels suffice.** $m=1,2,3$ (three $E_m$'s) plus the ground branch are assumed to span the spectrum; dropping the $m=3$ term changes entropy by $<1\%$, but this is an untested truncation, not a derivation.
7. **$g_1\approx$ degeneracy of first level, with $g_1\ge1$ for bosons.** The fit absorbs near-degeneracies into a single large factor ($g_1\approx5.53$); this is assumed physical (CF single-pair degeneracy = 1) only approximately.

**Numerical / validation**
8. **Tiny systems + assumed finite-size scaling.** All exact numbers come from $N\le 9$ (bosons, and $N\le12$ for the stochastic method). The scaling forms (Eqs. 9–13) are **assumed**, not derived — the authors explicitly note there is no known finite-size scaling form for these functions.
9. **The middle temperature regime $T\sim\Delta_T$ is never validated against exact data.** Small-$N$ exact results don't reach it (the $N$-scaling breaks down, Fig. 3 top note) and the stochastic trace cannot produce reliable entropy there. The ansatz is pinned only at the $T\to0$ and $T\to\infty$ ends, so its central prediction (and $S_L$) is essentially an **unvalidated interpolation** across the region that contains the quantity of interest. The supporting Fig. 7 is a weak single-quantity (heat-capacity-peak entropy) trend toward the ansatz.
10. **Small error bars.** The $(2)$ on $S_L$ reflects only a $5\%$ variation of one cumulant ($\kappa_0$); it does not propagate the model, scaling-form, or finite-$N$ uncertainties that dominate the systematic error.

**Scope / interpretation**
11. **Bosons only.** The main quantitative result is for the bosonic $\nu=1/3$ state; the fermionic $\nu=1/2$ FQH state (the primary experimental target) requires p-wave scattering the paper deliberately defers, so the reachability claim does not directly cover the fermionic case.
12. **No experimental validation at all.** The "experimental implications" compare a theoretical ansatz number ($S_L$) to a cited evaporation limit from a different geometry (finite, with edges) with no measurement of Laughlin-state thermodynamics; the reachability statement rests on that cross-model comparison.

**Bottom line.** The methodology — LLL CF-spectral decomposition + a gapped, high-$T$-exact per-particle product ansatz fit to stochastic-trace cumulants and extrapolated from $N\le9$ — is clean and internally consistent at its two asymptotic ends, and it does usefully raise the cooling target from the naïve $\approx0.29$ to $\approx0.80$ per particle. But that central number rests on a factorized ansatz whose behavior at $T\sim\Delta_T$ is never checked against exact solutions, on unvalidated finite-size scaling from a handful of tiny systems, and on the exclusion of CF–CF interactions, edge modes, and non-LLL states — the very physics that determines the entropy needed to see Laughlin correlations.

## [1609.01709v2-logical-fallacies] Logical fallacies


# Understanding the Paper's Logical Argument

**Problem setup.** Ultracold-atom experiments are effectively closed (adiabatic), so cooling is done by fixing entropy; the actual temperature is set by the entropy–temperature relation of the target phase. To demonstrate Laughlin correlations (anyon statistics, quantized edge currents) one needs temperatures low enough that excitations are exponentially suppressed, but in strongly correlated regimes S(T) itself is the hard many-body quantity. The naïve gapped two-level model (supplementary, Eq. 4–6) requires S/N ≲ 0.29 kB to reach the exponential regime — *below* the ≈ 0.35 kB evaporative floor for bosons — which would rule out cold-atom FQH experiments (the same logic was used to pessimistically assess Fermi-Hubbard quantum simulation). The paper's claim is that the naïve estimate is wrong because it ignores the continuum of higher-energy states, and that the true entropy at the heat-capacity (Schottky) peak is S_L/N ≈ 0.799(2) kB, within current reach.

**Method chain (the logical backbone):**
1. **Model.** Disk/spherical geometry, δ-interaction pseudopotential H, Laughlin state as exact ground state; lowest Landau level; neutral excitations at fixed N.
2. **Ansatz partition function** Z_A = (1 + Σₘ gₘ e^{−Eₘ/T})^N (Eq. 2). The m=1 term encodes the gapped low-T asymptotics (Schottky); higher m-levels model the nearly-Gaussian high-energy continuum (histograms in Fig. 1); it is "straightforward to use" across all T.
3. **Parameter fixing.** Cumulants κₗ of the full many-body spectrum (Stochastic Trace Method, exact in N up to 12 after finite-size scaling, Table I) fix {gₘ, Eₘ} via a Gröbner-basis match to the exact high-T series. So the ansatz is *exact at high T by construction* and *correct in the low-T asymptotic by form*.
4. **Validation.** (a) Direct comparison vs. exact diagonalization at fixed finite N (N=8 heat capacity, Fig. 2); (b) thermodynamic-limit comparison of the ansatz against finitely-scaled entropies at very low and very high T (Fig. 3); (c) qualitative trend at intermediate T (Figs. 6–7: finite-N curves approach the ansatz; the entropy inflection point trends toward S′_L).
5. **Conclusion.** Read off T_L (the C peak) and S_L = 0.799(2); compare to the 0.35 kB experimental floor → S/N = 0.35 corresponds to T ≈ 0.5 T_L, so cooling below the Schottky peak is feasible; edge states and quasiparticle degeneracy (S_D = N_q log d) only make it easier.

# Feedback

**Strengths.** The motivation is excellent and well framed: the thermometry bottleneck for FQH in atomic gases is a real, under-addressed gap, and the paper converts it into a concrete, actionable number (S_L/N). The method is clean and honestly scoped — exact high-T match, correctly-motivated low-T asymptotics, multiple independent validation layers, and a stated check that dropping terms changes S by <1%. The stochastic-trace cumulant machinery plus the finite-size scaling forms (Stirling log-linear, polynomial, exponential) are sensible.

**Weaknesses in the logic.**

1. **The result lives exactly where the ansatz is unvalidated — and the paper concedes this.** The headline number S_L is defined at T ~ Δ_T, precisely the intermediate regime where (a) finite-size scaling of the entropy has no known form and fails, (b) Fig. 7's "trend" is the only quantitative check, and (c) the ED inflection-point data in Fig. 7 are noisy and do not obviously converge monotonically — the point at 1/N ≈ 0.09 lies *above* the ansatz arrow while later points undershoot, so extrapolation in 1/N is not trustworthy without a scaling function. The quoted error (0.002) propagates *only cumulant-fit uncertainty*; it excludes, by far the dominant, systematic error from the unvalidated T-dependence of Z_A. S_L should carry a much larger, acknowledged systematic error bar. The claim "within reach" is margin-safe (0.799 vs 0.35) but presented with false precision.

2. **The T_L-based criterion is asserted, not justified.** Using the Schottky peak as *the* necessary marker for "low-temperature Laughlin correlations" is a convenience: the actual observables (anyon statistics, edge current) require only T ≪ Δ, arguably weaker than T < T_L, or a different quantitative threshold. Since the whole conclusion is a binary "reachable/not reachable," the choice of criterion should be defended quantitatively (e.g., excitation density below T_L vs. a bound on quasiparticle error in measurements).

3. **Gaussian-continuum assumption rests on N = 8.** The histogram that motivates the multi-level structure (Fig. 1) is from a nine-particle system (D ~ hundreds); whether the shape and density are N-independent by N ≈ 12 (the STM limit) is untested. The large renormalization g₁ = 5.53 versus the CF-theory expectation ≈ 1 is a red flag absorbed silently into the fit: it signals that nearby many-body states distort the low-T asymptotic more than composite-fermion physics suggests.

4. **Scope leaks.** Table I includes fermion (ν = 1/3) cumulants but the analysis is explicitly deferred to "future work" — the table invites comparison the paper doesn't support. The S_D = N_q log d quasiparticle-degeneracy cooling mechanism is dropped in without deriving when it applies or how N_q scales, and the 0.35 kB figure from ref. [8] is a generic evaporative bound, not an entropy budget for the specific loading procedure into a large artificial field (diabatic/finetuning costs are assumed negligibly adiabatic).

5. Minor: k-dependence of E₁ is dropped on the basis of "does not alter results" without a quantitative statement; the v2 (2021) revision against a 2016 submission leaves the experimental landscape discussion somewhat stale relative to later FQH-in-atomic progress.

**Overall assessment.** A solid, well-motivated methodological contribution that answers a practical question with the right machinery and honest limitations — but its headline conclusion inherits the unvalidated region of its own interpolating ansatz, and the error analysis understates that. I would accept it with a substantially widened error bar on S_L and an explicit statement of what excitation density T_L implies for FQH observables.

# Weakest link: the ansatz's interpolation at $T \sim \Delta_T$ (exactly where $T_L$ and the accessible-entropy contour lie)

## The argument chain

1. **Goal.** Decide whether the entropy accessible by evaporative cooling of bosonic atoms, $S/N_{\min} \approx 0.35\,k_B$ (Ref [8]), is low enough to push the gas below $T_L$, the heat-capacity (Schottky) peak — a *necessary* condition for the exponentially suppressed excitations that equilibrium FQH observables assume.
2. **Naïve model.** A two-level gapped model gives a required entropy $S/N < \approx 0.29$ at its peak, which is *above* the $0.35$ floor → infeasible. (This is the straw the paper sets up to overturn.)
3. **Ansatz.** $Z_A = \left(1 + \sum_{m=1}^{3} g_m e^{-E_m/T}\right)^N$, chosen to reproduce the exact high-temperature cumulant expansion, the low-temperature gapped asymptotics, and to interpolate in between.
4. **Validation.** High-temperature agreement (Fig. 2/3); low-temperature agreement via finite-size-scaled exact data; and $N=8$ exact diagonalization.
5. **Result.** Reading the ansatz at its peak gives $S_L/N \approx 0.799(2)$, and $S/N=0.35 \Rightarrow T \approx 0.5\,T_L$.
6. **Conclusion.** Since $0.35 < 0.799$, Laughlin correlations are within reach.

## The step that is not entailed by the data

**Step 4 → 5: the claim that the *validated* ansatz is reliable at $T \sim \Delta_T$.** Every genuinely independent validation the paper possesses lives in the two asymptotes, and the paper states this explicitly:

- **High-temperature** agreement is *by construction* — $g_m, E_m$ are a Gröbner-basis match of the first six cumulants (Table I, "reproduce the exact high temperature expansion up to $O(T^{-6})$"). It is not a test.
- **Low-temperature** agreement only pins $g_1, E_1$ through the $C/N \sim g(\Delta_T/T)^2 e^{-\Delta_T/T}$ form.
- The one independent benchmark, $N=8$ ED (Fig. 2), has its high-$T$ tail again *fitted* and therefore tells you nothing new at $T_L$.

The paper concedes the gap in three places: the finite-size-scaling symbols are "**not accurate for $T \sim \Delta_T$ because $N$-scaling breaks down here**"; only "$T \ll \Delta_T$ and $T \gg \Delta_T$" scaling forms could be constructed; and "intermediate temperatures ($T \sim \Delta_T$) were **not accessible because the fitting functions fail** to capture numerical data." Yet $T_L$ — the heat-capacity peak, the anchor of the entire result — sits *in that gap*, and so does $S/N=\tfrac1{2}T_L \approx 0.35$.

The physics that does the overturning work — shifting $S_L$ from $0.29$ to $0.799$ — is **entirely the $m=2,3$ ("continuum") terms**. Those parameters are fixed *only* by the high-temperature cumulative match, i.e. in the regime where $e^{-E_m/T}$ for large $E_m$ is a slowly varying function whose influence on $\kappa_1,\dots,\kappa_6$ is hard to segregate. **Accept. Score: 7/10.**

# Peer Review: "Thermometry for Laughlin States of Ultracold Atoms" (Raum & Scarola, arXiv:1609.01709v2)

## Summary

The authors address a practical and important question for ultracold-atom fractional quantum Hall (FQH) experiments: what entropy per particle is required to cool a bosonic Laughlin state (ν = 1/2) below its Schottky-type heat-capacity peak, and is that entropy within reach of evaporative cooling? Validating against exact diagonalization (small N) and finite-size scaling, they find S_L/N ≈ 0.799(2), far above the naive two-level model estimate of 0.29, and therefore within reach of the ≈ 0.35 k_B entropies currently accessible with bosonic gases.

## Strengths

1. **Clear, well-motivated problem.** Thermometry in strongly correlated atomic gases is a recognized bottleneck (Refs. 9–12 for the Fermi-Hubbard analog), and no entropy–temperature relation existed for large FQH atomic systems. The framing as an experimental decision criterion (S_L relative to the 0.35 k_B cooling floor) is appropriate.

2. **Sensible, economical methodology.** The central idea — a low-parameter ansatz that is exact at high T (fixed by cumulants), has the correct gapped low-T asymptotics by construction, and is trivially evaluable over the whole temperature range — is a practical contribution. The validation strategy is honest and multi-pronged: direct heat-capacity comparison with ED at N = 8 (Fig. 2), finite-size scaling of cumulants with explicit consistency checks (κ₀/N → 3 log 3 − 2 log 2 ≈ 1.9095 vs. 1.899(1); κ₁/N → 2ν = 1 vs. 1.006(5)), robustness to the number of fitted levels (dropping m = 3 shifts the entropy < 1%), and linear-error propagation from cumulants to S_L.

3. **Physically interesting central finding.** The result that the continuum of high-energy excitations dramatically shifts the entropy–temperature relation — lowering T at fixed S relative to the naive gapped model — explains why the earlier "impossible to reach" conclusion (S/N ≲ 0.29 < 0.35 k_B) was pessimistic. This is a genuinely useful correction for the experimental community, and the argument that the method generalizes to other topological states (chiral spin liquids, fractional Chern insulators, fermions at other fillings) is reasonable.

## Weaknesses

1. **The headline number rests on a model-form assumption in precisely the unconstrained regime.** The ansatz is exact at high T (by construction, parameters fitted there) and correct at low T (by construction, gapped asymptotics). The entropy S_L/N = 0.799(2) is evaluated at T_L ~ ΔT, the intermediate regime where the authors themselves state N-scaling breaks down (Fig. 3 caption) and where no exact data can be computed. The only genuine (non-constraining) validation is Fig. 2 at N = 8 and the finite-N trend in Figs. 6–7, where the data merely *trend* toward the ansatz. The quoted error (±0.002) quantifies cumulant-fit uncertainty only; the model-form uncertainty (three levels vs. the true continuum, and the product structure that forces non-interacting, single-particle-like excitations) is not quantified and is plausibly the dominant error source. I would expect true uncertainty on S_L to be several times larger than quoted.

2. **Strained parameter: g₁ ≈ 5.53 vs. expected ~1.** Composite-fermion theory gives roughly one particle-hole excitation per k-state. The fitted degeneracy g₁ ≈ 5.5 is ~5× larger, and the authors acknowledge it "renormalizes to account for additional nearby states." This is the clearest indication that the three-level structure is absorbing spectral weight it was not designed to hold — a reminder that the fit parameters are effective, not physical, which reinforces the concern in point 1.

3. **Saturated fit, no higher-order cross-check.** Six parameters (g₁–g₃, E₁–E₃) are fixed against cumulants κ₀–κ₅; matching is claimed only up to O(T⁻⁶). A fit this saturated cannot be independently cross-checked, and it would strengthen the paper substantially to show how the fitted S_L responds to including a fourth level (the authors only show that *removing* the third level changes little).

4. **Small-system limitations of the benchmarking.** ED reaches N ≤ 12 (N = 12 only for cumulants, via stochastic trace). At ν = 1/2 this means 2M ≈ 3N − 3 flux quanta, and N = 8 systems are too small to display unambiguous topological order. The N=8 heat-capacity comparison is therefore a check of thermodynamic book-keeping more than of the physics being approximated.

5. **Model specificity of the headline number.** S_L/N ≈ 0.8 is for the ideal contact-interaction model (b₀ = 1). Realized systems (1/r⁸ dipolar/power-law interactions in rotating clouds, lattice gauge fields) have different gaps and different high-energy continua. The authors note only that gaps change with the interaction (Refs. 72–74); the *entropy* conclusion may also shift, and no estimate of its sensitivity is given. Likewise, the fixed-N, neutral, bulk-only treatment excludes charged excitations and edge modes. The authors conjecture edges would help (adiabatic cooling), which is plausible but unquantified — and it is the one-directional term that matters most for the experimental bottom line.

6. **Minor.** E₁ = 0.88(2) vs. Δ_T = 0.627(3) for bosons is a noticeable mismatch handled with "including k-dependence does not alter our results" — the actual k-dependence and its effect on the peak would have been worth one figure. The abstract's phrasing "entropies required to observe Laughlin correlations" slightly overstates what is shown below the peak is a *necessary*, not sufficient, condition (the text is more careful than the abstract). The paper is also quite dense for its length; several steps (e.g., the spherical-geometry mapping to disk, why κ₁/N → 2ν) would benefit from a sentence of explanation rather than citation.

This is a careful, modest, and directly useful paper. The combination of high-temperature series expansion, stochastic trace methods, and a gapped-continuum ansatz is a genuinely effective new construction, and the correction to the naive entropy bound is a valuable experimental input. However, the single most important number in the paper (S_L/N) is evaluated in a temperature regime where the ansatz is not independently validated, the effective degeneracy g₁ ≈ 5.5 signals that the model form is being pushed beyond its design, and the reported errors omit the dominant model-form uncertainty. These issues are acknowledged to varying degrees in the text but should be foregrounded and, ideally, bounded (e.g., by bracketing S_L with larger ansatz families). I recommend minor–major revision with the uncertainty analysis of point 1 addressed.

2. **Interaction robustness of the dimensionless result.** $S_L/N = 0.799$ is computed for the contact pseudopotential. Real systems (rotating BECs, gauge-field arrays) have finite-range interactions that you note (Refs. 72–74) shift the gap. The *experimental* verdict depends on $T_L$ scaling, which is where the gap enters, but the dimensionless $S_L/N$ can shift if the continuum energy distribution changes shape. One comparison with a finite-range or harmonic interaction — even at one system size — showing $S_L/N$ moves by <10–15% would convert "within reach" from an interaction-specific claim into a generic one. As written, a skeptical experimentalist can reply "fine, but for *my* potential?"

3. **The "$N$-th power" structure is doing real work.** $Z_A = [\cdots]^N$ assumes $N$ independent excitation ladders (independent CFs), i.e., factorization of the many-body spectrum. That's what makes $Z$ extensive and is justified by the CF picture, but it's an assumption, not a theorem. Your supplemental caveat about CF–CF interactions improving the dashed (naïve) low-$T$ line is right; extend the same honesty: bound or estimate how factorization error could shift $S_L$. Even an order-of-magnitude argument would do.

4. **The "within reach" claim hangs on one 2011 benchmark.** You compare against $S/N \approx 0.35$ from McKay–DeMarco. That's a snapshot. Present your result also as the *function* $T(S/N)$ (Fig. 3 top basically is — promote it), so the verdict stays self-updating as cooling improves, and say explicitly that the claim "low enough" refers to crossing the peak, i.e., entering the exponential regime, not to the deeper low-$T$ observables (edge current, braiding) which may need still lower entropy.

5. **Minor items, but a paper that cites error bars throughout should not fudge its own:**
   - The supplemental says your fit gives $\kappa_0/N = 1.899(1)$, "close to our expected value $\approx 1.90954$." Those differ by 0.55%, about ten times the stated error. Either the error estimate is underestimated or "close" is doing work; say which.
   - $g_3 = 0.0004(2)$ has 50% relative error. Your drop-out test covers the thermodynamics, but state that explicitly right there rather than making the reader find it.
   - Typo/garbled glyph: "we expect $g_1$ & 1" in Method (should be $g_1 \gtrsim 1$ or the like); the Fig. 4 axis labels are badly mangled in layout.
   - The STM error claim ($\sim 10^{-6}$ at $N=12$) is asserted; include one convergence-vs-$R$ panel — it costs nothing and preempts a referee who distrusts $R = 100$.
   - Detail the 6×6 constraint counting ($\sum g_m = D-1$, $\sum g_mE_m = \mathrm{Tr}\,H/N$ constraints) in the supplemental; "Gröbner basis fit" currently appears as magic.
   - The $E_1 = 0.88(2)$ from the $N=8$ fit vs. $\Delta_T = 0.627(3)$ in Table I will draw a "which gap?" question from a careless reader; one sentence clarifying finite-$N$ vs. thermodynamic-limit gaps would head it off.

## Verdict

This is a solid, well-motivated PRL-grade piece of work: an important question, a practical and honest method, layered validation, and a quantitative answer that changes an experimental feasibility assessment in the field's favor. Its core weakness — the interpolated intermediate-temperature regime carrying the final number — is intrinsic to the method, and you handle it more honestly than most, but items 1–2 above are the difference between "convincing" and "difficult for a reviewer to contest." I'd hold the "within reach of current cooling" language to item 2 being done, and add item 1(i) in response to review in any case.

On the scores: the ingredients (composite-fermion theory, Gaussian density distributions, high-$T$ cumulant expansions, stochastic tracing) are all pre-existing, and the factorized multi-level partition function is a modest but genuinely useful methodological contribution whose principal value is the inverted viability verdict. Quality is sound throughout with honest error accounting. Impact is real for the cold-atom FQH/topological-state subfield — it reframed the thermometry budget for a class of experiments — though not field-defining in the broader sense.
