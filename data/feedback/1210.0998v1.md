# Review: Guo et al. — Spherical-Geometry NLFFF Optimization Applied to an SDO/HMI Active Region

## Summary of what the paper attempts

The paper (a) validates J. McTiernan's spherical-geometry NLFFF optimization code against the Low & Lou (1990) analytic flux-rope solution, including ideal, realistic (bottom-only vector + PFSS side/top), and noisy (±preprocessed) boundary conditions; (b) quantitatively compares the spherical code to a Cartesian code at fields of view of 20°, 40°, 60°; and (c) applies the method to the NOAA 11402 region before and after an M8.7 flare on 2012 Jan 23, initializing from global PFSS models built on two different boundary data sets (synoptic frame vs. flux-dispersion model), and comparing the resulting NLFFF field lines to SDO/AIA EUV loops.

## Strengths

1. **The geometry comparison (Table 2) is the paper's clearest contribution.** The statement that flat-box NLFFF extrapolations degrade once the bottom-of-viewport field of view exceeds ~20° is a concrete, actionable guideline for the SDO era, where HMI routinely gives 40–70° vector fields. This is genuinely useful.

2. **Use of a global PFSS to fix side/top boundaries** rather than the local-patch potential-field assumption of Wiegmann et al. (2006) is the right practical choice for an active region embedded in a group (11401/11402/11405/11407), and the authors explicitly discuss the isolation/torque-free constraints (Molodensky, Sakurai).

3. **The pseudo-Monte-Carlo error analysis on the global radial field** (10 realizations at 10 G, Table 3) is better than most NLFFF papers of the period, which typically report no uncertainties. It has the virtue, here, of showing the two boundary data sets are consistent within errors.

4. **The application cleanly illustrates a known physical point** — that before the flare the EUV loop system is strongly non-potential, NLFFF field lines hug the EUV loops, and after the flare the free energy drops and the NLFFF relaxes toward PFSS. The free-energy release estimate (0.5–0.8 × 10³² erg) is of the correct order for an M8.7.

5. The paper is well organized, carefully written, and cites the right prior art (Wheatland; Wiegmann; Tadesse; DeRosa et al. 2009 on field-of-view limits).

## Weaknesses

### 1. Methodological (most important)

- **Single analytic benchmark.** All code validation rests on one Low & Lou eigen-solution, the P₁,₁ flux rope. That is a smooth, axisymmetric, isolated structure. It does not test the parts of the method that matter for real ARs — sheared arcades, multiple polarity inversions, fan-spine connectivity, or the failure modes when the field is close to but not quite force-free to start with. A second family of benchmarks (e.g. a sheared-potential field, or a multi-pole synthetic HMI-like field) would be far more convincing. As it stands, the "validated" claim in the abstract is narrower than it reads.

- **The Cartesian vs. spherical comparison is somewhat contaminated.** The authors place *heliographic* components (B_φ, −B_θ, B_r) on the *flat* Cartesian bottom plane. On a flat plane those three components are not mutually consistent as a coordinate-triadic field at θ = 78° — the transverse "components" point in directions that are not actually tangents of the flat plane's axes. The very large L_f^C = 96.9 for the θ_l = 20° Cartesian case looks less like "Cartesian geometry is bad at 20°" and more like the Cartesian optimizer failed to converge under an inconsistent boundary prescription. The authors attribute the whole difference to geometry, but at least part of it could be the component-frame mismatch. This deserves an explicit caveat, and ideally a Cartesian run fed proper planar (B_x, B_y, B_z) components for a cleaner baseline.

- **Convergence behavior is not characterized.** The paper never states when (or a criterion for when) the optimizer is stopped, nor does it show the L_f, L_d convergence history, nor a grid-resolution study. NLFFF optimization is famously non-convex and history-dependent (the authors correctly note this); the absence of any convergence diagnostics — for 30 × 54 × 61 grids — is a real gap. It is also never stated how 6 buffer cells were chosen, even though the result is known to be sensitive to that choice.

- **The "flux-dispersion is better than synoptic" narrative rests on self-consistency, not on correctness.** The two NLFFF runs differ only in the side/top boundaries (PFSS of two different global fields). Saying the one that produces slightly lower L_f, L_d and higher free energy is "better" is circular: the optimizer will always push L_f, L_d down, and free energy is just E_nlfff − E_pot, which is larger whenever the boundary is more strongly non-potential. None of these three quantities is an independent validation. The only *external* check is the qualitative EUV comparison (see below), and the authors are honest about that — but the abstract and Section 5 lean on the L_f/L_d/E_free ranking more than the evidence supports.

- **Circularity in the EUV conclusion.** "NLFFF field lines reproduce the EUV loops better than PFSS" — where the NLFFF was constructed *from* footpoints at the same latitudes/longitudes and the PFSS from a 2.6-solar-rotation-old global map that does not include the AR at all (its AR-region field is essentially extrapolated potential field). It is almost guaranteed that an NLFFF initialized from local HMI data will hug the local EUV loops and a global potential field will not. The comparison would be far more informative if PFSS were built from a *local* source-surface potential-field fit (or a local NLFFF with a different initial condition) so that both models share comparable local boundary information.

- **The EUV comparison is almost entirely visual.** Figure 7 is an overlay, not a metric; Figure 8 (footpoint distance vs. apex height) is the first quantitative comparison, and even there the claim that NLFFF has "very few" field lines with height > distance while PFSS has "quite some" is asserted, not measured (no counts, no distribution, no error bars). A simple loop-height distribution or footpoint-to-EUV-emission correlation would make this the strongest part of the paper.

- **No handling of the 180° ambiguity, and no quantitative statement about the HMI transverse-field error.** For a 5–10 kG AR the transverse azimuth is one of the dominant error budgets for NLFFFs (Metcalf et al. 2008; DeRosa et al. 2009). The authors describe their ambiguity resolution (Metcal minimum-energy + weak-field threshold of 200–400 G; the weak-field branch uses a *potential-field* azimuth, which biases the transverse direction toward "un-twisted" in exactly the regions where it most affects the NLFFF) — but never test the NLFFF result against, e.g., perturbed transverse azimuths. The pseudo-MC study perturbs only the *global* radial map, leaving the most physically important error source (local transverse azimuth) untested.

### 2. Quantitative

- **Free-energy differences are within the published errors.** In Table 3, at 02:58 UT the free-energy difference flux-dispersion − synoptic is 1.63 − 1.58 = 0.05 × 10³² erg, with error bars ±0.03 and ±0.02; at 04:10 it is 1.69 − 1.67 = 0.02 × 10³² vs ±0.01/+0.01. The conclusion that "the flux-dispersion model has higher free energy, therefore is better-supported" is not statistically supported by the authors' own error analysis. (The corresponding pre–post flare difference, ~0.05 × 10³² erg, is likewise of the order of the error bars.)

- **0.5° NLFFF resolution for a ~20° × 30° AR.** That is ~40 × 60 transverse cells across the active-region group — enough for global flux connectivity, but a factor of ~12–17 coarser than the input HMI 0.03" pixel, and coarser than the typical active-region bipole. The PFSS correlation coefficient between $b_r$ and $B_r$ of only 0.93–0.96 and normalized error 0.27–0.34 in §4.2 already show how much of the radial structure the global PFSS *misses* for this AR — that gap is then carried straight into the NLFFF side/top boundaries. A resolution/initialization independence study is missing.

- **The PFSS "well reconstructed" claim in §3.3 coexists with non-trivial residuals.** For the Low & Lou test, the PFSS has C = 1.00 and normalized error 0.05 in $b_r$, but its C_vec = 0.85, C_CS = 0.83, energy 82% — i.e. a 5% radial error and ~15–18% in vector metrics already before any NLFFF step. That's fine for a *test* of the NLFFF itself, but the PFSS quality is also a hidden parameter in the real-data section; a 5% radial error in $b_r$ becomes an O(5%) mismatch on the global PFSS side/top boundaries of the NLFFF box and should be discussed.

### 3. Presentation

- **Table notation is misleading.** The table headers write e.g. "$C_{\rm vec}^{\,2}$" with a footnote "²", but the superscript is a reference marker, not an exponent — readers will naturally read these as squared correlations. Use a different (e.g. asterisk) marker, or move the footnote to a line.
- **Figure 7 caption has a typo:** panel (d) is described as "Field lines of the NLFFF model at 02:58 UT overlaid on the EUV image" but should say "at 02:58 UT for NLFFF, at 04:10 UT for PFSS/NLFFF (e, f)" — the present caption lists 02:58 twice.
- **A stray "e"**: "Solar Dynamic Observatori**e**" in a few places; should be SDO or Solar Dynamics Observatory.
- **Table 3 column headers "²/³"**: same footnote-as-superscript issue as Table 1–2; also E_m^free² vs E_m^free³ should read E_m^{free} (foot)² / (foot)³.
- **Notation consistency:** the text says "the objective functional defined in Equation (3) divided by volume V is the sum of L_f and L_d" — but L_w in (3) already contains the r² sinθ measure and the weight ω; dividing by V is a different object than "the integrand of (3)". A small algebra check in (3)↔(6)↔(7) would help.
- **§3.4 setup is hard to follow.** "The bottom boundary for the Cartesian extrapolation is also the analytical magnetic field at 1 R⊙, but the geometry of the magnetic fields are projected to the tangent bottom surface of the cubic box, while the vector components are kept in the heliographic coordinates." This is exactly the point I flagged in §Methodological above, but the prose doesn't state it — a figure showing what (B_x, B_y, B_z) means in this setup, and why, would help.
- **§3.1 coordinate transformation (Eq. 4)** is introduced but then only used to define the Low & Lou solution; the rest of the paper re-uses the local coordinate name "X, Y, Z" loosely. Pick one convention.

### 4. Missing or under-argued material

- **No comparison against the then-current state-of-the-art MHD relaxation** (Su et al. 2011; Bobra et al. 2008, both cited). The authors note at the end that Wiegmann & Inhester (2010) and Tadesse et al. (2012) use an error-admissible optimization, but do not discuss how the present result would change under a Tikhonov-regularized objective with HMI error bars. This is a serious omission in a 2012-era NLFFF paper on HMI data.
- **No statement on how the PFSS radial-field-at-2.5 R⊙ assumption** interacts with the choice of a flux-dispersion map that is itself a 2-D spatial/temporal model — the two are not guaranteed to be physically consistent at the source surface.
- **The claim "NLFFF is necessary to construct the magnetic configuration for active regions... especially those with eruptive activities"** (p. 3) could be argued from the observed non-potentiality of the EUV loops alone; quantifying non-potentiality (e.g. current estimates, twist, relative helicity) from the NLFFF itself is not done.

## Suggested revisions, in order of priority

1. Add at least one more benchmark family (sheared multi-pole AR synthetic, or a non-axisymmetric analytic NLFFF) before claiming validation.
2. Redo the Cartesian comparison with proper planar components, or explicitly caveat that the comparison is confounded by the heliographic-on-flat-plane choice.
3. Add a convergence criterion / convergence curve and a grid-resolution study for the real-data run; report how the 6-cell buffer choice affects the result.
4. Replace the "flux-dispersion has better L_f, L_d, larger E_free ⇒ better" sentence with one about the *EUV agreement* and the free-energy *release* estimate, which are the claims the data actually support; explicitly note the free-energy difference is within Monte-Carlo error.
5. Make the EUV comparison quantitative: loop-height distribution, footpoint distance to the peak-emission contour, or a correlation integral between NLFFF/PFSS and EUV.
6. Add a sensitivity study to transverse azimuth (e.g. ±10° azimuth perturbation in the strong-field region) rather than only to the global radial field.
7. Fix the table/figure typos and clarify the (4) coordinate convention.

## Overall assessment

This is a competent, well-organized NLFFF case-study paper. The geometry-comparison result and the demonstration that NLFFF initialized from a *global* PFSS can track the non-potentiality of a real SDO/HMI active region across a large M-class flare are genuinely useful for the field at the time. The main caveats are: (i) validation on a single analytic solution is thin; (ii) the Cartesian-vs-spherical comparison is not a clean geometry experiment; (iii) the "better NLFFF" narrative leans on metrics that are endogenous to the optimization; and (iv) the external check on the model — the EUV comparison — remains largely qualitative. With items 1–5 of the revision list addressed, this would be a clearly stronger paper.

## [1210.0998v1-expert-collegue-review] Expert Collegue Review


# Review: "Modeling Magnetic Field Structure of a Solar Active Region Corona using Nonlinear Force-Free Fields in Spherical Geometry" (Guo et al.)

## Summary

The authors (1) test J. McTiernan's spherical NLFFF optimization code against the Low & Lou P₁,₁ analytical solution under ideal and noisy boundary conditions, including a Cartesian-vs-spherical comparison on identical boundary data, and (2) apply the code to SDO/HMI full-disk magnetograms before/after an M8.7 flare in NOAA 11402 (2012 Jan 23), comparing NLFFF and PFSS field lines with SDO/AIA EUV loops.

## Strengths

- **Well-structured validation.** The ideal cases (L&W solution on all six boundaries) are recovered essentially exactly (C_vec ≈ 1, ε ≈ 1), and the noisier cases show monotone, physically sensible degradation. This is the right experimental design for a code-test paper. The choice to re-test the code "although Tadesse et al. (2009) have already tested it" gives an independent confirmation, which is a legitimate contribution.
- **The Cartesian vs. spherical comparison (Table 2, §3.4) is the most useful new result of the paper.** Showing that a naive Cartesian extrapolation degrades by roughly two orders of magnitude in L_f²/L_d² as the field of view grows from 20° to 60°, while the spherical code degrades gracefully, is a concrete, quantified argument for when spherical geometry is necessary. That is a genuinely actionable guideline for the community.
- **Sound handling of the practical problems.** Initializing from a *global* PFSS model (rather than a local one), using a global radial magnetic field for the side/top boundaries, comparing synoptic-frame vs. flux-dispersion-model surface fields, and adding a Monte Carlo error estimate (10 noisy realizations) on the observational results (Table 3) are all thoughtful touches. Discussing the Molodensky force-free/torque-free isolation conditions and the net-flux/torque preprocessing is appropriate framing.
- **Physically plausible narrative.** The free-energy budget (pre-flare 1.5–2.0 × 10³² ergs, post-flare 1.0–1.2 × 10³², difference ~0.5–0.8 × 10³²) is broadly consistent with M8.7 flare energies, and the finding that NLFFF loops track the pre-flare EUV nonpotential loops while PFSS field lines fall short is the expected and convincing outcome.

## Weaknesses

- **The observational portion is essentially confirmatory.** It re-confirms the well-established finding (Schrijver et al. 2006; Metcalf et al. 2008; DeRosa et al. 2009) that NLFFF beats PFSS in nonpotential active regions. The EUV comparison is explicitly qualitative (the authors concede "the qualitative nature of this comparison is not ideal"), with no quantitative loop-matching or statistical measure of field-line/EUV coincidence. The footpoint-distance-vs-height plot (Fig. 8) helps but is still only a shape proxy.
- **Low spatial resolution.** The HMI data are downgraded to 0.5°/pixel for the surface field and the PFSS (l_max = 360), versus HMI's native ~0.12″–2″ scale. The authors acknowledge this limits small-scale structure; it also means the "core region" comparison is really about the large-scale envelope, which dilutes the claim that NLFFF reproduces the flare-productive-region structure.
- **No α or helicity diagnostics.** The paper never estimates the force-free function α, the twist number, or the relative magnetic helicity of the NLFFF model — all of which were available with this same machinery (Guo et al. 2010b; Jing et al. 2009, 2012, all cited) and would have strengthened the physical content of the flare-arcade result substantially.
- **Minor issues:** In Table 3, "m" in E_m^nlfff/E_m^pot appears to be a typographic/notation slip (m for NLFFF). The energy difference being used as a proxy for flare energy releases energy from an idealized pre/post pair that differ not only by the flare but also by the evolving surface field; this conflation could be flagged more explicitly. The "preprocessing improves results" claim (§3.3, noisy models) is modest and could be stated less strongly — gains are small (0.81→0.85 etc.).

## Verdict

A competent, careful code-validation-plus-application paper. The new, durable content is the quantified Cartesian-vs-spherical guidance and the global-PFSS-initialized spherical NLFFF workflow; the observation comparison is solid but confirmatory. Sound methodology and honest limitation assessment, but modest conceptual novelty and limited quantitative reach against observations. Suitable for publication in a mainstream solar-physics journal; a good reference for anyone choosing between geometries or initializing NLFFF from global fields.

**Originality:** incremental — standard test model and standard flare application; the Cartesian/spherical table and global initialization choices are the genuinely useful additions.
**Overall:** sound and well executed, with the weaknesses above preventing a higher grade.
**Impact:** useful practical reference for the NLFFF-extrapolation community, with a few concrete takeaways, but not field-changing (follow-ups such as Wiegemann & Inhester et al. 2012 and Tadese et al. 2012 quickly advanced the method further).

# Editorial Review: Guo et al., "Modeling Magnetic Field Structure of a Solar Active Region Corona using Nonlinear Force-Free Fields in Spherical Geometry" (arXiv:1210.0998)

## Overview

A 23-page AAS-format paper (Guo, Ding, Liu, Sun, DeRosa, Wiegelmann) validating a spherical-geometry NLFFF optimization code against the Low & Lou analytical solution and then applying it to SDO/HMI observations of an AR group surrounding an M8.7 flare, with comparison against PFSS models and SDO/AIA EUV images. The paper's job — prove the code works, then demonstrate it on real data — is clear from the abstract onward.

## Structure and Argument

The logical spine is conventional but well laid out:

1. **Intro** — motivation, background on NLFFF methods, and a gap statement (large FOVs need spherical geometry; HMI now provides such FOVs).
2. **§2** — the optimization functional, cleanly defined.
3. **§3** — validation: (a) analytical solution, (b) PFSS initialization, (c) ideal and noisy boundary tests, (d) Cartesian vs. spherical comparison as FOV grows. This is the strongest and best-organized section.
4. **§4** — application: data prep, construction of the global radial field from (i) a synoptic frame and (ii) a flux-dispersal model, PFSS/NLFFF runs, and comparison with EUV loops.
5. **§5** — recap, comparison to earlier spherical NLFFF work (Wiegelmann 2007; Tadesse et al. 2009, 2011), future directions (higher resolution, the Wiegelmann & Inhester 2010 variant).

The argument is coherent: spherical geometry matters for large FOVs, the code reproduces the analytical solution under both ideal and noisy conditions, and on real data NLFFF traces EUV loops better than PFSS before a flare, with the gap closing after. The "Summary and Discussions" section explicitly flags the sensitivity of the result to surface-field treatment (synoptic frame vs. flux-dispersal) and the marginal significance when errors are included — a fair, appropriately hedged claim.

Two minor argument weaknesses a copyeditor would flag: the Introduction is long (~4 pages) and somewhat repetitive — it rehashes the field-isolation requirement in three different formulations, and the same DeRosa et al. 2009 "large FOV" point is made in two paragraphs. The energy-difference between pre- and post-flare NLFFF models is presented as the flare's energy release; a careful editor would nudge the authors to label this as an *upper-bound estimate* (it is, effectively — the post-flare field is not assumed to be the relaxed state — even if §4.3 gestures in that direction).

## Prose and Grammar

The writing is functional English — comprehensible throughout — but carries a noticeable count of errors that would not pass a first copyedit. These are not obscure; they are the sort a careful proofreader catches:

**Typos / factual slips**
- `"Solar Dynamic Obseratory (SDO)"` — §1, missing "v" in *Observatory*.
- `"NOAA 11042"` — §5, should be **11402**, which is the AR named consistently everywhere else.
- `"In this section, We add some Gaussian-distributed random noise..."` — §3.3 and again in §4.3 (`"For each of the four cases in Table 3, We add..."`): stray capital **W** mid-sentence (twice). The Abstract has the same error (`"Additionally, We apply..."`).

**Subject–number and pronoun disagreement**
- `"the distance between the footpoints are less than the height of the loops"` — §4.3; *distance* is singular.
- `"the geometry of the magnetic fields are projected to the tangent bottom surface"` — §3.4; *geometry* is singular.
- `"the polar fields ... are stronger than that with the other boundary"` — §4.2; *fields* is plural, so *those*.
- `"gives better ... measures, and larger magnetic energy than that computed from the synoptic frame"` — §4.3; *measures* is plural.

**Articles and hyphenation**
- `"a apriori requirement"` — §1; should be *an a priori* (two words, hyphen only as adjective).
- `"A NLFFF procedure"` appears at least twice. *An NLFFF* is the standard since the abbreviation is pronounced with a leading vowel ("en"); consistent usage would be preferable.
- `"Three dimensional magnetic fields"` — §1, unhyphenated; other multi-word modifiers are hyphenated elsewhere.

**Recurring phrasing**
- `"information of magnetic field (line) connections"` (≈ twice in the Intro) — should be *information about*.
- `"there are quite some NLFFF field lines"` — §4.3; colloquial in this register.
- `"Such a behavior becomes weaker"` — behavior does not weaken; *a tendency* or *a preference* is meant.
- `"we test again J. McTiernan's version ... in this paper carefully"` — §1; the adverb placement is awkward.
- `"one among the active region group"` — §1; *one member of the active region group* is smoother.

**Sentence construction**
- `"We recognize that the qualitative nature of this comparison is not ideal, however it remains the best option..."` — §4.3; comma splice. Should be a semicolon before *however* or a conjunction after the comma.
- The Abstract's final sentence (NLFFF performs better than PFSS "not only for the core field ... but also for large EUV loops higher than 50 Mm") packs a lot into a single sentence that would read better split.

**Tense / voice**
There is an occasional drift: §3.3 opens "in this section, we will perform" (future) while the rest of the paper uses present/past. A trivial fix, but it reads as a leftover from an earlier draft.

Cumulatively, none of these is disqualifying; individually they are small; together they mark the manuscript as a solid first draft that still needs a careful English-language pass before it would read as polished for a journal.

## What Reads Well

- The "This paper is organized as follows" paragraph is clear.
- §3 (the testing section) is well sectioned and the case structure (Case 1/2/3, noisy models 1/2, with/without preprocessing) is stated cleanly — a reader can track which run is being discussed.
- §4.2's "First / Secondly / Finally" scaffolding for constructing the global radial field is easy to follow.
- Tables 1–3 carry a lot of the load and are clearly captioned; the Notes under each table give the reader the definitions they need.
- The concluding paragraph of §5 fairly positions the work against the Wiegelmann 2007 line of earlier spherical-NLFFF work, which an editor would want to see.

## Scores

Structure and argument: **clear, conventional, well motivated**; the argument is honestly hedged where the data only marginally support a claim.
Prose: **functional but under-edited** — a dozen small grammar/typo issues in a 23-page manuscript is on the lower side of what peer reviewers typically accept but on the high side of what would be returned with "minor language edits."
Originality of argument (as presented): a standard validation + real-data application.
Impact of prose/argument presentation: no structural weaknesses that would impede understanding; a competent read.

## [1210.0998v1-critique] Critique


# Critique: "Modeling Magnetic Field Structure of a Solar Active Region Corona using Nonlinear Force‑Free Fields in Spherical Geometry" (Guo et al., 2012; ApJ 764, 37)

## What the paper does

The paper tests an existing nonlinear force‑free field (NLFFF) optimization code implemented in **spherical geometry** (J. McTiernan's SSW implementation of the Wieglermann/Wheatland optimization) against the Low & Lou (1990) analytic solution under ideal and noisy boundary conditions, then applies it to a large field‑of‑view M8.7 flare active region group observed with SDO/HMI before and after the 2012 Jan 23 event, comparing field lines (NLFF vs. PFSS) against SDO/AIA EUV loops.

## Strengths

- **Sound validation methodology.** Recovering an analytic NLFF against which to benchmark an optimizer is the correct way to test such codes, and the authors run a sensible ladder: ideal six‑surface BCs → bottom‑only BC (Case 2) → bottom‑only + buffer/weighting (Case 3) → noisy, preprocessed surfaces. The results (Table 1) cleanly show the weighting buffer rescues the top/lateral degradation (Case 2 → Case 3), which is a genuinely useful and well‑isolated result.
- **A clear, actionable finding on geometry.** Section 3.4 gives a quantitative demonstration that the spherical code outperforms the Cartesian one once the bottom field of view is ≳ 20°×20° (e.g. L_f² drops from 96.9 to 0.36 at θ_l = 20°). This directly motivates using spherical NLFFF for SDO/HMI full‑disk data and is the paper's most defensible contribution.
- **Better‑than‑typical error handling.** For the real‑data section the authors add a pseudo‑Monte‑Carlo uncertainty (10 noisy realizations of the global radial field with 10 G noise) and propagate it to the free‑energy, which is more rigorous than a single deterministic run.
- **Physically coherent application.** The before/after comparison (expanded, higher NLFF loops pre‑flare; smaller NLFf–PFSS difference post‑flare; drop in free energy) is consistent with the flare ejecting twist and relaxing the configuration. The observation is chosen well — a large interacting AR group for which spherical geometry is arguably required.

## Weaknesses

- **Incremental, code‑validation character.** The optimizer (Wheatland), the test field (Low & Lou), and the spherical code (Wieglermann 2007; Tadesse 2009, 2011) are all pre‑existing; the authors themselves say they "test again J. McTiernan's version." The genuinely new pieces are the buffer/weighting application, the Cartesian–spherical comparison, and one SDO case study. The headline scientific conclusion (NLFF reproduces non‑potential large/EUV loops better than PFSS) was already established in the authors' own prior work (Guo et al. 2010). So the paper is best read as careful independent validation plus a first large‑FoV SDO application, not new physics.
- **Observational validation is only qualitative — and the authors concede it.** Overlaid EUV images and a height‑vs‑footpoint‑distance scatter (Figs. 7, 8) are the only check that the *coronal* model matches reality, and there is no quantitative loop‑matching metric (no loop‑axis correlation, no line‑of‑sight intensity vs. model). Force‑free/divergence‑free residuals and magnetic energy, which dominate the quantitative content, confirm the field *satisfies the equations*, not that it is *correct*. The "spherical is better" claim is therefore anchored on synthetic‑truth tests, not on observational superiority of one extrapolation.
- **The Low & Lou test is somewhat thin.** A single eigenfunction (P_{1,1}) at a modest resolution (30×60×120 globally; 30×60×(φ) in the wedge) is a standard but simple benchmark. There is no test of the *α* profile recovery, no higher‑twist configuration, and no test where the true field is less well sampled — all of which would probe robustness more than the near‑recovery of Table 1 Case 1.
- **The Cartesian comparison is somewhat handicapped by construction.** The Cartesian run keeps heliographic components as if Cartesian in a cube whose side is tangent to the surface, uses a PFSS side/top for both geometries, and applies the buffer. That mimics a real observation, but it means the "victory" of the spherical code conflates *correct coordinate treatment* with *a better algorithm*; a cleaner test would re‑express the same boundary problem so both solvers are given comparable, physically consistent side conditions.
- **Sensitivity to global‑boundary choice is acknowledged but under‑explored.** The PFSS/NLFF outcomes differ between the "synoptic frame" and "flux‑dispersal" insertions, but in error bars the two end up "marginally comparable" (Table 3, discussed in §4.3). The paper could have pushed harder on how much of the NLFF "improvement" over PFSS is actually inherited from the chosen surface model / polar interpolation versus the force‑free solve itself.
- **Notational inconsistencies.** The text in §3.3 says the tables list 1−E_N and 1−E_M, but the table headers read 1−E_N² and 1−E_M²; the 180° ambiguity scheme (strong‑field annealing + weak‑field PFSS acute angle, threshold 200 G→400 G) is described in prose but is an important and fragile step that is not sensitivity‑tested. Minor, but they erode the otherwise careful presentation.

## Bottom line

A competent, rigorous, and well‑executed **code‑validation paper** with a clear and useful methodological result (spherical NLFFF preferred for large fields of view; boundary weighting improves extrapolations) wrapped in a physically sensible but scientifically modest single‑event case study. ## Stated contribution, in my own words

Guo and co-authors take an existing nonlinear force-free field (NLFFF) reconstruction algorithm — the optimization code that minimizes an energy functional combining the Lorentz force and the divergence (Eq. 3) — and run it in **spherical** geometry. They benchmark it against the Low & Lou analytic solution (Cases 1–3, noisy boundary conditions, buffer/weighting function), and compare it with the Cartesian version. They then apply the spherical NLFFF to a real SDO/HMI active region (NOAA 11402, which produced an M8.7 flare on 23 Jan 2012), initialize the reconstruction from PFSS models built on two different global radial fields (a synoptic frame vs. an evolving flux-dispersal model), and compare the resulting NLFFF and PFSS field lines to SDO/AIA EUV loops (qualitatively, and quantitatively via a footpoint-separation-vs-max-height metric), and track the free-energy change across the flare.

So the paper's own framing is: validate the spherical-geometry code, establish *when* spherical beats Cartesian, and demonstrate it on a real, large-field-of-view, multi-active-region flare event.

## Is this novel relative to what the authors cite?

**What is genuinely new:**

1. **The Cartesian-vs-spherical benchmark (Section 3.4 / Table 2) is the paper's main technical novelty.** The claim that the spherical code outperforms the Cartesian one specifically when the bottom-boundary field of view exceeds ~20°×20° is a concrete, quantitative comparison the authors say they have "made ... for the first time" in this study. This is a legitimately new result and a useful practical guideline.
2. **The specific case study** — a large multi-AR group (11401–11407, ~71°×71.5°) — is exactly the kind of geometry where the spherical version is *needed* (Cartesian breaks down at that field of view). Applying spherical NLFFF + AIA loop comparison to a pre/post-flare event with a footpoint-vs-height metric and a free-energy estimate (≈0.8 ×10³² ergs, vs. the M8.7 flare release of ≈0.5 ×10³²) is a reasonable piece of new application work.
3. **Sensitivity to the global boundary condition** (synoptic frame vs. flux-dispersal model) is a somewhat fresh angle, and the pseudo–Monte-Carlo error estimate on the boundary field is a nice touch.

**What is *not* new (and the paper is largely upfront about this):**

1. **The core method does not originate here.** The authors explicitly note that Wiegmann (2007) already implemented the NLFFF optimization in spherical geometry, and Tadesse et al. (2009, 2011) further developed it. They are testing "J. McTiernan's version" of McTiernan's own FORTRAN 90 / SSW code.
2. **The Low & Lou validation was already done.** The paper states plainly that "Tadesse et al. (2009) have already tested the NLFFF code implemented by Wiegmann (2007) with the Low & Lou analytical solution." Guo et al. re-run it as an "independent validation." That is a re-benchmark, not a discovery.
3. **The individual techniques are all borrowed.** The optimization method is Wheatland et al. (2000); PFSS is Schatten et al. The real novel content is (a) the head-to-head Cartesian-vs-spherical field-of-view benchmark and (b) the SDO/AIA case study. But the underlying code, the analytic validation, the weighting scheme, the preprocessing, and the diagnostic metrics are all inherited from Wiegmann (2007), Tadesse et al. (2009), Wiegmann et al. (2006), and Schrijver et al. (2006)/Metcalf et al. (2008). The authors are honest about this, which is a strength, but it also caps the originality: the contribution is "we re-validated someone else's spherical NLFFF code and showed it beats the Cartesian version at large fields of view, and we applied it to one M8.7 flare." The application results (NLFFF matches EUV loops better than PFSS, energy drops after the flare) are expected and broadly consistent with the field at the time rather than surprising. Well-executed and serviceable, but not field-shaping — the free-energy and loop-agreement conclusions are qualitatively what prior NLFFF work had already suggested, and the whole line of work was soon subsumed by Wiegmann & Inhester (2010) and the Wiegmann et al. ## Methodology (described to reproduction level)

The paper has two parts: an **analytic code validation** (§3) and a **real-data application** to an M8.7 flare active region (§4). The "central experiment" is the §4 before/after-flare spherical NLFFF, but it is only meaningful once the §3 pipeline is reproduced, so I describe both.

## Assumptions the methodology papers over

1. **Force-free validity at the base.** Method requires ∇×B = αB everywhere, but the same paper (citing Gary 2001) concedes plasma β ≈ 1 at the photosphere where the drive data live, so the drive field is *not* force-free. They "paper over" this with preprocessing that strips net force/torque — i.e., they impose force-freeness on the input rather than relax the field to it.

2. **Isolation condition is violated, not met.** NLFFF optimization is only well-posed on an isolated, flux-balanced, self-contained field (Molodensky/Aly/Sakurai). The data contain open field lines from NOAA 11401/11407 into interplanetary space, and the four ARs interconnect. The paper itself says flux balance is necessary *but not sufficient* for isolation, yet proceeds without demonstrating the wedge is isolated.

3. **The side/top boundaries are assumed current-free (PFSS-like) and their influence is damped by a weighting buffer.** Since the true field on those surfaces is unknown, the final model inherits whatever topology the (poorly-constrained) PFSS puts there. n_buf=6 and the cosine profile are not studied for sensitivity; results depend on them.

4. **PFSS boundary/initial error (27–34% normalized, correlation 0.93–0.96) is accepted as "good enough."** That is a large discrepancy between the intended global field and what the PFSS actually reproduces, and it propagates into the NLFFF initialization and top/side boundaries.

5. **The global radial field itself is an unvalidated assumption.** (a) Synoptic map implicitly ignores differential rotation and the authors assert — without checking — that the field *outside* the AR group was essentially static over a ~27-day Carrington rotation. (b) Polar fields are unobserved and filled by Sun et al. (2011) interpolation, and these polar fields control the open-field/"hairy Sun" structure. (c) For flux-dispersal, they assume its physics (meridional-flow speed, dispersal rate, etc.) makes it more physical than the synoptic frame.

6. **The off-FOV field is replaced by the *radial* (LoS→radial) component only**, assuming that captures the true field there; the full vector information that exists in the FOV is dropped for the surrounding disk.

7. **The 180° ambiguity resolution injects a force-free/potential prior into the input.** Weak-field regions are resolved by a *potential-field* acute-angle method after annealing, so the "observed" data already presuppose a near-potential configuration — a circular prior that biases the whole NLFFF.

8. **VFISV inversion assumptions**: Milne–Eddington atmosphere, filling factor = 1 (uniform filling, no partial filling or multi-strand structure), fixed damping 0.5 — all bias field strength/area and hence the NLFFF drive.

9. **Preprocessing assumes residual net force/torque is instrumental noise**, not real photospheric stress. Removing it within the "noise level" silently converts a non-force-free drive into a near-force-free one.

10. **The noise model is synthetic and hand-chosen** (Gaussian, component-scaled to max field strength), matched loosely to Wiegelmann 2006's "noisy model II," so the *improvement-from-preprocessing* claim is tied to a fabricated noise model rather than a measured one.

11. **Low & Lou is a weak proxy for reality.** The "gold standard" is a single, smooth, axisymmetric point-source solution; passing it says little about recovery of real multi-scale, non-axisymmetric, twisted active regions. Validation of the method is extrapolated beyond the regime it tested.

12. **No ground truth for the real AR.** The §4 "validation" is purely qualitative visual matching of field lines to EUV (the authors admit it "is not ideal"), with no independent coronal-field measurement and no quantitative goodness-of-fit. The central real-data claim rests on eyeball comparison.

13. **Treating EUV loops as direct proxies for magnetic field lines.** EUV is optically thin, temperature-selective, line-of-sight-integrated plasma emission, not a field-line tracer; matching it to the model presumes the plasma is uniformly and temperature-independently distributed along the lines.

14. **The crude shape metric** (footpoint distance vs. max height) implicitly assumes quasi-semicircular loops and that height>separation ⇒ non-potentiality; it carries no twist/direction information and is sensitive to the chosen footpoint root set.

15. **The "flare energy" interpretation is the weakest link.** E^free(02:58) − E^free(04:10) (0.5–0.8×10³² erg) is *taken* to be flare energy, but (a) the two epochs use *different* global boundary maps and *different* PFSS evolutions, so the difference conflates flare evolution with boundary/model change; (b) the flux-dispersal and synoptic choices give two different answers (0.8 vs. 0.5×10³²), with no objective way to choose; (c) a typical M8.7 flare's radiated/kinetic energy is ~10³¹–10³² erg, so the claimed free-energy release is plausibly an overestimate that mixes in modeling systematics.

16. **Convergence/iteration-history dependence is acknowledged but not addressed.** The paper states results "depend on the initial condition and the iteration history," yet shows no convergence curves, no stopping criterion, no multi-initialization sensitivity, and no exploration of l_max at large radius (just "a smaller l_max is used") — so the real-data numbers are not demonstrated to be converged or robust.

17. **Resolution is far below the data.** HMI is ~0.03″≈0.03°/px; the vector field is downsampled to ~0.5° and the NLFFF run at 0.5°/point. The authors claim this is fine for "large-scale" properties but do not test how much of the (qualitatively) claimed structure is a smoothing artifact of this resampling.

18. **The Cartesian comparison is partially self-degrading.** Its bottom boundary keeps heliographic components on a tangent plane "because people usually assume heliographic ≈ Cartesian at small FOV," so part of the "spherical wins" result is an artifact of that forced assumption rather than a clean geometry comparison.

19. **Same-footpoint tracing of PFSS and NLFFF** (Figs. 7–8) is imposed though the two models have different topologies; the comparison metric is thereby conditioned on a footpoint set that is not equally natural for both.

## Assessment

This is a competent, unusually candid SolarPhysics *methodology* paper. Its §3 validation (Cases 1–3, noise, Cartesian-vs-spherical) is rigorous and standard, and the authors are refreshingly honest that the EUV comparison "is not ideal" and that, once errors are included, the flux-dispersal vs. synoptic free-energy difference is only "marginally comparable." The contribution (re-validating an existing spherical NLFFF code, plus a before/after-flare flux-dispersal-initialized application) is an incremental extension of Wiegelmann (2007)/Tadesse (2009, 2011) rather than a new method, and its validation is a single analytic solution that barely overlaps real AR complexity.

## Understanding of the paper's logical argument

**Problem.** Nonlinear force-free field (NLFFF) extrapolations for solar coronal fields were almost all implemented in Cartesian geometry. Cartesian grids assume the field of view is small relative to the solar disk; when a large region is observed (e.g., the ~71.7° × 71.5° group of interacting active regions around NOAA 11402, observed by *SDO*/HMI on 23 Jan 2012), spherical curvature matters. **The argument proceeds in four steps:**

1. **Validation on an analytical solution (Sec. 3).** The code (J. McTiernan's implementation of the Wiegelmann-style optimization, minimizing a functional of Lorentz force plus divergence, Eq. 3) is tested against the axially-symmetric Low & Lou P₁,₁ NLFFF. Three ideal cases bracket the practical situation:

   - **Case 3** (Case 2 plus a cosine tapering buffer zone on the data-less boundaries): improves all metrics to near-Case-1 levels — justifies the weighting function, a substantive practical contribution.

4. **Validation against EUV (Sec. 4.3).** Overlaying modeled field lines on *SDO*/AIA images: before the flare the PFSS projection of the NOAA 11402 loops deviates clearly westward, while the NLFFF reproduces the observed non-potential loop shapes (bipolar and quadrupolar) and the high EUV loops above 50 Mm; quantitatively, many NLFFF field lines have maximum height exceeding footprint separation (a signature of non-potentiality) where PFSS lines essentially never do. **Strengths.**

- **Rigorous validation hierarchy.** The bracketing design — ideal all-boundary data, bottom-only data, bottom-only + weighting buffer — is the right way to test a solver. - **Honest scoping of uncertainty.** The pseudo-Monte-Carlo error estimate on the global radial boundary, and the explicit statement that the two free-energy estimates differ only "marginally" beyond those errors, give the paper a notably careful statistical posture for 2012-era NLFFF work.

1. **Validation of the physics, not the code alone, is left open.** The Low & Lou test validates the *solver*, but the *physical claim* (the NLFFF picture of the corona) rests on qualitative overlay of field lines on EUV images. The authors acknowledge this ("the qualitative nature of this comparison is not ideal, however remains the best option"), but a small-quantification of the overlay (e.g., correlation of loop traces, or loop-top heights as a function of height with uncertainties) would have strengthened the central observational claim. The footnote/height-vs-footprint-separation statistic in Figure 8 is a good start but is presented without error bars or a defined sample.

2. **Sensitivity to the initial condition is reported but not fully exploited.** The NLFFF optimization is non-convex and known to be sensitive to the initial guess (the authors state "the final state … depends on the initial condition"). They initialize with a single PFSS per boundary case. A multi-initialization study (e.g., PFSS with and without a vertical background field, or PFSS plus a twisted component) would be the natural robustness test, especially since the whole point is to capture non-potential structure that the potential initial guess must be driven away from.

3. **The isolation/torque-free argument is brief relative to its importance.** Sec. 3.3 invokes Mlodensky/Alvy/Sakurai torque-free and force-free conditions on closed surfaces as motivation that a larger field of view reduces the violation of the isolation requirement — this is a real physical constraint on the validity of the whole spherical NLFFF enterprise, and a quantitative estimate of the violated flux/torque integrals on the top boundary of their actual computational domain would have made the "spherical is better" conclusion sharper.

4. **Cartesian comparison is not fully apples-to-apples.** In Sec. 3.4 the Cartesian code uses the "fulfilled in the Cartesian coordinate system without weighting" version, i.e., an older variant without the buffer zone, while the spherical code uses the new weighting version (though with buffer zones of 6 grids and then also without). The conclusion "spherical is better for F.O.V. > 20°×20°" therefore partly conflates geometry with the presence of the weighting function. A control run — Cartesian *with* weighting — would cleanly isolate the geometric contribution.

5. **Free-energy comparison with the flare is suggestive but loose.** The free-energy difference between the two times (0.8 × 10³², 0.5 × 10³² ergs) is used as if it tracks the flare energy, but 10³² ergs is a substantial fraction of, or of the order of, the total flare energy in different channels (radiative vs. particle vs. mechanical), and the errors quoted would likely swallow much of the *difference* in free energy. Stating the flare energy comparison more conservatively (orders of magnitude, not matching) would prevent over-reading.

6. **Resolution.** 0.5° per point is low relative to HMI's native ~0.5"/pixel (~0.03° as the authors note). While adequate for the large-loop scale argued, it means the NLFFF cannot resolve the actual flare core structure, and the pre-flare/post-flare differences may partly stem from large-scale reconnection rather than from the specific eruption physics of NOAA 11402. The authors flag this but it does cap the fidelity of the "better model" claim.

**Overall.** This is a well-structured, careful, and internally consistent paper. The logical chain from code validation → geometry justification → observational validation is sound, the experiments are controlled, and the conclusions are appropriately hedged. The main limitations are (a) the observational validation remaining mostly qualitative, (b) robustness to the NLFFF initialization being stated but not tested, and (c) the Cartesian comparison conflating geometry with the weighting-function upgrade. For a 2012 methods-and-application paper in solar physics, it is solid work whose operational recommendations (weighting buffer, larger F.O.V., spherical geometry for full-disk data) became standard practice in the field; its originality lies in the validation and application rather than in new mathematics.

## Audit

**1. Is the comparison fair?**

- **Spherical vs. Cartesian (Table 2):** This is a genuinely well-designed test — same Low & Lou "truth" field, same PFSS initialization, same 6-grid buffer, metrics computed over the same inner region (buffer excluded) in both geometries, with L_f and L_d honestly computed over each method's own domain. Same McTiernan SSW codebase both ways, so it is a geometry comparison, not a code comparison. **But** it rests on exactly one analytical field (the P₁,₁ axially symmetric Low & Lou mode), only three field-of-view sizes (θ_l = 20°, 40°, 60°), and a single wedge grid (30 × 54 × 61). The "beats Cartesian above 20°×20°" threshold is interpolated from one point sitting right at the boundary — it is an assertion about an unmeasured crossover, not a demonstrated one. No statistical treatment whatsoever, only point estimates.
- **Synoptic frame vs. flux-dispersal (Table 3):** **Fair in an internal sense, but the two arms are not equivalent models.** The flux-dispersal model embeds a physical advection/diffusion simulation plus data assimilation; the synoptic frame is a geometric composite (a 27-day-old Carrington-2119 map with the observed disk pasted in). Finding that the more sophisticated arm gives slightly lower L_f/L_d and higher energy is almost built into the design — one would expect a model that explicitly enforces flux continuity to produce more consistent initial/boundary conditions. There is no control in which the physics is stripped, so "slightly better metrics" cannot be cleanly attributed to a cause.
- **NLFFF vs. PFSS:** This is a near-tautological comparison. PFSS is not a competing coronal extrapolation in the same class — it is the current-free limit and, in this very pipeline, also serves as the *initialization* for the NLFFF. It will of course lose on force-freeness metrics (∇×B ≈ 0 by construction is not the point; the point is that PFSS cannot bend high over its footpoint separations) and on "tubes taller than footpoint spacing." The authors are candid that the real test — the EUV-line overlay in Figure 7 — is **qualitative** and that they "recognize the qualitative nature of this comparison is not ideal" and is "the best option in the absence of a more reliable quantitative alternative." The one quantitative surrogate (Fig. 8: max-height vs. footpoint-distance) is a scatter comparison with no overlap metric, no statistical test, and an arbitrary 50 Mm cut.
- **Energy-released claim (0.5–0.8 × 10³² erg from ΔE_free):** This is where fairness breaks down further. A "free energy" is relative to the minimum-energy potential field; a decrease in modeled free energy after a flare measures *change in the extrapolated configuration*, and is only loosely tied to actual flare energy radiated. The calculation stacks at least four assumptions: (a) the 2.5 R☉ outer bound where energy is integrated; (b) the PFSS side/top boundaries being representative; (c) the flare being the *only* energetic change between 02:58 and 04:10 UT, while the active-region group (11401/11402/11405/11407) was simultaneously undergoing a filament eruption and CME and evolving structurally over ~70 minutes; (d) the 0.5° grid resolution the authors themselves note is low. The 60% spread between the two boundary-choice arms (1.5→2.0 vs 1.0→1.2 ×10³² erg) is larger than the quoted pseudo-Monte-Carlo error bars, so the "flare energy" is dominated by the boundary-model choice, not by measurement precision.

**2. Are the baselines current?**

Adequate *for 2012*, and the paper is honest about it. PFSS is a standard (if weak) baseline; the Cartesian NLFFF comparison uses the same code, which is fair. The flux-dispersal model is the current Schrijver & De Rosa (2003) lineage. Notably, the paper explicitly names the newer Wiegelmann & Inhester (2010) optimization method with its error/missing-data regularization term and declines to test it, citing compute cost — so the authors knowingly compare against the older objective functional rather than the best available one. For the observational validation, only line-overlay and a scatter plot are used; by 2012 there were already pixel-to-line quantitative metrics in use (e.g., in the SDO-NLFFF comparison literature), and the paper admits it has none. For a 2012 paper this is defensible rather than negligent.

**3. Error bars / statistical significance?**

- The only error bars in the paper are the **pseudo-Monte-Carlo standard deviations in Table 3**: 10 realizations with 10-G Gaussian noise added *only to the global radial field* (citing Hoeksema et al. 2012), combined with the original, i.e., n = 11 per case. Deliberately, **no noise is added to the HMI vector magnetogram itself** — the actual input with the largest and most structured errors (180° ambiguity resolution, Milne–Eddington inversion, preprocessing). So the error bars reflect only a fraction of the actual error budget, and they are *understated*. The authors even admit this produces underestimates by construction ("the random errors are not added to the vector magnetic field," in order "to focus on the effects of the two global magnetic field models").
- **No statistical significance anywhere.** The Low & Lou comparisons (Tables 1–2) are single point estimates with no error analysis (arguably fair for a deterministic code test, though a small parameter sweep would strengthen it). In the application, there is a **single event, single active-region group, single pre/post pair** — no ensemble, no repeated trials, no significance test between the flux-dispersal and synoptic-frame metrics, and no test on the NLFFF-vs-PFSS quality differences. The summary concedes the point: "if we consider the errors as listed in Table 3 ... the metrics are [only] **marginally comparable**" — i.e., the flux-dispersal advantage is within the (albeit partial) error bars. The "NLFFF > PFSS above 50 Mm" claim is likewise supported only by the qualitative figure.

## Verdict

The paper is methodologically honest — it labels clearly which conclusions are qualitative, admits the NLFFF/PFSS comparison is a category error in the strong sense, concedes the free-energy difference is within errors, and acknowledges it skipped the newest optimizer. But audited against its own strongest claim (flare energy release 0.5–0.8×10³² erg from ΔE_free with flux-dispersal initialization), the evidence is thin: n=1 event, under-stated error bars that exclude the primary data errors, an uncontrolled two-arm comparison in which the favored arm is intrinsically more physical, a tautological main-model baseline, and no significance testing anywhere. The Low & Lou Cartesian-vs-spherical test is the strongest piece of quantitative evidence in the paper and is reasonably fair, but it is one analytic case, three FoV sizes, one grid, with no error analysis. This is a competent, incremental SDO-era modeling paper — the kind that was standard for its year — but the flagship quantitative claim is suggestive rather than established.

## [1210.0998v1-weak-point] Weakest link in the argument


# Weak-link review: Guo, Ding, Liu, Sun, DeRosa & Wiegelmann, *Modeling Magnetic Field Structure of a Solar Active Region Corona using Nonlinear Force-Free Fields in Spherical Geometry* (arXiv:1210.0998v1)

## The argument chain

1. **Code validation (Sec. 3):** Recover the known Low & Lou $P_{1,1}$ NLFFF. With full-boundary data (Case 1) the reconstruction is essentially exact ($L_f,L_d\to0$, $C_{\text{vec}}=1$); with realistic bottom-only + PFSS boundaries (Cases 2/3) it stays good ($\epsilon\approx0.92$–$1.01$). *Entailed — there is ground truth here.*
2. **Spherical vs. Cartesian (Sec. 3.4, Table 2):** For fields of view $\geq20^\circ$, the spherical code yields much smaller $L_f,L_d$ than the Cartesian one. *Entailed by the table.*
3. **Application to SDO/HMI (Sec. 4):** Run NLFFF for an active region before/after an M8.7 flare, initialized from two PFSS surfaces (synoptic vs. flux-dispersion). **Conclusion: the flux-dispersion NLFFF is "better," and NLFFF beats PFSS both in the flare core and out to large EUV loops above 50 Mm.**

## The single weakest link

The load-bearing, non-entailed step is in **Sec. 4.3 / the summary: the move from "slightly lower force-free and divergence-free metrics, and larger stored free magnetic energy" to "this NLFFF (with the flux-dispersion boundary) is the better / more physically accurate model, and NLFFF outperforms PFSS."**

Why the data do not support this specific leap:

- **The force-free metric measures self-consistency, not accuracy.** $L_f=\langle B^{-2}|(\nabla\times B)\times B|^2\rangle$ and $L_d=\langle|\nabla\cdot B|^2\rangle$ quantify how closely the solver satisfies *its own assumed governing equations*, not the distance to the true coronal field. In the validation section that distinction is harmless, because a ground-truth field (Low & Lou) exists and the *correlation* metrics ($C_{\text{vec}},1-E_n$) are computed against it — there NLFFF genuinely wins. In the observational section there is **no ground truth**, so $L_f,L_d$ are the only residual "evidence" that one NLFFF is preferable to another. But both the synoptic-initialized and flux-dispersion-initialized runs are driven to small $L_f,L_d$ by the optimizer from the *same* HMI bottom boundary; the small difference between them reflects how cleanly each converged, not which is closer to reality. Using "slightly lower, **therefore** better" as the criterion to prefer the flux-dispersion model is a category error: it rewards a model for being a better solution of an assumption, not for being right.

- **The authors' own error analysis voids the distinction.** The pseudo-Monte-Carlo in Sec. 4.3 (10 noisy surface fields, 10 G level) gives standard deviations on the metrics; the summary concedes that once errors are allowed, "the metrics are marginally comparable." A within-error-bar difference cannot license "better."

- **The "50 Mm" claim is qualitative, not measured.** The only thing that can actually speak to physical fidelity in the observation section is the Figure 7 overlay of extrapolated field lines on the EUV image — an eye-ball of projection overlap, which the paper itself flags: *"We recognize that the qualitative nature of this comparison is not ideal… in the absence of a more reliable quantitative method."* The "loops higher than 50 Mm" wording in the abstract/summary is then backed only by Figure 8's proxy criterion (field-line max height vs. footpoint separation, "roughly the shape of a field line"), with no error bars and no calibration against observed loop heights. EUV emissions are not strictly along force-free field lines, and 2-D projection makes loop-height recovery from an image impossible. So the quantitative-sounding "50 Mm" is, in substance, an unvalidated qualitative impression.

- The **free-energy/flare consistency** argument (flux-dispersion difference $0.8\times10^{32}$ ergs vs. the ~$0.8\times10^{32}$ ergs released by the flare, vs. $0.5\times10^{32}$ for the synoptic case) is a secondary, physically weaker cross-check on top of this: flare energy is not the available free energy, and only a fraction of free energy is released per event, so a "covers the flare energy" argument does not entail the model is the correct one.

**Net:** the paper's headline conclusion — spherical NLFFF, particularly with the flux-dispersion initialization, is the *more accurate* model and reproduces loops out to >50 Mm — stops being entailed at the point where, lacking ground truth, the authors substitute (a) self-consistency metrics that by their own significance analysis are indistinguishable between the two NLFFF variants, and (b) a self-admittedly unreliable qualitative overlay, for a measure of accuracy. The data establish that the code produces a *self-consistent* force-free field; they do not establish that this particular field (as opposed to the PFSS, or the other NLFFF, or the true field) is the accurate one. The validation section (where ground truth exists) is clean and credible; it is precisely the step from "validated solver" to "validated physical reconstruction of a real active region" that is the gap.

## Assessment

The paper is competent, well-structured, and unusually honest about its limitations, and its Low & Lou validation plus the quantitative Cartesian-vs-spherical comparison (Table 2) are genuinely useful. But the method is not new (optimization NLFFF, spherical geometry, and the Low–Lou test all predate it in Wiegelmann 2007 and Tadesse 2009/2011), and its central physical claim rests on a metric that does not measure what the claim asserts, confirmed only by a qualitative overlay the authors themselves distrust. A solid incremental code-validation/application paper whose strongest-looking conclusion is its weakest-evidenced one.

# Critique: Writing and Framing

**Paper:** Guo et al., "Modeling Magnetic Field Structure of a Solar Active Region Corona using Nonlinear Force-Free Fields in Spherical Geometry" (arXiv:1210.0998v1)

## Sentences that overclaim

**1.** Abstract: *"NLFFF extraplations using the flux-dispersion model as the boundary condition have slightly lower, therefore better, force-free and divergence-free metrics, and contain larger free magnetic energy."*
The connective "therefore better" is doing unearned work. Lower $L_f$ and $L_d$ only say the optimizer got closer to the *mathematical* force-free/divergence-free target — which any NLFFF optimizer is tuned to do regardless of how well the field matches reality. They do not by themselves constitute evidence of *better physical fidelity*. The pseudo-Monte-Carlo error bars in Table 3 (±0.02–0.04 in $10^{32}$ erg units, against a free-energy difference of ~0.05) show the residual differences are inside the estimated noise, so "slightly lower" is arguably "the same within uncertainty."

**2.** Abstract / §5: *"...the NLFFF performs better than the PFSS not only for the core field of the flare productive region, but also for large EUV loops higher than 50 Mm."*
This is stated with the confidence of a validated result, but the only evidence is a field-line overlay on EUV images — and in §4.3 (p. 18) the authors themselves write *"We recognize that the qualitative nature of this comparison is not ideal, however it remains the best option in the absence of a more reliable quantitative alternative."* A generalised performance claim ("large EUV loops higher than 50 Mm") inferred from a single event with one pair of timestamps is a clear overreach.

**3.** §4.1 (p. 14): *"Due to the large field of view, the NLFFF in the spherical geometry is necessary for studying the magnetic field configuration of the flaring active region and the magnetic connections between these active regions."*
"Is necessary" is too strong. The Cartesian code exists and is the default for smaller FoVs; what is defensible is that *spherical geometry is preferable / reduces projection-induced error* at this FoV size. The paper's own Table 2 shows the gap is large at 20° but the qualitative conclusion holds — the word "necessary" isn't justified.

**4.** §4.3 (p. 17): *"The difference in free energies between the two times, which we assume corresponds to the energy released by the M8.7 flare, is 0.8 × 10^32 ergs or 0.5 × 10^32 ergs..."*
The word "assume" signals that the *entire quantitative flare-energy linkage is asserted, not demonstrated*. There is no physical argument for why the 42-minute snapshot difference in NLFFF free energy should equal the flare's radiated (or total) energy — most free-energy change in a flare is released over a much longer timescale and into particles, flows, and turbulence that the snapshot pair doesn't see. The sentence also commits the classic "equating the bookkeeping number with the physical quantity" fallacy. In the *next* sentence the authors do the natural extension (compare to $E_{\rm flare}$), but the "assume" caveat is so weak it functions as if the assumption were proven.

**5.** §3.4: *"All the metrics in Table 2 show that the NLFFF code in the spherical geometry performs better than that in the Cartesian one when the field of view of the bottom boundary is larger than 20° × 20°."*
This is both *understated* and *overstated*. Understated: at 20° the spherical is already more than two orders of magnitude better in $L_f^2$ (0.36 vs 96.92 G² Mm⁻²), so "large, say 20°" undersells the actual crossover. Overstated: the result comes from a single axisymmetric Low & Lou $P_{1,1}$ test on a single cut location. Generalising the "20° × 20°" rule to arbitrary active regions from one test geometry is not justified by the evidence in the paper.

**6.** §3.1 / p. 2: *"A limited class of axisymmetric semi-analytical NLFFs also exist (Low & Lou 1990), and these are usually adopted as the standard test model of the above mentioned numerical algorithms, as has been done in Schrijver et al. (2006)."*
These tests are the *only* quantitative validation in the paper. Using a single axisymmetric eigenfunction as the sole benchmark, then concluding from it that the code is "validated," is a framing that lets the authors use the strong word "standard test model" (borrowed from prior literature) as cover for a rather thin test set. A more robust test would use multiple $(n,m)$ eigenfunctions and compare against the analytic solution at the source surface (not just the volume metrics).

**7.** §4.3 (p. 18): *"The magnetic loops in active region NOAA 11402 are highly nonpotential, since the distance between the footpoints are less than the height of the loops, and the core region is much brighter than the surroundings, which is an indication of large electric current."*
"Indication of large electric current" is an interpretive leap from *loop geometry + EUV brightness* to a *current-density claim*. In a force-free formalism, geometry (small footpoint separation relative to apex height) can imply non-potentiality, but the EUV brightness statement is a thermodynamic observation and isn't a direct current measurement (coronal EUV comes from hot plasma, not from $J$ itself). The sentence should say "consistent with non-potentiality" rather than "indication of large electric current."

**8.** §4.1 (p. 13): *"...the damping constant has been set to 0.5 and the filling factor to 1."*
This is the only mention of a knob that materially affects the inferred $B_h$ and the 180° ambiguity resolution; no sensitivity test is given. The paper's framing lets VFISV's choices pass as a background detail when in fact they feed the entire downstream NLFFF. An adversarial review will note that the conclusions of §4 hinge on a single parameterisation chosen without justification.

**9.** §5 (p. 20): *"We conclude that the NLFFF model performs better than the PFSS model, especially for highly non-potential active regions before eruptive activities."*
The conclusion is general, but the evidence base is one flare (M8.7, NOAA 11402) with 1–3 ARs in the field of view, two timestamps 42 min apart, and qualitative EUV comparisons. The "before eruptive activities" restriction is reasonable but the phrase "conclude" is again too strong; "we find evidence consistent with..." would match what the data actually show.

**10.** Abstract: *"NLFFF extraplations using the flux-dispersion model ... contain larger free magnetic energy."*
Same sentence as (1) above, but note the asymmetry in the *sign of* the claim: a higher free energy in the model is generally *not* a quality attribute — it's a property of the boundary condition (the flux-dispersion map has more small-scale structure to be extrapolated and stores more potential energy that gets "spent" by the force-free fit). Calling it a result about the model without tying it to an observed phenomenon misleads the reader.

## Numerical inconsistency worth flagging

On p. 17 the authors state *"the free magnetic energy derived from the flux-dispersion model at 02:58 UT (2.0 × 10^32 ergs) is thus larger than that from the synoptic frame (1.5 × 10^32 ergs). Similarly, the magnetic energy at 04:10 UT from the flux-dispersion model (1.2 × 10^32 ergs) is also larger than that from the synoptic frame (1.0 × 10^32 ergs). The difference in free energies between the two times ... is 0.8 × 10^32 ergs or 0.5 × 10^32 ergs."*

But Table 3 gives $E_m^{{\rm free}}$ = **0.20, 0.15, 0.12, 0.10** (in units of $10^{32}$ ergs) respectively — i.e. the table's values are exactly 0.1× the text, and the quoted "difference" of 0.8 / 0.5 is 10× what the table's numbers give (0.08 / 0.05). This is a decimal-place slip somewhere, and a careful reader will not know which of the table or the text to trust. An adversarial reviewer will call this out immediately because it touches the only quantitative "flare energy" claim in the paper.

## Other writing issues

- The abstract has a hyphenation artifact in "Solar Dynamics Observato-ry" and a consistent misspelling of "extrapolations" as "extraplations" throughout §4.3 and §5 (used at least five times). This is a cosmetic issue but signals the paper has not been carefully proofread.
- The §3.3 "noisy model" tests add 20% per-component Gaussian noise to the *bottom boundary* only, with no noise on the PFSS side/top boundaries. The "improvement" from preprocessing may partly be an artifact of how the noisy case is constructed, not a robust measure.
- The PFSS used as the side/top boundary for the NLFFF extrapolation is at $l_{\max} = 360$ over the full disk, which is extraordinarily high for what is usually a coarse diagnostic. The paper does not comment on this or justify it, even though it materially affects the "no data" boundary.
- The §4.2 discussion of the "polar region" problem ends by using a Sun et al. (2011) interpolation to fill it in; no test of how sensitive the NLFFF is to that fill is given, even though the whole purpose of §4 is to *use* the global map.

## What an adversarial reviewer would put in their first paragraph

> This paper validates an existing NLFFF optimization code in spherical geometry against a single analytic test (the Low & Lou $P_{1,1}$ solution) and then applies it to a single flare event (M8.7, NOAA 11402, 2012 Jan 23) at two timestamps four dozen minutes apart. The headline claims — that "the NLFFF code in the spherical geometry performs better than that in the Cartesian one when the field of view of the bottom boundary is large, say, 20° × 20°" and that "the NLFFF performs better than the PFSS" — rest almost entirely on (i) one axisymmetric eigenfunction in one particular cut of a synthetic Sun, and (ii) qualitative overlays of field lines on EUV imagery, which the authors themselves acknowledge "is not ideal." ## Bottom line

The paper is methodologically competent and the code validation (Test 1 in Table 1) is clean, but the *framing* oversells the evidence at every level: a single $P_{1,1}$ benchmark and a single-event EUV overlay are not enough to support either "spherical is better, threshold 20° × 20°" or "NLFFF better than PFSS for large loops" as stated. The "assume corresponds to the flare energy" sentence, combined with the table/text 10× energy discrepancy, means the most concrete quantitative claim in the paper — the one that actually *ties* the model to a physical observable — is the least defensible.

**Recommendation: Borderline (weak accept)**
**Score: 6 / 10**

This paper validates the Wiegmann (2007) spherical NLFFF optimization code against the Low & Lou analytical solution, performs a Cartesian-vs-spherical comparison at increasing field-of-view sizes, and applies the spherical NLFFF to SDO/HMI data of NOAA 11402 ahead of an M8.7 flare. The validation is methodical, with multiple cases (ideal, realistic, noisy, pre-processed boundaries), and the application to a spatially extended, multi-AR active region group is well motivated.

**Strengths**
- Systematic validation against an analytical solution with well-documented metrics.
- Useful quantitative comparison showing the spherical geometry outperforms the Cartesian box for FoV ≳ 20° × 20° (Table 2).
- Thoughtful treatment of boundary-condition choice (synoptic frame vs. flux-dispersion model) with pseudo-Monte-Carlo error estimates.
- Good recognition that NLFFF field lines at 02:58 UT expand higher than PFSS, consistent with the non-potential EUV loop structure before the flare, and relax after the flare.

**Weaknesses**
1. **Limited novelty of method.** The authors explicitly test a code already implemented by Wiegmann (2007) and validated by Tadesse et al. (2009, 2011). Their stated purpose is "independent validation," which is valuable but does not advance the method.
2. **Qualitative EUV comparison.** The authors overlay field lines on multi-wavelength AIA images (Fig. 7) and note that the NLFFF "performs better" than PFSS. They acknowledge this is qualitative and offer no rigorous metric (e.g., statistical matching of footpoint pairs, loop-height histograms, or a quantitative misalignment distance) that would allow a reader to assess the significance of the improvement.
3. **Resolution limitation.** The NLFFF is run at 0.5°/pixel, whereas SDO/HMI has a native spacing of ~0.03°/pixel. The authors acknowledge this in §5 but do not test how resolution affects the NLFFF results. For a flare-productive AR with strong small-scale flux concentrations, this is a significant source of error that could alter the free-energy estimate.
4. **No time evolution.** The study is limited to two snapshots (pre- and post-flare). The paper's motivation (§1) mentions studying the evolution of free magnetic energy and relative helicity, but these quantities are neither computed nor discussed.
5. **Single event.** One active region studied before and after one flare; claims about the general superiority of spherical NLFFF or the NLFFF-vs-PFSS performance for "large EUV loops >50 Mm" rest on a single example.

**Three changes that would most improve the paper**

1. **Quantify the NLFFF-vs-PFSS comparison against EUV loops.** Replace (or supplement) the visual overlays of Fig. 7 with a statistical metric — e.g., the mean footpoint-to-footpoint separation of field lines that pass through or near EUV loop footpoints, or a histogram of the vertical offset between model field-line apexes and the peak-intensity height of corresponding EUV structures. Even a simple nearest-neighbor distance distribution would give a confidence-weighted statement of how much better the NLFFF is.

2. **Include a resolution-convergence study.** Run the spherical NLFFF at 2 or 3 coarser and finer grid spacings (e.g., 1.0°, 0.25°, 0.1° — interpolating the HMI vector field accordingly) and report how the force-free/divergence-free metrics, the free energy, and the derived large-loop heights change. This directly addresses the primary concern a reviewer will have about whether the computed free energy (which the authors link to flare energy release) is artefact of the coarse grid.

3. **Extend the comparison to a second active region.** Applying the full pipeline (PFSS initialization from both boundary models, spherical NLFFF, EUV comparison) to at least one additional active region of different size, helicity sign, or eruptive outcome would make the conclusions about NLFFF-vs-PFSS performance and the synoptic-vs-flux-dispersion boundary sensitivity much less vulnerable to selection from a single event.

---

In short, the paper is well executed and the validation is careful, but it does not introduce a new method or a decisive new result. It reads as a solid confirmation-and-application note — useful for practitioners choosing between Cartesian and spherical NLFFF, but not one that shifts the field. It sits at the low end of acceptability for a regular paper.

# Peer Review

**Paper:** Modeling Magnetic Field Structure of a Solar Active Region Corona using Nonlinear Force-Free Fields in Spherical Geometry

## 1. Summary

The paper (i) validates the SSW optimization NLFFF code (J. McTiernan's implementation) in spherical geometry against the standard Low & Lou (1990) axisymmetric analytic solution — under ideal six-boundary data, bottom-boundary-only with PFSS on the other five surfaces (with and without a cosine taper-weighted buffer zone), and under two levels of Gaussian boundary noise (with and without force/torque removal preprocessing); (ii) compares the spherical code against its Cartesian counterpart at three bottom-field-of-view scales (20°, 40°, 60°); and (iii) applies the spherical NLFFF to SDO/HMI vector magnetograms of a five-region group (NOAA 11401–11407) before and after an M8.7 flare on 2012 Jan 23, initialized and sided by PFSS extrapolations built from either a synoptic frame or a Schrijver & De Rosa flux-dispersal model, with results compared to SDO/AIA EUV observations.

## 2. Strengths

- **Systematic code validation.** The staged test design (Case 1–3, two noise levels, preprocessed/unpreprocessed) is thorough and useful as a community reference, and the standard figures of merit (Cvec, CCS, EN, EM, ε, Lf, Ld) make it directly comparable with the Schrijver et al. (2006) and Metcalf et al. (2008) code comparisons.
- **Cartesian vs. spherical, FoV-scaled comparison (Table 2).** This is the paper's most valuable new element: it quantifies, for a realistic "bottom boundary only" setup, that Cartesian extrapolation degrades badly (Lf grows from 6.6 to 97, ε drops to 0.68) as the field of view increases, while the spherical code stays stable. The ~20° threshold is a practically useful guideline for anyone choosing between the two SSW codes.
- **Clean observational application.** The before/after-flare design, the two global-boundary strategies (synoptic frame vs. flux-dispersal inserted with HMI), the pseudo Monte Carlo error estimates, and the aspect-ratio (footpoint distance vs. maximum height) diagnostic of Figure 8 give a reasonable — if limited — quantitative handle on whether the NLFFF loop shapes match the EUV loops.
- The authors are generally honest about the limitations (qualitative nature of the EUV overlay, "marginally comparable" metrics in Section 5, coarse 0.5° grid).

## 3. Major concerns

**M1. The free-energy / flare-energy claim is not supported by the statistics.** Section 4.3 states "the difference in free energies between the two times, which we assume corresponds to the energy released by the M8.7 flare, is 0.8×10³² ergs or 0.5×10³² ergs." Against Table 3, this difference is only ~1–2σ given the quoted uncertainties (e.g., 2.0±0.3 vs 1.2±0.1 ×10³²), and it is obtained by subtracting two large, nearly equal energies (e.g., 1.69−1.57 = 0.12), a numerically fragile operation. More fundamentally, the Monte Carlo error budget injects noise (10 G) *only* into the global radial field and explicitly not into the HMI vector magnetogram, which is the dominant NLFFF input — so the quoted bars are almost certainly underestimates. A 72-minute separation between observations and the 2–6 h age mismatch of the global-field inputs further limit the interpretation. I would (a) remove the "corresponds to the energy released by the M8.7 flare" interpretation, or at least present it as an uncalibrated upper-bound comparison, and (b) extend the pseudo Monte Carlo to the vector magnetogram (including a representative 180°-ambiguity error model) before attaching errors to ΔEm_free.

**M2. The comparison of the two global boundary conditions is marginal, and the paper's own admission should be moved up.** The headline result that flux-dispersal-initiated NLFFFs have "slightly lower, therefore better" force-free metrics and "larger free magnetic energy" rests on differences (e.g., 3.41 vs 3.53 in Lf; 0.20 vs 0.15 ×10³³ in Em_free at 02:58) that are within or at the edge of the stated errors. The authors concede in Section 5 that "the metrics are marginally comparable," but the Abstract and Summary still present the flux-dispersal model as the preferred one as if it were settled (and Figure 6 shows only flux-dispersal results). The presentation should be brought into line with the statistics, and ideally a second active region or a different initialization (e.g., PFSS at another source-surface height) should be shown to check robustness.

**M3. The Cartesian vs. spherical comparison (Section 3.4, Table 2) is not fully volume-matched as described.** Lf and Ld are integrated over the full computation domains — the cubic box for Cartesian, but the *wedge-shaped region* for spherical — yet the text does not state the wedge's radial/angular extent for each θl. If the spherical wedges extended to 2.5 R⊙ while the Cartesian boxes' tops sit near r ≈ 1.35 R⊙ (l = 2R⊙tan(θl/2) above the tangent plane), the reported Lf, Ld and even ε are not directly comparable integrals. The authors must give the exact domain used for each pair so the comparison is apples-to-apples; alternatively, compute all metrics in the common intersection volume. Also, part of the Cartesian deficit stems from the *boundary-condition projection* (heliographic components imposed on a tangent plane), i.e., a user-practice error rather than a discretization deficit — worth stating explicitly so the 20° threshold is not over-interpreted as a property of the discretization.

**M4. Sensitivity of the observational NLFFF to its auxiliary inputs is untested.** The top/side boundaries are pure PFSS built from global maps that themselves match their input radial fields only at 93–96% correlation with 27–34% normalized error; the source-surface height, the lmax choice ("when the radius is large, a smaller lmax is used" — please quantify), the 0.5° resolution (≈3.5× coarser than native HMI), and the buffer width are all fixed with no sensitivity analysis. Given that the NLFFF result is being used to make (marginal) quantitative energy claims, at least a one-parameter-at-a-time study of Rs and resolution would substantially strengthen the observational section.

**M5. The validation-vs.-observation gap is under-discussed.** The code was validated on a smooth, single-axisymmetric Low–Lou field with a PFSS of only l = 7, whereas the observed global field is highly small-scale (hence the poor 0.93 correlation). It is not demonstrated that the *same* weighting/buffer settings remain optimal when the PFSS sides carry real solar-scale structure. A brief discussion of how much the Lf = 3.5–4.8 values in Table 3 (roughly two orders of magnitude above the clean Case-1/Case-3 values in Table 1) reflect boundary inconsistency versus genuine non-force-free structure would be valuable.

## 4. Minor points and errata

- Abstract/Intro: "Solar Dynamic **Obseratory**" → Observatory.
- Discussion: "active region **NOAA 11042**" → NOAA 11402 (line ~795). This is in the main-text conclusion and must be fixed.
- References: "Mc**Tti**ernan" in Metcalf et al. (2008).
- Fig. 2 caption: "**wadge** shaped region" → wedge-shaped.
- Fig. 7 caption: panel "(e) ... (d) Field lines of the NLFFF model at 04:10 UT" — the second label should be (f); there is no (f) as written, and Figure 8's caption refers to "Figures 7(c), 7(d), 7(e) and 7(f)."
- "A NLFFF procedure" → "An NLFFF procedure" (Sec. 1); capitalization drift between EN/EM and En/Em in Sec. 3.3.
- Noise model wording is self-contradictory: "ri is the random number in the range of −1 to 1 ... ri has a Gaussian distribution." Specify whether samples are truncated.
- In Section 3.3, clarify whether the PFSS for the top/side boundaries of the noisy runs is built from the *clean* analytic radial field (it should be) — as written it is ambiguous.
- Table 3: "They have the same value for the two different boundaries at each observation time" — they are equal only *as printed*; PFSS energies do depend on the input. Say "equal to within the quoted precision" and give the energy to one more significant figure to show the rounding.
- Figure 8: state whether open field lines are included — a single footpoint makes "distance of the two footpoints" ambiguous.
- Consider a one-sentence positioning vs. the Metcalf et al. (2008) multi-code comparison results, and vs. the Wiegelmann & Inhester (2010) improved regularization, since the latter is only mentioned at the very end.

## 5. Assessment

This is a careful, competently executed code-validation and first-application study. The Low–Lou verification is solid, and the FoV-scaled Cartesian/spherical benchmark is genuinely useful for the community. However, the originality is modest — the method, test model, preprocessing, and figures of merit are all standard and mostly pre-exist (Schrijver et al. 2006; Wiegelmann 2007; Tadesse et al. 2009, 2011; the authors themselves note Tadesse et al. 2009 already tested the spherical code) — and the observational section's two quantitative claims (flux-dispersal preferred; ΔEm_free ≈ flare energy) are statistically marginal, with error budgets that demonstrably exclude the dominant input. The physics returned is confirmatory (pre-flare loops are non-potential, post-flare more potential), and the authors themselves leave the physical questions to "further study." There are no correctness-breaking errors I can identify, the internal numbers are consistent (I checked the free-energy differences against Table 3), and the fixes required — repositioning the marginal claims, extending the error analysis to the vector magnetogram, specifying the Section 3.4 domains, and the errata list — are all feasible in a revision.

**Recommendation:** Minor-to-moderate revision; resubmit with the claims in M1–M3 addressed.

**Scores (0–5):** originality 2 (incremental extension of standard code-testing practice; the FoV-scaled geometry comparison is the only genuinely new element); overall 3 (sound and internally consistent execution, honest about limitations, but marginal statistics on the key observational claims and one incomplete comparison); impact 2 (useful community reference within the NLFFF sub-field, not practice-changing).

## Review: "Modeling Magnetic Field Structure of a Solar Active Region Corona using Nonlinear Force-Free Fields in Spherical Geometry" (Guo, Ding, Liu, Sun, DeRosa & Wiegelmann)

This is a careful, honest read-through of your method, and I want to be equally careful in grading it. The work is competent and internally consistent — the kind of paper that would be well received — but as it stands it is a methods-validation exercise plus one application, and I think it currently undersells its genuinely useful parts while overreaching on its most ambitious claim. Let me be specific.

### What you did well

1. **The Low–Lou benchmark is the right choice.** Verifying the code against an exact, axisymmetric NLFFF is the single most important thing in a "we tested a code" paper, and you do it correctly: both the ideal case (vector field on all six boundaries, Case 1) and the realistically constrained case (data on the bottom boundary only, PFSS on the rest, Cases 2–3). The Case 1 result — recovering the P₁,₁ solution to high accuracy — is exactly the sanity check a reviewer wants to see before any application is trusted.

2. **The weighting-function / buffer-region treatment is a real, practical contribution.** The cosine-tapered buffer that relaxes the top and side boundaries where data are absent (Section 2, Case 3) is a genuinely useful trick, and you demonstrate that it improves the Lorentz-force and divergence integrals over the no-weighting alternative. This is the sort of methodological detail that makes the code usable by others.

3. **The preprocessing study is well motivated.** Adding Gaussian noise and comparing with/without the net-force-and-torque-removal routine, and showing that the benefit grows with the noise level (Section 3.3), is a sensible and reproducible result.

4. **The spherical-vs-Cartesian comparison is the most original part of the paper.** This is where I think you have your strongest new result: that the spherical NLFFF outperforms the Cartesian one once the bottom-boundary field of view is large (≳ 20°×20°). This is practically important given that modern full-disk magnetograms (HMI, and future DKIST) make large fields of view the norm, not the exception.

### What needs work

1. **Originality is limited and you don't differentiate yourself clearly enough.** The code is not yours (Wiegelnann 2007 optimization, J. McTernan's SSW implementation, building on Tadese 2009/2011). The Low–Lou test and the PFSS initialization are standard. What makes *this* paper worth reading — the Cartesian/spherical comparison, and the use of a *global* PFSS to initialize an NLFFF for a multi-activity-region group — should be stated explicitly and early. Right now it's buried. A reviewer skimming the abstract (which emphasizes "we test a code") will under-rate the paper. **Recommendation:** reframe the abstract and intro around the two contributions you actually advance, and add a sentence on how your Cartesian/spherical test differs from DeRosa et al. (2009) and the earlier verification papers.

2. **The "spherical is better for ≳ 20°" conclusion rests on a single configuration.** The P₁,₁ solution is one specific NLFFF. Generalizing to "large field of view" from one test model is weak. Even one additional eigenfunction (e.g. P₂₁ or a tilted/less-symmetric configuration), or a small parameter sweep in the viewing angle, would make this claim much more persuasive than "we cut a cubic box of three sizes around one solution."

3. **Your boldest physical claim is the weakest part.** In Section 4.3 you say the change in free magnetic energy between the two snapshots (0.8×10³² and 0.5×10³² ergs, for the flux-dispersion vs. synoptic initialization) is what you "assume corresponds to the energy released by the M8.7 flare." This is a stretch in two ways: (a) stored NLFFF free energy is not flare energy — only a fraction goes into radiation and particles; (b) you're comparing two models with *different boundary conditions*, so the energy difference is part model, part input, not a clean measurement of released energy. You even note that "the metrics are marginally comparable" (Table 3) — so be consistent and soften this to order-of-magnitude consistency, not "corresponds to." A reviewer will pick this off.

4. **The main observational comparison is qualitative, and you know it.** The NLFFF-vs-PFSS comparison against AIA is explicitly admitted to be qualitative. You partially fix this with the Figure 8 footprint-distance-vs-peak-height diagnostic (loops taller than their footing separation before the flare, and the relaxation after — nice, and it does support the "expanded wider" picture), but that's a single scatter plot. If you can, add a small quantitative loop-matching statistic so the "NLFFF matches the EUV better" claim isn't left to eyes-only. At minimum, make the limitation more prominent so the claim isn't over-read.

5. **The PFSS anchoring undercuts the independence of the NLFFF result.** Both the side/top boundaries and the *initial condition* come from PFSS. So when you say "NLFFF expands higher than PFSS," recall that the NLFFF field lines are born from a PFSS skeleton. State this clearly — it's not a fatal flaw, but the NLFFF improvement is partly bounded by the quality of the PFSS it's built on.

6. **Missed opportunities / small issues.**
   - You don't compute twist number or signed helicity for the observed AR, even though the method (and your own Guo 2010 work) makes that natural for a "highly non-potential" region. That would be a much stronger demonstration than a qualitative EUV match.
   - Only one active region, one flare. The "multi-AR interaction" story (NOAA 11401/11401→11405/11407 connections, the long-loop arcade) is genuinely interesting and I'd expand it — it's arguably more novel than the code test — but it's given short shrift.
   - You mention the Wiegelnann & Inheser (2010) error-aware variant in the discussion but don't apply it to the observations; a short paragraph on why you didn't, or a comparison, would close the loop.
   - Notation drift: the mean vector error is called E_M in Eq. (11) but E_m in the text; B⁻ᴮ-notation for the energy integral is a little loose. Clean these up.
   - 34 pages is long for what is essentially validation + one application; the prose is descriptive in places and could be tightened.

### Bottom line

This is a solid, honest contribution that a solar-physicist can trust and re-use: it validates a spherical NLFFF code properly, adds a genuinely useful buffer/weighting treatment, makes the important Cartesian-vs-spherical point, and demonstrates the method on a physically interesting multi-AR event. It is **not**, however, a high-originality paper — the core method and code are established, the key comparison is qualitative, and the main application is a single case. If you sharpen the framing (lead with the two real contributions), harden the spherical/Cartesian claim with a second configuration, temper the free-energy/flare claim, and add even a modest quantitative loop diagnostic, this moves from "decent methods note" to "useful, clearly-motivated paper."

It's publishable as is, but do the reframing and the free-energy softening before it goes out.
