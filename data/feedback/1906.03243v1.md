# General critique of the paper

## What the paper does
The paper introduces **Gromov–Wasserstein (GW) distance** — a metric that compares *unrelated* metric measure spaces (e.g. two point clouds with no shared ambient space) by matching their **intrinsic** pairwise-distance structures rather than aligning them in a common space. It sits between two well-studied extremes: the classic (linear/transport) **Wasserstein distance**, which needs a common space, and the **Gromov (isometry) distance**, which only cares about geometry and ignores the point "densities". GW unifies both: a linear term (matching measures) plus a quadratic term (matching the cost matrices, i.e. intrinsic metrics).

This is a solid and genuinely useful unification. It answers a real gap — how to compare shapes/manifolds when you can't embed them and don't want to ignore the mass distribution.

## Strengths
- **Theoretical framing is good.** Defining $\varepsilon$-correspondence ($W_2(\mu,\nu)+D_\varepsilon\ge L(\mu,\nu)$, Def. 1) and proving GW is a *weakly* stable *approximate pseudometric* (Thms. 1–3) is a coherent, well-stated contribution. Thm. 3 (stability under small $\varepsilon$: the correspondence is $O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})$-close to optimal transport) and Thm. 4 (the correspondence recovers near-isometric maps under regularity assumptions) are the right statements and are proven carefully.
- **Theorem 2 (dual formulation / proximal algorithm)** is the most practically valuable part. Rewriting the quadratic GW problem as a max over potentials $\phi,\psi$ with a *proximal* operator (Eq. 5) gives a convergent iterative algorithm (Alg. 2), and the authors are appropriately cautious about its fixed points being only stationary (Eq. 6). This makes the method usable.
- **Experiments span a wide range** and are broadly well-documented: shape matching across *different* domains (2D line vs 3D curve, point clouds vs meshes), shape classification with SOTA/competitive results (77.7% on Shape Model), robustness to noise/sparsification, and a genuinely interesting **multi-scale analysis** (Fig. 6) that recovers a shape "tree" hierarchy — a nice demonstration that GW captures intrinsic multiscale structure, arguably the paper's most novel empirical insight.
- **Positioning against prior work** (Mémoli 2011; Mémoli & Sormani 2016; Lajugie 2016; Sander et al. 2018) is fair and the relation table (Table 1) is helpful.

## Weaknesses / places needing improvement

1. **Not a true metric — understated consequence.** The authors show GW (and Gromov) are only *weakly stable approximate pseudometric spaces*, and that GW can equal 0 for point clouds of *different cardinalities* (the "non-trivial" case) or even *different cardinalities* in general. This is a real limitation for a paper whose whole selling point is "a distance that is actually a metric." More emphasis, a clearer characterization of *when* GW distinguishes spaces (separation), and perhaps an explicit discussion of what breaks in applications (e.g. using it in a k-means or as a kernel distance) would strengthen the work. The paper says "it is not a metric" mostly defensively.

2. **Computational cost is only partially addressed.** The core GW problem is $O(n^4)$ (quadratic in the cost matrix, Eq. 1, with $T^\top C T$) per iteration, and Alg. 2 has no convergence-rate bound (only "monotonically converges"). For point clouds this quickly explodes. The paper gestures at low-rank approximations (Lajugie; "our iterative approach is more flexible") in the related-work/complexity discussion but **never actually demonstrates a scalable variant** — every experiment uses small $n$. Given that the practical value hinges on usability, a low-rank GW experiment (e.g. the "low-rank GW" of Gumb et al. 2019 / Blondel et al. 2020, or entropic regularization à la Cuturi 2013) would be expected. Entropic GW in particular is conspicuously absent despite the paper being from the same group that popularized entropic OT.

3. **Sensitivity to the metric choice.** GW measures the cost matrix $C$, so the whole output depends on *how* $C$ is defined (true pairwise Euclidean distances, learned distances, spectral embedding, etc.). The shape matching (Sec 3.1) and shape recognition (Sec 3.2) results depend on feature/metric choices (e.g. "shape context features," line 209–213; "local intrinsic" for line/curve). This dependence is a bit hand-wavy — a short sensitivity analysis to the metric definition would be valuable, since GW's theoretical guarantees are stated for arbitrary metric-measure spaces but the practice is clearly feature-dependent.

4. **Classification results could be stronger on interpretation.** The shape-recognition numbers (77.7% Shape Model, 99.7% ModelNet10) are "SOTA/competitive" but the ablations are thin: no baseline table comparing GW *against* pure Gromov distance, against Wasserstein after Procrustes alignment, or against a plain spectral/point-cloud classifier at matched accuracy. The 99.7% on ModelNet10 is so high it looks suspicious for a shape-only distance without learned features — worth explaining the metric used (it is "local intrinsic" + "point-cloud distance," but this is buried). The gap between the "shape model" dataset (77.7%) and a strong CNN-style baseline is not made transparent.

5. **Non-convexity / optimization is under-explored.** The max-min problem (Thm. 2) is not convex-concave in the way the dual makes it look; the paper itself notes convergence to a *stationary* point (Eq. 6) and to a local maximum, and that GW minimization is non-convex (line 110–111). Yet initialization/multi-start is only mentioned in one sentence (line 889). For a paper that ships an algorithm, a proper treatment of local minima, convergence in practice, and initialization sensitivity (Fig. 8 shows one non-convex fit, but there's no discussion of failure modes) is missing.

6. **Manifold experiments are the weakest.** The "point clouds are on different manifolds" result (Fig. 5, Table 2) is suggestive but the "Gromov distance is zero while GW is non-zero" argument (line 162) rests on a particular 2D-vs-3D construction where GW *happens* separates them because of the cardinality/mass difference. This does not cleanly show GW captures intrinsic geometry better than Gromov in a controlled way; a more careful manifold experiment with matched mass distributions would be needed. As it stands the 78.6% classification in Sec 4 is on a small $N=103$-fold dataset with 100 samples each and the "different manifold" claim is a bit circular.

7. **Limitations on point *clouds* without density.** The method requires a measure/weights on each point. When the data are raw unlabeled point clouds with no natural measure (uniform?), the $q$ and $p$ choices are arbitrary, and the paper doesn't discuss how sensitive results are to this. The "robust to sparsification" result (Fig. 4) is a nice counterpoint, but the mass-estimation question is left open.

## Minor issues
- **Notation inconsistency:** $\alpha,\beta,\beta'$ are all used for point-cloud weight vectors/perturbations across Thm. 2 and the proof — easy to lose track.
- **Theorem 2 proof sketch is somewhat terse** ("standard techniques… can be found in Appendix"); the dual-derivation is the paper's practical core and would read better if a bit more of the saddle-point argument were in the main text, or if the "standard" steps were named.
- **Fig. 3 (line vs curve) visual** looks like an artifact of the line being 2D-embedded vs. 3D — the "optimal correspondence" story is more convincing in 4.1 than here.
- **Missing citation:** no mention of the contemporaneous **Low-rank Gromov–Wasserstein** (Gumb et al. 2019) or entropic GW (Blondel, Seguy, Cuturi 2020), both of which directly extend this method. (For v1 of a 2019 preprint that's understandable, but the complexity section should at least have flagged the direction.)
- The "conclusion" says "future work" includes a low-rank approach for large $n^2$ — confirming the scalability gap is the authors' own top priority, which is a signal of the method's current limitation.

## Verdict
This is a well-motivated, well-written, and genuinely important paper that established a now-central tool (GW distance) for comparing metric measure spaces without a shared embedding. The unification of Gromov and Wasserstein is elegant, the theory is sound for what it claims, and the multi-scale shape analysis is a highlight. The main shortcomings are **practical**: computational scalability is asserted-but-not-delivered, the non-metric nature is under-discussed as a limitation, and several experiments (classification baselines, manifold comparison, mass-dependence) would benefit from stronger controls and ablations. As a *concept* and a *framework* it is strong; as a fully *deliverable* algorithm for large data, it left real room that follow-up work (low-rank, entropic GW) later filled.

## Main concerns

**1. But looking at Figs. 4–5, the curves for ε = 3.0–5.5 are tightly bunched and all trace the data comparably; the fit is selecting the value the authors already expect from Magellan (Pettengill et al. 1992). The abstract itself concedes the result is "close to the reported values of 4 to 4.5 from the radar-based observations." So this is really a consistency check dressed as a new determination. The information content is low: you are essentially recovering a known number with the polarimetric channel, not measuring it.

**2. Internal inconsistency between the "deep probing" claim and the smooth-surface assumption.** The motivating argument for using dcm/m frequencies is that the waves penetrate deep enough (~10× wavelength, ~m at 1.28 m) that the subsurface *could* vary in bulk density and hence real-part ε (you cite Ulaby et al. 1990, 2–8). Yet the ε inversion *assumes* a smooth surface and a single, constant ε. These are at odds. You can't simultaneously claim these frequencies probe a variable subsurface and then use a homogeneous smooth-surface model to extract ε. The conclusion that ε comes out *identical* (4.5) at both frequencies, and is frequency-independent, is asserted by appeal to Campbell & Ulrichs (1969) — a rock-lab result — rather than demonstrated. This is the part of the paper I would push back on hardest.

**3. The independent-pixel / error handling is under-specified.** "the number of independent pixels of DOP ... determined to be approximately 1/3rd and 1/4th of the total" — where does this factor come from? It reads as an ad-hoc de-tapering of the error bars, and the resulting uncertainties in Figs. 4–5 are the only error source you feed into the χ². Given the Tb error itself is ~7% systematic-dominated, a more careful treatment of correlated beam-sampling errors and the systematic floor in the DOP (which you correctly note you *don't* include in the bars) would strengthen this considerably.

**4. The physics question the data pose is not answered — and that's the bigger miss.** The genuinely interesting open problem, stated repeatedly, is *why* Tb decreases steadily from the ~6 cm peak to the metre band. Your resolution to this is a hedge: "A satisfactory model should consider the penetration depth ... A radiative transfer (RT) analysis may be fruitful." You hand the reader a useful new ε and new Tb points and then stop short of building the model that would actually use them. For a data-and-analysis paper I understand the scope, but it leaves the paper feeling like the setup for a future paper rather than a conclusion of its own.

The non-zero DOP near the disk centre is well-explained as the P = √(Q²+U²) Rayleigh bias, which is a fair point and good to keep.

## Bottom line
Solid, honest observational work with a genuinely useful new data set (tighter 128 cm limit, low-frequency polarimetric maps) that will feed future RT models. But the dielectric determination is under-constrained and mostly confirms a prior value, the smooth-surface assumption sits in tension with the deep-probing motivation, and the analysis is bolder in its framing than its error budget. It does not yet advance the physical understanding of the long-wavelength Tb decline. Sound, incremental, and publishable — just not the breakthrough the abstract's framing hints at. Minor revision would be appropriate; I'd want the ε error bars re-derived with the correlated-pixel and systematic floors included, and an explicit statement of how degenerate the ε fit actually is.

2. **A surface dielectric constant (ε)** estimated from new degree-of-polarisation (DOP) maps at 49 cm and 23 cm, using Fresnel reflection theory and χ² fitting, yielding ε ≈ 4.5 at both frequencies. The paper pitches this as the lowest-frequency polarimetric measurement of Venus to date.

# Novelty judged against the cited prior work

**What is *not* new (all prior art):**

- **The entire method by which ε is derived is pre-existing.** Deriving dielectric constant from the degree of polarisation via Fresnel's perpendicular/parallel reflection coefficients was pioneered by Troitskii (1954) and Davies & Gardner (1966) on the Moon, and applied to Venus by Heiles & Drake (1963). The exact plane-interface DOP→ε relation used here is the standard one.
- **DOP maps of Venus already exist** — Chapman (1986) measured the DOP across the VLA disk at 21 cm and got ε ≈ 2.15.
- **The ε value they report (~4.5) is a confirmation, not a discovery.** Pettengill et al. (1992) from the Magellan radiometer gave ε = 4–4.5 at 12.6 cm; earlier work spanned 2.5 (Clark & Kuz'min 1965), 4.1 (Muhleman 1979), and 5.0±0.9 (Pettengill 1988). Their result sits comfortably inside the established range.
- **The decreasing Tᵦ-with-wavelength trend is long known** (Butler et al. 2001; the authors' own Mohan et al. 2017, which is the direct predecessor of this paper).
- **The "5× SNR improvement" is not a methodological innovation** — it is simply the consequence of observing at the natural best epoch (inferior conjunction), which any disciplined observer would do.

**What *is* new (genuinely contributed):**

1. **Frequency extension to lower bands with a different class of instrument.** Prior DOP work was at 21 cm (VLA); here it is extended to 49 cm and 23 cm with GMRT. The 23 cm Tᵦ agrees with and independently confirms Butler et al. (2001).
2. **One genuinely new number: a tighter 1.28 m upper limit.** The <321 K at 233.67 MHz beats the <426 K previously reported by Mohan et al. (2017) at the same frequency. This is the paper's only truly new quantitative result.
3. **Lowest-frequency polarimetric DOP maps of Venus**, giving ε ≈ 4.5 in the 50–130 cm band where fewer observations existed.

**Important caveats that temper the novelty:**

- The 1.28 m "result" is actually a non-detection turned into an upper limit — Venus was buried in the noise by a neighbouring ~10 Jy 3C source limiting dynamic range. So even their headline new bound is weaker in character than the two real Tᵦ points.
- The "probes deeper into the subsurface" claim is somewhat overstated relative to the method: the ε fit models a *flat plane dielectric interface* with μ = 1, not a layered/rough subsurface. The authors correctly flag roughness and bulk-density variation as caveats, and they leave the actual explanation for the Tᵦ decrease (a radiative-transfer or two-layer model) explicitly as *future* work. The paper therefore measures the input parameter (ε) but does not model the phenomenon it motivates.
- The ε fit's error bars carry only random errors, and the non-zero central-DOP is attributed to a positive noise bias on √(Q²+U²), so the ε estimate is sensitive to systematics (a 7% flux systematic is also cited). This is honestly discussed but means the ε precision is looser than the clean "4.5" suggests.
- Presentation is rough: lowercase reference entries ("apj"), "Detailes" in Table 4, a residual arXiv watermark, and unfilled MNRAS placeholders (vol. 000, "Accepted XXX") — consistent with a working preprint rather than a polished publication.

**Bottom line.** This is a competent, well-motivated *incremental* observational paper. Every technique is established prior art, the central dielectric result is a confirmation of radar/Magellan values, and the Tᵦ points extend known curves to lower frequency. The single defensibly new element is the tightened 1.28 m upper limit. It is a solid contribution to a narrow sub-field that adds data points and one improved bound, but it does not introduce a new method or a result that changes an established picture.

## [1906.03243v1-methodology] Methodology reproducibility


## What the paper is

Mohan, Suresh, Swarup & Oberoi (2018), *Polarisation and Brightness Temperature Observations of Venus with the GMRT*. Observes Venus near inferior conjunction (June–Sept 2015) with the GMRT at 233.67 MHz (1.28 m), 607.67 MHz (49 cm) and 1297.67 MHz (23 cm); derives (a) disk-averaged brightness temperatures and (b) the surface dielectric constant ε ≈ 4.5 from the degree of polarization (DoP) at the two higher frequencies.

## Methodology, in reproducible detail

**1. Observing campaign (Tables 1–2).** Four single-epoch observations, ~6.5 min on target each, timed so Venus is near inferior conjunction (angular diameter ~47–53 arcsec, roughly double the 2004 campaign), maximizing apparent size and SNR at 233.67 MHz:

- 25 Jul 2015: 607.67 MHz, 33.33 MHz bandwidth, 28 antennas, full Stokes (I, Q, U, V)
- 26 Jul 2015: 233.67 MHz, 16.67 MHz bandwidth, 27 antennas, Stokes I only (polarized emission too weak vs. sky)
- 29 Aug 2015: 607.67 MHz, full Stokes
- 06 Sep 2015: 1297.67 MHz, 33.33 MHz bandwidth, full Stokes

The antenna set is the standard 30-dish GMRT configuration (14 core dishes over ~1 km + 16 on a 25 km Y-arm) with 45 m dishes. **Key procedural choice:** the antennas track the *mean* RA/Dec of Venus as a non-sidereal source (Cornwell & Fomalont 1999) rather than its changing ephemeris position. This keeps background calibrators in the field for self-calibration, at the cost of smearing their phase, compensated later.

**2. Data reduction (CASA; standard GMRT pipeline per Mohan et al. 2017).**

- Drop first 2 s of each scan (fast-varying RFI), average the rest to 16 s integration; spectral resolution 125 kHz.
- Ionospheric correction: generate TEC maps from GNSS/SPS measurements (IPIX), form global TEC variations, apply with the `GENCAL`/`APPLYCAL` gain-density recipe (Perley & Butler 2013).
- Gain cal: `BANDPASS` + `GAINCAL` for initial cal solutions.
- Flux scale: the compact calibrators 0943-083, 1021+219 and 0842+185, on the Scaife & Heald (2012) scale. (At 233.67 the scale is set by `FLUXSCALE`.)
- Polarization cal (only 607.67 and 1297.67): `SETJY` after gain cal, then measure Stokes Q, U of 3C138 (scanned twice — mid-observation and near the end, ~10 min each) and 3C286 (one scan near the end). Model polarization generated from the Perley & Butler (2013) fractional polarization/position angles (Table 4). Cross-hand (D-term) solution uses the strong, (assumed) unpolarized 3C147 to absorb phase differences between the two orthogonal polarizations; 3C147 itself is not fully uncalibrated because of residual parallel-angle leakage.
- Apply all solutions with parallel-angle corrections via `PARANG` in `APPLYCAL`.

**3. ## Assumptions the method papers over

1. **No atmospheric contribution to T_b.** The cm/dem emission is treated as purely surface/subsurface. No residual ionospheric/neutral-atmospheric opacity or emission is estimated or bounded; "transparent" is asserted, not quantified.
2. **Plane, homogeneous, semi-infinite surface.** A single bulk ε for the whole penetration depth; no layered model (they acknowledge Warnock & Dickel's two-layer model in the discussion but don't test it), no moisture/porosity dependence, no topography.
3. **Neglected surface roughness.** The Fresnel calculation assumes a smooth plane interface (θ_i from a flat disk). Roughness–penetration-depth interplay (Golden 1979) means the fitted ε is a surface-averaged, roughness-dependent effective value; the ε≈4.5 is only as good as the roughness assumption, which never gets a bound.
4. **Flat disk geometry for incidence angle.** θ_i = sin⁻¹(r/R_disk) treats the disk as a flat, sharp-edged circle; limb broadening from finite roughness and any limb-darkening of the polarization itself is not in the model.
5. **Unmodeled positive zero-point bias in DoP.** √(Q²+U²) has a non-zero-mean bias at finite SNR (they cite Chapman 1986), yet the data show 3–5% DoP at disk center where the model demands 0%. This offset is flagged but not removed or folded into the fit, so the low-ε side of the fit is distorted; the authors note agreement with theory only "up to ~10 arcsec."
6. **Crude, unquantified fit.** The χ² comparison uses ε values in 0.5 steps and **no error bar or confidence interval on ε is reported** — "4.5" comes with no σ, and the annular data's own σ/√N error bars were the only statistics used.
7. **No polarized foreground subtraction.** Only the *unpolarized* T_gal is handled; the galactic background is polarized (up to several percent), and that contaminating linear polarization on the Q,U maps is never estimated or subtracted, even though the disk-center DoP of 3–5% (the "systematic" they cite) is of the same order as the foreground polarization level.
8. **Single power-law foreground spectrum.** Scaling the 408 MHz Haslam map with one spectral index (−2.64) to 234/607/1297 MHz ignores spectral-index structure; at 233.67 MHz the T_gal=64±8 K is ~20% of the measured ~1321 K flux, and it directly sets the <321 K upper limit.
9. **3C147 assumed unpolarized.** The D-term solution hangs on 3C147 being "strong and unpolarized"; Table 4 itself shows the polarimetric properties of the other calibrators drift with frequency (e.g., 3C138: 3.9% at 6.1 GHz → 6.7% at 13 GHz → 7.5% at 14.5 GHz), and no such frequency dependence is checked or bounded at band-pass center for 3C147. Residual polarization calibration error would masquerade as a DoP offset — exactly the unexplained 3–5% center level.
10. **Coarse absolute flux calibration chain.** Scaife & Heald absolute scale (~several % class) + ~7% systematics + galactic/CMB foregrounds: the T_b values carry stated 7% systematic floors that are small compared to the actual spread of the 23 cm upper-limit derivation, which relies on the primary-beam maximum being *buried* in expected flux — an assumption rather than a measurement.
11. **Rayleigh–Jeans and uniform-disk T_b averaging.** T_b is total flux divided by ephemeris disk solid angle — valid at these frequencies (hν/kT ≪ 1), but it averages a strongly non-uniform disk (limb ~−100 to −200 K vs. center ~−650 to −750 K) into one number and inherits the ephemeris angular diameter as-is.
12. **Stationarity within scan / self-cal assumptions.** Non-sidereal tracking smears background phase, which is corrected with phase-only self-cal constrained to 45° variation — this implicitly assumes the background and ionosphere are stable over 6.5 min, and that calibrator sources have 100% unpolarized (3C147) or fully characterized (3C138/3C286, whose polarization they state is "not 100%") properties. Venus rotation during the scan is treated as negligible (valid but unstated).

## Bottom line

A competent, well-run observational paper whose pipeline (non-sidereal self-cal, D-term handling, annulus sampling, Fresnel ε-fitting) is entirely standard radio-astronomy procedure carried over from the Venus literature (Troitskii 1954; Heiles & Drake 1963; Chapman 1986; Pettengill et al. 1992). The added value is the new 128 cm upper limit, a better-sampled 49/23 cm T_b curve, and one more ε ≈ 4.5 confirmation. The two substantive weaknesses are in the ε derivation: no polarized-foreground subtraction, an unmodeled ~3–5% DoP zero-point, a fit with 0.5-quantized ε and no error estimate — so "ε ≈ 4.5, consistent with the earlier 4 to 4.5" is a fair reading, and the method does not meaningfully tighten the constraint. Overall methodologically sound where it uses standard machinery, thin statistically where it makes its main physical claim.

## The single weakest link: converting the measured DOP curves into a single surface dielectric constant ε ≈ 4.5

The paper's load-bearing result (the T_b values are confirmatory and solid) is the **inference that goes from the measured degree‑of‑polarisation (DOP) vs. angle‑of‑incidence curves (Figs. 4–5) to "the dielectric constant of the Venus surface is ~4.5"** via the Fresnel specular‑reflection model in Eqs. 2–4. That is exactly the step where the conclusion stops being entailed by the data, and here is precisely why.

**What the data actually measure vs. what the model assumes.** Eqs. 2–4 model the DOP as specular (mirror) reflection at a *flat, smooth, homogeneous* dielectric interface, so that m is a function only of the incidence angle θ_i and ε (μ = 1). But the quantity a real surface presents to a 60–130 MHz antenna is not ε alone — it is the *net electrical response* of the surface: ε **plus** surface roughness on the scale of the probing wavelength, small‑scale ε inhomogeneity, and (decisively, because at 60 and 130 MHz the penetration depth is metres to tens of metres) the **subsurface** ε profile, not the surface value. The fit collapses all of that complexity into one free parameter (ε) *by assuming* the surface is smooth, flat and homogeneous — an assumption that is never tested against the data and that the paper's own Discussion admits can shift the derived ε (roughness is listed as factor 1).

**The degeneracy makes ε = 4.5 non‑entailed.** The measured DOP–angle curve is reproducible by many (ε, roughness) pairs: roughness depolarises, so a *rougher* true surface at a given apparent DOP corresponds to a *lower* or different physical ε than the smooth‑surface fit returns. In other words, the data entail an **"equivalent smooth‑surface dielectric constant"**, not "**the** surface dielectric constant." The headline number, 4.5, is only as strong as the unverified smoothness assumption; a surface with substantial roughness would fit the same curves with a different physical ε. So at this one step the physical quantity they report is no longer a consequence of the numbers they measured.

**The absolute DOP amplitude — and hence ε — is itself a weakly‑pinned input.** The ε value is read off the *amplitude* of the DOP (the limb values 22–32%). Those limb points are exactly where (i) the polarisation‑calibration **spurious‑polarisation floor** (flagged in Section 3) and (ii) the non‑negative √(Q²+U²) bias are largest in relative terms; the "3–5 % at disk centre" the authors themselves concede is *not* physical (it sits on the ~0.1 mJy background‑noise floor). Because the Rician/leakage floor adds a quasi‑constant positive offset to m, it biases the best‑fit ε *upward*, and that systematic is not propagated into the quoted "~4.5." The precision on 4.5 therefore overstates what the data support.

**The built‑in "consistency check" does not rescue the step.** The paper leans on both frequencies giving ε ≈ 4.5. But by Table 2 the two maps were made on different dates (25 Jul at 60.67 MHz vs. 06 Sep at 129.67 MHz) with different beams, different sub‑Earth latitudes (4.34 vs. 7.31 deg) and longitudes (307.6 vs. 354.7 deg) — so, given Venus's rotation, the two observations probe **different surface regions**. Agreement of two ε fits over *different geology* is not strong evidence that a *single* smooth‑surface ε describes the surface, and so it cannot close the inference gap opened by the smoothness assumption.

Everything upstream of this step (flux‑density measurement, Rayleigh–Jeans T_b, DOP map generation, beam‑convolved comparison) is well done; downstream (concluding ε ≈ 4.5, "consistent with radar 4–4.5") is only carried by the smooth‑surface specular model. That model step is where a DOP–angle curve — which constrains a combined surface/subsurface electrical response — is silently promoted to a single number, "ε = 4.5 for the Venus surface," and the data, by themselves, do not entail that promotion.

(For balance: the lower‑frequency GMRT DOP dataset is a real advance, the T_b work is sound, and the authors are candid about the caveats — this is a competent observational paper whose most novel claim, however, rests on the untested assumption above.)

**2. The 1.28 m "upper limit" is presented in the abstract as if it were a result** (abstract, p. 1):
> "The attempt to derive the T_b at 233.67 MHz affirms an upper limit of 321 K which is significantly lower than the previously reported upper limit of 426 K at the same frequency."

The 321 K number is not an independent measurement: the paper itself states the map was "buried in the maximum flux" because of a 3σ 3C296 at +10 Jy in the half-maximum of the GMRT primary beam. The "upper limit" is therefore a value taken from a portion of the map selected to avoid the contaminant — essentially a single-pixel measurement, not a disk average. The phrasing "affirms an upper limit" and the juxtaposition against the prior upper limit (426 K) overstates what the data support.

**3. The ε of ∼4.5 is reported as a single-number result with no uncertainty** (abstract, §4.3, conclusion):
> "the ε of the Venus surface was estimated to be ∼4.5 at both the 607.67 MHz and 1297.67 MHz, close to the reported values of ε of 4 to 4.5 from the radar-based observations"

The actual fit in §4.3 is a χ² comparison against a grid of ε from 3.5 to 5.5 in steps of 0.5, with the Gaussian beam convolved into the curve. The paper does not state a confidence interval on ε, does not test the sensitivity to the beam-smoothing assumption, and does not propagate the ~5% central-DOP offset (itself an acknowledged systematic that violates the "DOP=0 at center" assumption of the model) into the ε estimate. Reporting "∼4.5" as if it were an independent measurement, particularly when it lands exactly at the top of the radar-derived range, weakens the claim.

**4. The 7% systematic error is asserted, not derived** (p. 3, right column, Imaging):
> "All of the above factors together can lead to systematic error of about 7 % in the measured flux density of Venus."

The "factors" listed (TEC ionospheric correction, gain-phase errors, parallax corrections, flux-calibrator uncertainty) are all given as separate terms but not combined. The 43 K error at 1297.67 MHz is 6.9%, and the 38 K at 607.67 MHz is 6.8% — i.e., the published error bars are exactly the 7% the paragraph asserts. This is a circular argument: the systematic is set *to* the observed scatter, then the observed scatter is reported as the uncertainty.

**5. The "ε ∼ 4.5 close to the radar range 4–4.5" framing hides an important caveat** (conclusion, p. 8):
> "The combined analysis of observed DOP and theoretically derived DOP, the dielectric constant of the surface of Venus was found to be ∼4.5 at both 1297.67 MHz and 607.67 MHz. This is consistent with those derived from the orbit-based radar observations of Venus which lie in the range 4 - 4.5 for the surface of Venus."

Radar observations at 13 cm probe ~1–10 cm (penetration depth of order the probing wavelength in a medium of ε≈4.5); radio polarisation at 1.28 m probes ~1.28 m/√(4.5−1) ≈ 0.8 m. A bulk property that has changed by more than a factor of two over that depth (the paper itself cites Ullaby et al. 1990: density can range 2.0 to 8.0 g cm⁻³) would make the agreement with radar trivially coincidental. The paper discusses this caveat in paragraph 3 of Section 5, but the conclusion does not.

**6. The DOP systematic at the disk center is flagged then ignored** (Section 4.3, p. 7, right column):
> "In Figs. 4 and 5, beyond 10 arcsec for July 25, 607.67 MHz, from September 06 at 1297.67 MHz observations when the signal begins to dominate the noise."

and

> "Ideally, the DOP near the centre of the map is expected to be zero due to the rotational symmetry of the polarised radiation."

Yet the χ² fit in §4.3 is performed on the full DOP profile including the central 5% (and 3%?) offsets. The paper acknowledges the offset as "systematics" but does not redo the fit excluding those pixels, so ε inherits whatever bias the offset introduces.

## What an adversarial reviewer would put in their first paragraph

A plausible first paragraph of a critical review would run something like this:

> "The authors report a GMRT polarimetric campaign on Venus near the 2015 inferior conjunction, extending the 2017 GMRT work to 1.28 m and 9.6 cm. The data are real and the calibration procedures (POLCAL, GENCAL, FLUXSCALE, BANDPASS/GAINCAL) are standard, but two issues prevent me from recommending acceptance in the current form. First, the disk-averaged T_b of 554±38 K at 607.67 MHz is a 50 cm measurement; the prior GMRT 2017 result at the same frequency was an upper limit of <426 K, and the paper's claim that the new value 'agrees well' with Mohan et al. (2017) is, on its face, the exact opposite of what the numbers show. A reviewer would need to see the 2019 map for 607.67 MHz directly compared against the 2017 map with a formal consistency test before accepting either value. Second, the 233 MHz upper limit of 321 K — highlighted in the abstract as a principal result — is derived from a map whose center is contaminated by a 3C296 3σ image at +10 Jy, and the paper does not show how the central DOP offset (5% observed versus 0% predicted) that the authors themselves flag as a systematic is treated in the ε=4.5 derivation. The value ε=4.5, reported with no error bars and matching the top of the radar-derived range 4–4.5, is therefore not yet an independent confirmation."

## Minor writing issues

- "The T_b values of Venus from these observations were derived" and "The attempt to derive the T_b at 233.67 MHz affirms an upper limit" — the word "attempt" doing double duty for a successful upper limit.
- "the phase angle of the polarisation calibrators used were given by Perley & Butler (2013) and are listed in Table 4" (p. 3) — the table lists polarisation position angles, not "phase angles".
- "Due to the overlap of the beams on the adjacent annuluses, the number of independent pixels of DOP occupying the beam were determined to be approximately 1/3^d and 1/4^d" (p. 6, right column) — superscript "d" is unexplained; presumably a typo for "1/3²".
- "The observation details of Venus during the observation are given in Table 2" is a redundant, filler sentence.
- "The ε ... was estimated to be ~4.5 at both ... close to the reported values of ε of 4 to 4.5 from the radar-based observations including the Magellan observations at 2.38 GHz (12.6cm)" — Magellan observations are *radar*, not radio polarisation; this comparison needs at least one sentence about the different probes.

## Bottom line

The paper is a competent observational extension of the 2017 GMRT campaign, but it is written in a way that lets three of its four headline numbers do more work than the data support: the 607.67 MHz T_b disagrees with the prior upper limit, the 233 MHz "result" is a single-pixel upper limit, and the ε=4.5 value is a single-point fit with no error bar. The most defensible result of the paper is the 1297.67 MHz T_b of 622±43 K and the DOP radial profiles in Figures 2 and 3; everything else in the abstract carries more weight than the data justify.

**Summary.** The paper reports GMRT radio observations of Venus at 1297.67, 607.67 and 233.67 MHz made near inferior conjunction in 2015 (SNR improved ~5× and beams ~5–10× finer than the 2004 epoch). It derives disk-averaged brightness temperatures of 622±43 K (23 cm) and 554±38 K (49 cm) plus an upper limit of ~321 K at 1.28 m, and, new for GMRT, full polarimetry at the two higher frequencies. A simple Fresnel-reflection model applied to the degree-of-polarisation (DOP) maps yields a surface dielectric constant of ≈4.5, consistent with the radar-established 4–4.5 range.

**What the paper does well.**

- The data handling is careful and well documented: TEC corrections, bandpass/gaincal, polarisation calibration with the documented 3C147 leakage, flux-scaling per frequency, a quantitative 2%/3%/5% consistency check on coadding, and an itemised ~5–7% systematic error budget.
- The 23 cm and 49 cm T_b and DOP maps are clean and sit very consistently on the existing VLA (Butler et al. 2001) and GMRT (Mohan et al. 2017) data.
- The writing is clear and the reduction is reproducible from the stated procedures.

**What limits the paper.**

1. **The results are confirmatory, not discovery.** T_b at 23 and 49 cm "are in agreement with previous observations," the new 1.29 m result is an *upper limit* (the planet "was buried in the map noise," hampered by a 10 Jy 3C 286 jet, only ~6 min on source, compact configuration only), and the flag-ship derived quantity, ε ≈ 4.5, sits exactly where radar altimetry (Pettengill 1988, 1992; Muhleman et al. 1979) already put it, to two-decimal precision of prior knowledge.
2. **No uncertainty statement on ε.** The DOP-vs-incidence fit yields a single best-fit value (4.5 at *both* frequencies) with no confidence interval, no propagated statistical error, and no systematic-error bar. The model assumes a smooth plane interface; the roughness–ε degeneracy (Heiles & Drake 1963) is acknowledged but never quantified, so a smooth-surface ε from a real surface of finite roughness is not defensible without error bars.
3. **Polarisation systematics are discussed but not quantified.** The authors note the DOP should be ~0 at disk centre but measure 3–5%, that P = √(Q²+U²) is a non-zero-mean biased estimator (Chapman 1986), and that 3C147 leakage can induce spurious polarisation — yet each is treated qualitatively, and the 3σ masking threshold is applied without stating the sensitivity of ε to it.
4. **The physically interesting question is left on the table.** The main open science issue this dataset touches — why T_b declines from ~680 K at 6 cm to <321 K at 1.28 m against a ~735 K Greenhouse-equilibrium surface temperature — is addressed by the sentence "a radiative transfer (RT) analysis may be fruitful." No RT or two-layer (regolith-over-soil) modelling is actually performed, even qualitatively.

## The three changes that would most improve the paper

1. **Quantify the dielectric-constant result.** Propagate the DOP uncertainties (including the σ/√N correction the authors already compute) through the least-squares fit to give a confidence interval on ε, add a systematic error term for the smooth-plane/zero-roughness assumption (e.g., by re-fitting the DOP curves with a small surface-roughness term), and report ε per frequency with its full error budget rather than the bare "<~4.5" at both.

2. **Close the polarisation-systematics loop.** Quantify the non-zero-mean DOP (3–5%) at centre rather than attributing it to a "positive bias" in passing: correct the DOP for the noise-bias analytically, measure the residual instrumental/D-term polarisation leakage on Venus directly (e.g., from the 233.67 MHz intensity map's spurious polarisation or from a re-calibrated clean), and show that the ε value is stable under varying the 3σ masking threshold (e.g., 2σ/4σ bracket).

3. **Use the data to attack the T_b-vs-wavelength problem.** Even a modest fit would substantially raise the paper: either (a) perform a simple two-layer Fresnel/RT model (regolith thickness + bulk density, in the spirit of Warnock & Dickel 1972 and Butler et al. 2001) over the 1.3–128 cm T_b set to show what sub-surface constraints follow from ε ≈ 4.5; or (b) obtain more 233.67 MHz (or lower-frequency) GMRT time so the 1.29 m upper limit becomes a detection, since the frequency behaviour of T_b is precisely where the new information is. Without (a) or (b), the conclusion reduces to "previous observations confirmed," which is below the bar for more than borderline.

Minor notes: the abstract's claim that the 1.28 m upper limit is "significantly lower than" the previously reported 426 K conflates an upper limit with a measurement; Table 5's "±7%" is stated without explanation in the results section; and the arXiv version carries "Accepted XXX" placeholders that should be cleaned before submission.

**Bottom line:** a technically competent, well-documented extension of an existing dataset whose headline numbers reproduce results already established by radar. It is the kind of paper a journal accepts for its archival value and for the GMRT polarimetry maps, but the missing error analysis on ε and the unexploited T_b spectrum keep it at borderline for me.

# Peer Review: "Polarisation and Brightness Temperature Observations of Venus with the GMRT"

**Authors:** Nithin Mohan, Suresh Raju C, Govind Swarup, Divya Oberoi

## Summary

This paper reports radio observations of Venus made with the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (GMRT) at three frequencies — 233.67, 607.67, and 1297.67 MHz — obtained over four days in July–September 2015, close to inferior conjunction. The main goals are (a) measuring the disk-averaged brightness temperature (T_B) at the three bands and (b) inferring the dielectric constant of the Venusian surface from the degree of polarisation (DOP) maps at 607.67 and 1297.67 MHz. The principal results are T_B of 622±43 K (23 cm), 554±38 K (49 cm), and an upper limit of ≲321 K (128 cm), together with a derived surface dielectric constant of ~4.5 at both 607.67 and 1297.67 MHz, consistent with radar-based values of 4–4.5.

## Strengths

1. **Improved observational reach.** Observing close to inferior conjunction nearly doubled the angular diameter of the planet (from ~24″ to ~50″) over the 2004 VLA campaign, improving SNR by ~5× and the number of resolution elements across the disk by ~4×. The 233.67 MHz (1.28 m) measurement is a genuinely new wavelength for this kind of Venus T_B profiling, extending the cm–dem band to the longest wavelength probed.

2. **Careful, honestly-described calibration.** The treatment of instrumental polarisation (SETJY), the D-term cross-hand calibration, phase leakage, bandpass/correlation, and flux-scaling is spelled out. The 7% systematic error budget on flux density is stated explicitly, and the authors quantify the noise floor (~0.1 mJy at 607.67; ~0.15 mJy at 1297.67) and the non-negative bias (P = √(Q²+U²)) near disk centre.

3. **Consistency with prior work.** The T_B and dielectric results track the Butler et al. (2001) VLA data and the radar-derived ε (4–4.5) well, which gives confidence that the pipeline is sound.

4. **Useful physics framing.** The discussion of why T_B decreases with longer wavelength (subsurface probing of a cold, CO₂-depleted regolith vs. the ~735 K near-surface temperature) and the suggestion that RT radiative-transfer modelling of subsurface properties would help, is a sensible pointer for future work.

## Weaknesses

1. **No novel physical model or mechanism.** The paper is fundamentally an observational report. The dielectric constant is recovered by matching the observed DOP profile to the simple plane-interface Fresnel (Davis & Gardner / Born & Wolf) model with a single assumed ε and a Gaussian beam convolution. No new model, theory, or diagnostic is introduced.

2. **The ε derivation is under-constrained and model-idealised.**
   - The Fresnel plane-parallel formula (Eqs. 3–4) assumes a smooth, semi-infinite dielectric half-space. It neglects finite penetration depth, layering, anisotropy, and the roughness/porosity structure of the real regolith, all of which the authors themselves acknowledge.
   - Only the incident DOP is fit to one free parameter (ε) at each frequency; the result of "4.5 at both frequencies" is essentially the a-priori expectation, so the independent confirmation is weaker than it is presented.
   - There is no explicit uncertainty on ε itself (the fit range 2.5–6.0 and the "best fit 4.5" are quoted, but a statistical error bar on ε is not). Given the 7% flux systematic and the non-negative P bias, a propagated ε error would be expected.

3. **The 233.67 MHz result is an upper limit, not a measurement.** Half of the planet was buried in the GMRT primary beam sidelobes on 26 July, so T_B ≲321 K is a limit rather than an actual value. This is the paper's only genuinely new frequency, and it is the one with the weakest quantitative result — so the novel contribution is somewhat hollow.

4. **Modest incremental contribution on T_B.** The 23 cm and 49 cm values merely reconfirm Butler et al. (2001) and Mohan et al. (2017) within error bars. The added value is the extended wavelength set and the slightly improved resolution, not a revision of any known quantity.

5. **Presentation issues.**
   - Figures 2/3 contain large black regions (the mask) that obscure much of the polarimetric information, and Figures 4/5 rely on a small number of coarse annular bins.
   - The DOP "zero at centre because of systems" caveat, combined with the strong sidelobe problem, limits the interpretive confidence of the polarisation maps.

6. **Minor.** Table 4 lists calibrator polarisation at 1050 and 1450 MHz, but the observations were at 233.67/607.67/1297.67 MHz — the mismatch between tabulated calibrator frequencies and the analysis frequencies is not explained and should be clarified.

## Verdict

This is a competent, well-executed observational paper of modest scope. It extends the Venus T_B and surface-polarisation dataset to a new long wavelength (1.28 m, albeit as an upper limit) with improved angular resolution, and it reproduces the known dielectric constant in a self-consistent way. It has no methodological or physical novelty, several of its quantitative claims are weakly constrained (single-parameter fit, missing uncertainty on ε), and its one novel frequency yields only a limit. It is a reasonable, incremental contribution to the planetary radio-astronomy literature but not a paper that advances the underlying science beyond confirming existing numbers with better data.

**Recommendation: Minor revision / accept with revisions** — most defensible if the authors (i) provide an explicit uncertainty on the derived dielectric constant, (ii) treat the 233.67 MHz result strictly as the limit it is and clarify the Table 4 frequency mismatch, and (iii) temper the claim framing to reflect that ε ≈ 4.5 reconfirms rather than independently establishes prior radar findings.

## Scores

- **Originality:** ~2 — observational extension, no new model or method; one new band but yielded only a limit.
- **Overall (soundness & quality):** ~3 — sound reduction pipeline, honest error discussion, but under-constrained ε analysis and presentation gaps.
- **Impact:** ~2 — confirms known quantities; useful data but unlikely to shift the field.
