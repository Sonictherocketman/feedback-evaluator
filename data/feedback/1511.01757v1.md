## Summary of the argument

The paper's chain of reasoning is:

1. **Dirac–Heisenberg vs. Weyl quantization are only equivalent for regular representations** of the canonical Weyl algebra, i.e. where Stone–von Neumann uniqueness applies. For systems whose canonical variables do not all correspond to observables (gauge systems, systems on non‑simply‑connected configuration manifolds), a *gauge‑invariant ground state* forces a **non‑regular** Weyl representation: the gauge Weyl operators are regular, but the gauge‑dependent Weyl operators have expectations vanishing at all non‑zero parameter values, so their generators (e.g. position) do not exist as operators (Prop. 2.2, the Baume et al. type representation).

2. **Algebraic structure**: a larger "field" Weyl algebra $F_W$ embeds the observable subalgebra $A$, whose **non‑trivial center $Z$** generates the gauge transformations. $Z$ is (claimed to be) of topological origin — from the fundamental group / first homology of the configuration manifold — and labels superselection sectors (the $\theta$ sectors); the non‑observable Weyl operators act as intertwiners between them.

3. **Main result (Theorem 5.2)**: an automorphism $\beta$ of $A$ that does not leave $Z$ pointwise invariant is **spontaneously broken in every irreducible ($\theta$) representation**. Crucially, the Goldstone theorem (their Thm 5.1) is *evaded*: because $\beta$'s would‑be generator cannot live in the strong closure $\pi(A)^{\prime\prime}$ (it is an intertwiner that does not act within a single sector), the symmetry‑breaking **Ward identity cannot be written**, and an **energy gap is compatible with the broken symmetry**. Examples: particle on a circle, Bloch electron, two‑body problem.

---

## What the paper does well

- **The core physical insight is correct and important.** That a gauge transformation is a redundancy — not a real symmetry implemented by a conserved generator — is why gauge "breaking" produces no physical Goldstone boson. Presenting this **purely algebraically** (through the center of the observable algebra and the failure of the Ward identity), and giving a **non‑Higgs version** of the phenomenon (no potential, no dynamical long‑range/Coulomb mechanism — purely kinematic/topological + gap), is a genuinely useful contribution and a clean framing the Higgs textbook story does not give.
- The **non‑regular Weyl / Zak‑algebra machinery** (Sec. 2, the Gelfand‑spectrum classification $L^2((T^2)^d,d\mu)$) is handled competently and the two‑body example is a clean illustration that a "CM‑momentum‑zero ground state" is consistent with Weyl but inconsistent with a naive Heisenberg (non‑normalizable vector) treatment.
- Unifying circle / Bloch / two‑body under one center‑of‑topological‑origin picture is a nice synthesis.

## Substantive points of contention

**1. "Spontaneous symmetry breaking" for finite systems is an overreach.** The whole edifice is a *finite* quantum system with a discrete‑spectrum Hamiltonian. In such a system what actually happens is: $V(2\pi)=e^{i\theta}\mathbf 1$ is a c‑number in a $\theta$ sector, and the continuous rotation/translation is an *intertwiner* that maps one superselection sector to another — it is not a symmetry implemented within the GNS of a chosen ground state. That is more precisely **superselection** (sector structure from the center) than SSB in the usual (thermodynamic‑limit, degenerate‑vacuum) sense. Calling it "spontaneous symmetry breaking by topology" imports the SSB banner onto what is really topological‑sector superselection. The non‑triviality of $\pi_1$ plays the role of "infinity at spatial infinity," and the paper leans on that, but it never states the thermodynamic‑limit analogy explicitly, so the strongest form of the terminology is hard to justify. The defensible claim is the weaker one: *the gauge automorphism is not inner within a sector, so its Ward identity is unavailable.* The dramatic "breaking + gap" framing does more work than the finite models support.

**2. A concrete notational/topological error (Sec. 3).** The paper says the topological invariants come from "the fundamental group $\pi_1(\tilde M)$, of the universal covering space $\tilde M$ … i.e. the first homology group $H_1(\tilde M)$ of $\tilde M$." But the universal cover is simply connected, so $\pi_1(\tilde M)=0$ and $H_1(\tilde M)=0$ — taken literally this says the center is generated by *trivial* invariants, contradicting the central thesis. It must be a slip for the configuration manifold $M$ itself ($\pi_1(M)$, $H_1(M)$; e.g. $\pi_1(S^1)\cong\mathbb Z$). Since topological origin of $Z$ **is** the paper's thesis, this should be corrected and the precise manifold (base vs. cover) stated unambiguously.

**3. The energy‑gap attribution is precise only for the free/circle case, and overstated for the Bloch electron.** For the particle on a circle (free) the gap of $H=p^2/2m$ is set purely by the circle — a clean "gap from the spectrum of the fundamental group." For the **Bloch electron** the paper says "the spectrum of $p^2$ has a gap given by the spectrum of the fundamental group of $\mathbb R/[0,a]$." But $H=p^2/2m+W(x)$ with a *periodic potential*, and the **physical energy gap** of the Hamiltonian depends on $W$ (the band gap), not on $\pi_1$ alone. Only $p$'s own spectrum in a $\theta$ sector, $\{2\pi n/a+\theta/a\}$, is purely topological. Theorem 5.2 is a statement about the *energy* spectrum, so the Bloch example conflates "topological spectrum of $p$" with "energy gap of $H$." It should read: the *existence* of a gap (as opposed to a Goldstone continuum touching the ground state) is guaranteed by the topological structure; its *size* is $W$‑dependent. As written this is a real overstatement.

**4. The leap to genuine GQFT is asserted, not shown.** The paper repeatedly claims the finite models "mimic the basic structures of GQFT" (QCD, instantons, $\theta$‑vacua) and "shed light" on infinite‑dimensional gauge theory. But no *local/non‑abelian* gauge example is worked: everything stays inside the abelian Weyl/Heisenberg toy algebras. The relevant topology in real QFT is that of field configurations (instantons, Gribov–Zwanziger, $\theta$ sectors on $\mathcal A/\mathcal G$), not a simple $\pi_1$ of a coordinate manifold. A single concrete non‑abelian example, or at least a precise statement of *what exactly* the circle/Bloch model captures of, say, $\theta$‑vacuum/Griffiths structure, is needed; as it stands the relevance to QCD is rhetorical.

**5. Theorem 5.2's proof is compressed to the point of being thin at its crux.** The logic is sound as a proof by contradiction (assume $\beta_\lambda$ generated by $Q$; with $[Q,V]\in Z$ and $e^{i\phi}\ne1$ for $\beta\not\le$ pointwise‑invariant, gauge invariance of $\omega_\theta$ forces $\omega_\theta(QA)=\omega_\theta(QA)+c\,\omega_\theta(A)$ with $c\ne0$ — contradiction, so no such $Q$, so the Goldstone Ward identity fails). But the paper expresses the resolution as "the expectations $\omega_\theta(QA)$ **cannot be defined**," which is asserted, not derived. The real reason — that $Q\in F_W$ is an *intertwiner* and does not map $H_\theta$ into itself, so $\omega_\theta(QA)$ is outside the sector's domain — is exactly the content that should be spelled out. Also note Thm 5.1 requires a generator sequence in $\pi(A)^{\prime\prime}$ (observable algebra) while 5.2 rules out a generator in the larger $\pi(F_W)^{\prime\prime}$; the proof is therefore *stronger* than the hypothesis it needs to refute, which is fine but should be pointed to so the reader sees the $A\subset F_W$ inclusion closes the gap.

**6. The Zak‑algebra classification is taken on faith.** The central mathematical claim (Sec. 2) that all spectrally‑multiplicity‑free, strongly‑measurable Weyl representations are $L^2((T^2)^d,d\mu)$ for a translation‑invariant Borel $d\mu$ is deferred to [13] (Cavallaro–Morchio–Strocchi); "spectrally multiplicity free" and "strongly measurable" are never unpacked, and the paper does not check its own models satisfy the hypotheses. A reader who has not read [13] cannot verify the premise the whole classification rests on.

**7. Minor.** Two‑body: "the vector $\Psi_0$ satisfies $P^2\Psi_0=0$" — in the $\theta\!=\!0$ sector $P$ is a c‑number ($P=0$), so $P\Psi_0=0$ is the natural statement; the squaring is loose. "The spectrum of $V(\beta)$ is pure point" in Prop 2.2 is loose language for a *non‑separable* pure point (a continuous parameter $\alpha\in\mathbb R$ of eigenvectors); calling it "pure point" without that caveat is misleading to a QFT reader.

Many ingredients are pre‑existing: Weyl non‑regular representations and their gauge interpretation (Acerbi–Morchio–Strocchi [3–5], Baume et al. [14]), the Zak‑algebra classification [13], non‑regular/polymer quantization, and "no Goldstone in gauge breaking" is textbook (Higgs/'t Hooft/Fradkin–Schwimmer). ## Verdict

A clear and largely mathematically sound treatment of an important topic, whose central message (gauge ≡ redundancy ⇒ no Goldstone, organized by the topological center of the observable algebra) is correct and well worth the unified presentation. A more careful, honest framing of *what is being broken and where the gap actually comes from in each example* would substantially strengthen it.

## Review: "Gauge Invariance and Symmetry Breaking by Topology and Energy Gap" (Heissenberg & Strocchi, arXiv:1511.01757)

### Summary

The paper addresses a well-defined structural question in the quantum description of systems on manifolds with non-trivial fundamental group: when a canonical Weyl (field) algebra $\mathcal{F}_W$ contains only a subalgebra $\mathcal{A}$ of observables that has a **non-trivial center** $\mathcal{Z}$, the complement plays the role of *intertwiners* between inequivalent representations of $\mathcal{A}$ and generates a **gauge group** $\mathcal{G}$. The center typically arises from the topology of the configuration manifold (via $\pi_1(\mathcal{M})$ / its first homology group), producing superselction ($\theta$) sectors. The main result is **Theorem 5.2**: an automorphism $\beta$ of $\mathcal{A}$ that does not leave $\mathcal{Z}$ pointwise invariant — in particular, one that acts non-trivially on the topological invariants — is spontaneously broken in *each* irreducible representation, and, because the symmetry-breaking Ward identities of the Goldstone theorem cannot be written, an energy gap is permitted. Examples: particle on a circle, Bloch electron, two-body (center-of-mass) problem.

### Strengths

1. **Rigor where it is usually missing.** The regularity condition on the Weyl exponentials, the non-separable GNS space $\mathcal{H}_\omega = \bigoplus_\theta \mathcal{H}_\theta$, non-well-continuity of $\beta^\lambda$ (vanishing one-parameter expectations), and the Zak-algebra classification ($L^2(\Sigma, d\mu)$ over Borel measures on the torus $(\mathbb{T}^2)^d$) are handled with genuine precision (Section 2, Prop 2.1–2.2, 3.2). This is the paper's real value.

2. **A clean general mechanism.** Theorem 5.2 correctly isolates the *precise* failure of the Goldstone-precondition: the generator of $\beta^\lambda$ cannot be formed in $\pi(\overline{\mathcal{A}})''$ because $\omega_θ(QA)$ is undefined ($[Q,V]\in\mathcal{Z}$ and gauge invariance block it). Stating the mechanism "at the level of the group, not its vectors" is a legitimate and useful abstraction.

3. **Concrete tie-down.** The Bloch-electron treatment (Prop 3.2, 3.1–3.6) is standard but presented algebraically, which helps the message.

### Weaknesses / concerns

1. **The novelty is overstated relative to the content.** The components — Zak algebras, non-regular canonical representations, superselection sectors from the center, topological origin of the center — are all known (the authors cite Zak [12], Zak classification [13], and their own earlier work [7,14]). What is new is the *synthesis* into a "symmetry breaking by topology **and energy gap**" thesis and the tidy Theorem 5.2. This is a packaging/re-framing contribution rather than a discovery; the abstract's "radically different from Goldstone **and Higgs**" over-promises, because the **Higgs** comparison is only a single passing clause (long-range Coulomb delocalization) and is never actually developed or contrasted.

2. **The "energy gap" claim is a negative/existence statement, not a construction.** Theorem 5.2(iv) says a gap is *allowed* (the Goldstone obstruction is removed); it does not **exhibit** a gapped Hamiltonian in any rigorous example. The finite-dimensionality claim is a bit misleading — the physical space dimension is finite, but the Weyl algebra/GNS structure is infinite and non-separable. A concrete gapped model (e.g., an explicit Hamiltonian on $S^1$ or the torus with a provable gap tied to the topological-spectrum) would substantially strengthen it.

3. **Familiar physics dressed as a new mechanism.** Bloch bands, the absence of a sharp crystal-momentum operator, and lattice-translation gauge structure are standard condensed-matter lore. A reader fluent in either algebraic QFT (Doplicher–Haag–Roberts superselection) or band theory will recognize these results; I'd be cautious asserting "no counterpart in Dirac–Heisenberg quantization" as a selling point.

4. **The two-body example (Sec. 2, Example 1)** leans on a comparison with [15] (non-normalizable states) that reads as a somewhat contrived foil, and the resolution — simply passing to the Weyl algebra — is asserted ("in our opinion") rather than argued as uniquely preferable.

5. **Presentation polish.** Minor but numerous typos/notation drift: "superselected" → "superselcted", "sectors" → "seclors", and the two-body example switches between $v(β)$ and $V(β)$; the symbol load ($α, U, V, γ, λ, θ, \bar p$) is heavy for a framework paper.

### Soundness

The mathematics is correct to my reading: Theorem 5.1 (Goldstone) is a standard result; Theorem 5.2's proof that the Ward identity cannot be written is valid as stated. I do not find a mathematical error, but the central claim is closer to "the Goldstone *theorem does not apply*" than to a strong, constructive breaking mechanism — which is a real but modest point.

### Verdict

A rigorous, well-motivated framework paper whose main contribution is a clean, general statement (Thm 5.2) that topologically-originated superselection structure evades the Goldstone obstruction and hence permits a gap. It is a useful reference for the algebraic treatment of these systems, but the novelty is moderate, the energy-gap result is non-constructive, and the abstract overstates the departure from known physics. Solid, not landmark.

## Critique: "Gauge Invariance and Symmetry Breaking by Topology and Energy Gap" (Heissenberg & Strocchi, 2015)

### What the paper does
The authors work in a framework where a quantum system is quantized by a full canonical Weyl algebra $\mathcal F_W$ (the "field algebra"), but only a subalgebra $\mathcal A$ describes observables. The remaining Weyl operators act as *intertwiners* between inequivalent (factor) representations of $\mathcal A$. The center $\mathcal Z$ of $\mathcal A$ generates a "gauge group" $\mathcal G$, and this center is traced back to the first homology of the universal cover, i.e. the fundamental group of the configuration manifold. The central physical claims (Prop 4.1, Theorems 5.1–5.2) are: any symmetry that moves one superselection sector to another is "spontaneously broken" in every irreducible representation, and because the would-be symmetry generator $Q$ does not exist as an operator, the Ward identity needed for Goldstone's theorem cannot be written — so an energy gap is "allowed." Examples: the two-body center-of-mass, the Bloch electron, and the particle on a circle.

### Substantive problems

**1. The headline mechanism is largely tautological and mis-labeled.** The core result — a symmetry that does not commute with the center $\mathcal Z$ is not implemented by a unitary inside any single factor representation — is just the *definition* of superselection in the Wigner sense: a sector-*changing* automorphism maps inequivalent representations to one another and therefore cannot be represented within a single GNS space. Calling this "spontaneous symmetry breaking" is a strong, nonstandard terminological move that the paper never adequately defends. "Spontaneous" breaking presupposes a *degenerate manifold of ground states within one representation*, related by a symmetry of the Hamiltonian. Here the symmetry maps to a *different, physically distinct representation* (different superselection charge); there is no degenerate ground-state manifold at all. A careful reviewer would read Thm 5.2 as: "a symmetry that shifts the superselection label is not an internal symmetry of a sector" — which is true but a restatement of setup, not a new dynamical result. The "evading Goldstone" conclusion then follows almost by construction: if the generator $Q$ does not exist, the premises of Goldstone fail, so no massless mode is forced.

**2. A concrete technical misstatement: energy gap vs. ground-state energy.** The particle-on-a-circle spectrum is $\sigma^\theta(H)=\{(n+\theta/2\pi)^2/2m\}$, so the ground-state energy is $(\theta/2\pi)^2/2m$. The paper says this spectrum "displays an energy gap above the ground state energy $(\theta/2\pi)^2/2m$." The stated quantity is the ground-state *energy relative to zero*, not the gap to the first excited level. The true gap is $[(n^*+\theta/2\pi)^2-(\theta/2\pi)^2]/2m$ with $n^*\neq 0$ the nearest; at $\theta=0$ it is $1/2m$, while the "gap" the paper cites, $(\theta/2\pi)^2/2m$, *vanishes*. So the advertised topological gap formula collapses to zero exactly in the most symmetric sector, which directly undercuts the "energy gap allowed by topology" narrative. This conflation should have been caught.

**3. The examples are elementary re-labeled kinematics, not new physics.**
- The particle on a circle: the "order parameter" $\omega_\theta(\beta^\lambda(V(\beta)))\ne\omega_\theta(V(\beta))$ is just the fact that a momentum (quasi-momentum) eigenstate is not translation-invariant; the "gap" is the familiar ring-level spacing.
- The Bloch electron: Eqs. (3.5)–(3.6) *are* the standard Bloch decomposition $\psi_\theta(x)=e^{i\theta x/a}\sum c_n e^{i2\pi nx/a}$ and the usual crystal-momentum superselection. Nothing here requires the non-separable/non-regular Hilbert space the authors demand; ordinary band theory in a separable space gives identical predictions.
- The two-body problem: the "gauge transformation" is a translation of an unobserved center-of-mass coordinate, a textbook kinematical redundancy.

**4. The word "gauge" is overloaded, and the QFT promise is unmet.** Center-of-mass/lattice translations and $2\pi$ rotations are called "gauge transformations," but they are global kinematical symmetries/redundancies, not local gauge symmetries in the QFT sense. The abstract and introduction promise to "shed light on the more difficult infinite-dimensional GQFT models," yet the paper delivers only finite-dimensional toy models and asserts — by bare analogy, with no demonstration — that the structure generalizes. This is the stated main ambition and it is only gestured at.

**5. Originality is a synthesis, not a discovery.** Every load-bearing ingredient is well established and drawn from prior work, much of it by the authors and collaborators: non-regular Weyl representations (Löffelholz–Morchio–Strocchi [16]), the Zak algebra and its $L^2(\Sigma,\mu)$ classification (Zak [12], Cavallaro–Morchio–Strocchi [13]), non-separable Hilbert spaces from non-normalizable ground states (Albeverio–Bogoliubov–Limańka–Landsman / Baumé et al. [14]), and superselection from the fundamental group. Theorem 3.1 is cited to the authors' own book [1]; the foundational decompositions to [13]. What is genuinely new here is a *narrative packaging* — bundling these under "symmetry breaking by topology and energy gap" plus Theorem 5.2's one-page commutator argument — and that new packaging contains the gap misstatement and the overstated "radically different from Goldstone and Higgs" framing (the paper never actually engages the Higgs mechanism).

### What is defensible
The math that is stated is internally valid within the (grantable) non-regular framework, the writing is coherent, and Prop 4.1 / Thm 5.2 do correctly and cleanly show that $\beta^\lambda$ cannot be generated when it moves $\mathcal Z$ (via $[Q,V]\in\mathcal Z$ so $\omega_\theta(QA)$ is ill-defined, so the Ward identity fails). The unification is a legitimate contribution to the algebraic-QM literature, and the critique of the non-normalizable-ground-state workaround (cf. Jackiw [15]) is fair. But these valid pieces are dwarfed by the gap between the bold claims and what is actually shown.

### Verdict
A technically coherent, correctly-written position paper in a small algebraic-QM subfield that re-packages textbook examples (ring, Bloch bands, center of mass) in non-regular-representation language, asserts "spontaneous breaking" for what superselection already labels sector-changing, misstates its energy gap, and fails to deliver its promised connection to QFT. Competent but its headline claims overshoot its actual new content.

## Stated contribution (in my own words)

The authors study quantum systems whose canonical (Weyl) algebra $\mathcal{F}_W$ is represented by a **gauge‑invariant state** while only a subalgebra $\mathcal{A}$ (the genuine observables) is gauge‑invariant. The observable algebra $\mathcal{A}$ has a **non‑trivial center $\mathcal{Z}$**, and in the cases of interest $\mathcal{Z}$ is generated by **topological invariants** coming from the first homology group / fundamental group of the configuration manifold. These give inequivalent irreducible representations (the "θ‑sectors"). The representation of the full field algebra $\mathcal{F}_W$ is then *non‑regularly* represented (the Stone–von Neumann theorem does not apply): the gauge‑dependent Weyl operators exist, but their would‑be generators (the canonical variables that are not observables) do not exist as operators.

The novel claim, centered on **Proposition 4.1, Theorem 5.1 and Theorem 5.2**, is a new mechanism of spontaneous symmetry breaking. They argue:

1. Any algebraic symmetry $\beta$ of $\mathcal{A}$ that **fails to commute pointwise with the center $\mathcal{Z}$** (in particular a symmetry that moves the topological/central invariants) is **spontaneously broken in every irreducible representation** of $\mathcal{A}$.
2. Because the broken one‑parameter group $\beta^\lambda$ does not leave $\mathcal{Z}$ invariant, the symmetry‑breaking "order parameter" cannot be written as a commutator with an operator in the strong closure, so the **crucial Ward‑identity hypothesis of the Goldstone theorem is absent**.
3. Consequently the usual Goldstone conclusion (a gapless excitation) does **not** follow, and the spectrum may carry a **gap** above the ground state whose size is set by the **spectrum of the topological invariants** (the fundamental group). They label this "symmetry breaking by topology and energy gap," contrasted with both the Goldstone and the Higgs mechanism.

The claim is illustrated on finite‑dimensional toy models: the quantum particle on a circle (where the gap from the θ‑vacuum is $(\theta/2\pi)^2/2m$), the Bloch electron in a periodic potential, and the two‑body problem.

## [1511.01757v1-methodology] Methodology reproducibility


# Evaluation: Heissenberg & Strocchi, "Gauge Invariance and Symmetry Breaking by Topology and Energy Gap" (arXiv:1511.01757v1)

## A note on what the "central experiment" is

This is a mathematical-physics construction paper, not an experimental one. The "central experiments" that a reader must be able to reproduce are: (a) building the gauge-invariant GNS representation (non-separable, non-regular) from a pure gauge-invariant state on the full Weyl algebra, and (b) verifying in the concrete models — two-body problem, particle on a circle, Bloch electron — that translations (the gauge group) fail to be Wigner-implemented in each θ-sector, while the Hamiltonian spectrum remains gapped, evading the Goldstone theorem. Details for (a)–(b) follow; the assumptions the paper papers over are listed afterward.

## Methodology, in reproducible detail

**1. Algebraic scaffolding (Sec. 2).** Start from the full Weyl C*-algebra $\mathcal{F}_W$ of a Heisenberg group $G_H$ in canonical variables $\{q_i,p_i\}$, with Weyl operators satisfying $U(\alpha)V(\beta)=V(\beta)U(\alpha)e^{-i\alpha\beta}$ (in the 1-D form, eq. 3.1: $U(n)V(\beta)=e^{-in\beta}V(\beta)U(n)$, $U(n)=e^{in\hat\phi}$, $V(\beta)=e^{i\beta p}$). Pick a *proper* observable subalgebra $\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{F}_W$ generated by a Heisenberg subgroup times an abelian subgroup $\mathcal{C}$ that commutes with it. Then $\mathcal{C}$ generates a group of automorphisms $\alpha_g$ leaving $\mathcal{A}$ pointwise invariant (eq. 2.1) — the **gauge group** — and $\mathcal{A}$ has a non-trivial **center** $\mathcal{Z}$ generated by $\mathcal{C}$.

**2. Gauge-invariant GNS construction.** Take a state $\omega$ on $\mathcal{F}_W$ with $\omega(\alpha_g(F))=\omega(F)$. In its GNS representation, gauge transformations are implemented by unitaries $U(g)$; the GNS vector $\Psi_\omega$ is an eigenvector of $\pi_\omega(V(g))$ with eigenvalue $e^{i\theta(g)}$ (eq. 2.2). The GNS space decomposes as a **direct sum** $\mathcal{H}_{\pi_\omega}=\oplus_{\theta\in\sigma(\mathcal{Z})}\mathcal{H}_\theta$ over the spectrum of the center — the **θ-sectors**, each carrying a disjoint irreducible representation of $\mathcal{A}$. The representation is **non-regular**: the gauge one-parameter groups are not weakly continuous ($\langle U(\lambda)A\rangle=0$ for $\lambda\neq0$, end of Sec. 4), so their generators do not exist as operators; only exponentials do. Proposition 2.1 packages this: any gauge-invariant state gives a non-regular representation, and $\mathcal{C}$'s elements are **superselected charges**.

**3. Explicit model (Proposition 2.2 — this is the reproducible core).** For $\mathcal{A}_W(\mathbb{R}^2)$ with $V(\beta)$ as the gauge group, define $\omega_0(U(\alpha)V(\beta))=0$ for $\alpha\neq0$, $\omega_0(V(\beta))=e^{i\bar\beta p}$ ($\bar p\in\mathbb{R}$). The GNS space contains a cyclic vector $\Psi_0$ with $V(\beta)\Psi_0=e^{i\bar\beta p}\Psi_0$ and $\langle U(\alpha)\Psi_0,U(\alpha')\Psi_0\rangle=0$ for $\alpha\neq\alpha'$ (eq. 2.4). A generic vector $\Psi_A=\sum_n a_n U(\alpha_n)\Psi_0$ is written as a formal wavefunction $\psi_A(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_n e^{i\alpha_n x}$, $\{a_n\}\in\ell^2$, with the **ergodic-mean inner product** $\langle\psi_A,\psi_B\rangle=\sum|a_n|^2=\lim_{L\to\infty}(2L)^{-1}\int_{-L}^{L}\bar\psi_A\psi_B$ (eq. 2.5). This directly yields a non-separable Hilbert space, dense non-normalizable-free vector structure, and pure point spectrum for $V(\beta)$.

**4. Topological origin (Sec. 3).** The center is tied to the configuration manifold $\mathcal{M}$: the quotient of $\pi_1(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})$ by its commutator (i.e. $H_1(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})$, first homology) yields topological invariants that are represented by central elements of $\mathcal{A}$ (citing [7]). In the **particle on a circle**: $\pi_1(S^1)=\mathbb{Z}$, the center is generated by $V(2\pi)$, which in each irreducible representation is $e^{i\theta}$, $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$; Theorem 3.1 says all irreducible *regular* reps with the same $\theta$ are unitarily equivalent and the full regular space decomposes $\mathcal{H}=\oplus_{\theta}\mathcal{H}_\theta$. In the **Bloch electron** with $W(x+a)=W(x)$ and $H=-d^2/dx^2+W(x)$, Proposition 3.2 (citing [16]) asserts a *unique* irreducible CCR representation in which $H$ is a strong limit of Weyl elements and $p$ has discrete spectrum, independent of $W$: the space is $\mathcal{K}=\{\sum c_n e^{i\alpha_n x}\}$ with the same ergodic inner product; Weyl operators act by $(\pi_0(U(\alpha))\psi)(x)=e^{i\alpha x}\psi(x)$ and $(\pi_0(V(\beta))\psi)(x)=\psi(x+\beta)$ (eq. 3.4); it decomposes $\mathcal{K}=\oplus_\theta\mathcal{H}_\theta$ with $V(a)\mathcal{H}_\theta=e^{i\theta}\mathcal{H}_\theta$, $p$-spectrum in $\mathcal{H}_\theta$ equal to $\{2\pi n/a+\theta/a:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ (eq. 3.5–3.6, Bloch waves).

**5. Symmetry-breaking criterion (Sec. 4).** A symmetry $\beta$ of $\mathcal{A}$ is unbroken in the GNS space of a state iff it is Wigner-implemented (eq. 4.1). Proposition 4.1: (i) any symmetry defined by an element of $G_H$ that fails to commute with each element of the gauge group $\mathcal{C}$ is spontaneously broken in **every** irreducible (θ) sector of $\mathcal{A}$; (ii) any one-parameter subgroup not commuting with $\mathcal{C}$ is non-regularly represented in any gauge-invariant state, so its generator is not an operator and $\langle U(\lambda)A\rangle=0$ for $\lambda\neq0$ — proved by the central-element trick $V^{-1}U(\lambda)^{-1}VU(\lambda)=R(\lambda,V)\in\mathcal{Z}\equiv e^{i\theta(\lambda,V)}\mathbf{1}$.

**6. The Goldstone theorem and how it is evaded (Sec. 5 — the central claim).** Theorem 5.1 states the standard C*-algebraic Goldstone result: if β^λ is a continuous symmetry and there exists $F\in\pi(\mathcal{A})$ with a non-zero breaking order parameter $\langle\delta F\rangle=d\langle\beta^\lambda F\rangle/d\lambda|_0\neq0$ **and** a Ward identity $\langle\delta F\rangle=i\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle[Q_n(t),F]\rangle$ (eq. 5.2) for a net $Q_n\in\pi(\mathcal{A})''$ (the strong closure of the observables), then there is **no** energy gap — there is a Goldstone-like state orthogonal to the ground state at the same energy. Theorem 5.2 is the payoff: if β^λ does not leave $\mathcal{Z}$ pointwise invariant, then (breaks in every θ sector) **and** β^λ **cannot be generated by any element of the strong closure** of the field algebra in π_θ, so the Ward-identity hypothesis fails and an energy gap is *permitted*. The proof: if some $Q\in\pi(\mathcal{F}_W)''$ generated β, then for a unitary $V\in\mathcal{Z}$ with $i[Q,V]=\delta V\neq0$, the Jacobi identity gives $[[Q,V],\mathcal{A}]=0$, so $[Q,V]\in\mathcal{Z}$ (a c-number $c\neq 1$ in sector θ). Gauge invariance then forces the identity $\omega_\theta(QA)=\omega_\theta(QA)+\omega_\theta([Q,V])\omega_\theta(A)$, i.e. $(1-c)\omega_\theta(A)=0$ — a contradiction for any $A$ with $\omega_\theta(A)\neq0$. Hence no such $Q$ exists and the Ward identity cannot be written, so the Goldstone conclusion does not apply.

**7. Verification in the models.** Particle on a circle: β^λ acts as $\beta^\lambda(U(n))=U(n)$, $\beta^\lambda(V(\beta))=e^{-i\lambda\beta}V(\beta+\lambda)$ (eq. 5.6), i.e. the adjoint action of $U(\lambda)\in\mathcal{F}_W$; the order parameter is $\omega_\theta(\beta^\lambda(V(\beta)))=e^{-i\lambda\beta}e^{i\theta\beta/2\pi}\neq\omega_\theta(V(\beta))$. With $H=p^2/2m$, the θ-sector spectrum is $\sigma^\theta(H)=\{(n+\theta/2\pi)^2/2m:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ — a genuine gap $(\theta/2\pi)^2/2m$ above the ground state, "given by the spectrum of the fundamental group." Bloch electron: same mechanism with gap in the $p^2$ spectrum set by $\pi_1(\mathbb{R}/[0,a])$. To reproduce everything end-to-end: construct the ergodic-mean Hilbert space (eq. 2.5/3.3), write β^λ as adjoint by a non-observable Weyl element, exhibit the order parameter, compute the θ-dependent spectrum — no numerics are involved or needed anywhere.

## Assumptions the methodology papers over

1. **The "incompatibility" in the two-body problem is asserted, not proved.** Example 1 (p. 10) states that the gauge-invariant ground state must satisfy $P^2\Psi_0=0$ and $v(\beta)\Psi_0=\Psi_0$, that this "is incompatible with the canonical commutation relations," and that the standard workaround (allowing non-normalizable $\Psi_0$, citing Jackiw [15]) has "catastrophic consequences." None is proved.

The paper's only defense is the claim (p. 6) that these structures "have a very sound mathematical status" and are "no problem for the physical interpretation." This is a contested area: standard condensed-matter physics builds the *same* Bloch physics — same $p$-spectrum, same Bloch waves — in a separable $L^2$ space using the *regular* representation and a block-diagonalized (reducible) Hilbert space. This is precisely the locality/density postulate that gauge symmetries are famous for violating, and the paper takes "if a generator exists, it is in the strong closure" as part of the setup rather than deriving it. The gap appears in the examples by independently recomputing $\sigma^\theta(H)$ — for a particle on a circle, the shift $(n+\theta/2\pi)^2/2m$ is textbook angular-momentum quantization obtainable entirely within regular quantization. So the "prediction" is re-derived, not predicted: a regular quantization of the *extended* system also has gapped θ-sectors and the same spectra. The paper's stronger claim (that only the non-regular representation is physically acceptable) is what actually generates novelty, and that claim is only ever argued rhetorically ("in our opinion… a much better price to pay," p. 10).

9. **Applicability to gauge QFT is asserted from external, partly unpublished, authority.** The claim that the same structure arises in positive representations of the QED/QCD field algebra (p. 4, Sec. 1) rests on [3, 4, 5] (1992–93) and — critically — reference [6], "F. Strocchi, Gauge invariance and Weyl-polymer quantization, **to be published**" at the time of this 2015 preprint. The bridge from finite-dimensional toy models to infinite-dimensional field theory is an explicit analogy ("models recognized to mimic the basic structures of GQFT," p. 6), not a construction.

10. **Only abelian topology is handled.** The mechanism identifies the center with the *abelianized* fundamental group ($H_1$). All examples use $\pi_1=\mathbb{Z}$ (circle, quotient plane) — yet the motivating application (QCD, non-abelian gauge groups) has genuinely non-abelian fundamental groups, where the center's structure and the superselection labeling are more subtle. 11. **Regularity is demanded of observables and granted to nothing else.** The paper's foundational stance — the Stone-von Neumann regularity conditions apply to the observable subalgebra only, and are *in principle* dispensable for the gauge (inter-twiner) operators — is asserted as physical doctrine (Sec. 1, p. 3–4). 12. **The Goldstone theorem statement (Thm 5.1) uses a net $Q_n\in\pi(\mathcal{A})''$ with tempered-distribution convergence** — a standard but non-trivial technical apparatus (citing [1] and [17]) that the paper reproduces without proof, and whose failure for gauge symmetries is then *defined* into the answer by assuming (as above) that putative generators live in that same strong closure.

## The single weakest link

The weak link is **step 4 — the inference from "Goldstone's hypothesis fails" to "an energy gap"** — and, more sharply, the corollary that this gap is "related to the spectrum of the first homology group of the manifold" (as the abstract and introduction assert).

- Theorem 5.1 is an implication $H \Rightarrow (\text{no gap})$. Theorem 5.2, condition (iv), establishes only $\neg H$ (no sequence $Q_n \in \pi(\mathcal A)''$ weakly generates $\beta^\lambda$ with $\langle \delta F\rangle\neq 0$). The move $\neg H \therefore \text{gap}$ is *denying the antecedent*. $\neg H$ means the no‑gap conclusion is **no longer forced**, i.e. a gap is **compatible** — it conveys **zero information in the direction of existence** of the gap. This is a proof‑by‑absence: removing the Goldstone massless‑mode obstruction does not produce a gapped spectrum.
- The paper is internally honest about this in Section 5 ("an energy gap is *allowed*", "break … *compatibly with* an energy gap"), and indeed those are the only claims the data support. But the **title, the introduction, and the abstract advertise it affirmatively** ("symmetry breaking by topology **and energy gap**", and "an energy gap **related to the spectrum of the first homology group**"). The "spectrum of the first homology group" in the paper is the spectrum of the central operator, $\theta \in [0,2\pi)$, which labels **superselection sectors (a charge)**, not energies. Conflating the sector label $\theta$ (eigenvalue of a topological invariant) with an energy‑spectrum gap is exactly where the conclusion overreaches: the data tie topology to $\mathcal Z$'s spectrum and to non‑regularity, but never bridge $\theta$ to a gap.
- The three "displaying" examples confirm the gap isn't established. For the Bloch electron the only "gap" is the **ordinary band gap**, which exists in the free‑particle‑in‑a‑potential spectrum entirely independently of the $\theta$‑sector/symmetry‑breaking machinery (i.e. it is a gap a regular quantization already has). For the particle on a circle the "broken" transformations (the $2\pi n$ rotations) are the identity on $S^1$ / the central element itself, so there is no distinct continuous internal symmetry whose breaking yields a new gap. The examples thus do not instantiate the headline mechanism; they supply the non‑regularity and superselection structure, not a symmetry‑breaking‑induced gap.

Strocchi's background in algebraic QFT guarantees the statements are made carefully.
- **Theorem 5.2's argument, taken on its own terms, is valid.** The gauge-invariance step ω_θ(QA) = ω_θ(QA) + ω_θ([Q,V]V⁻¹)ω_θ(A), which forces ω_θ(QA) to be zero or undefined for an order parameter with ω_θ(A) ≠ 0, really does show that the symmetry-breaking Ward identity cannot be formulated on θ states when [Q,V] ≠ 0. That is the correct structural reason the Goldstone proof breaks down here.
- **Honest scope in the examples.** For the particle on the circle the θ-sector Hamiltonian spectrum σ_θ(H) = (n + θ/2π)²/(2m) is worked out explicitly and a gap is indeed present; the construction of K in Proposition 3.2 (ergodic-mean scalar product, quasiperiodic Bloch waves) is correct, and the observation that the same representation is independent of the form of W is a nice point.

### Weaknesses and defects

1. **The headline result proves less than it appears to.** Theorem 5.2 shows only that the Goldstone hypothesis *fails*, i.e. that a gap is *allowed*. It says nothing in the direction of existence: a broken internal symmetry in a finite system can have a gap for any number of reasons (a 2-state system does). The examples' gaps are in fact explained by banal, pre-existing physics — quantization on a compact manifold / the standard energy cost of Bloch band structure — not by any mechanism *produced* by the topological argument. The paper would be stronger if it admitted this explicitly rather than repeatedly attributing the gaps *to* the center spectrum.
2. **Terminological slip about the universal cover.** Section 3 states that the quotient of the fundamental group of "the universal covering space M̃" with its commutator subgroup, "i.e. the first homology group H₁(M̃)," generates the center. Since M̃ is simply connected, H₁(M̃) = 0 by construction; the invariant in the particle-on-a-circle example is π₁(S¹) = ℤ ≅ H₁(S¹). The intended statement is clear but as written is self-contradictory, and the same loose usage underlies the abstract.
3. **Conflation of center and covariant generator.** In the circle model, the center Z is generated by the gauge group (the translations γⁿ), while V(2π) is only the θ-valued scalar *within a sector* (a covariant object under the gauge action). The paper alternately calls V(2π)-type operators "topological invariants which define elements of Z"; this is a category shift that a careful mathematical-physics referee will flag, since the superselection content is carried by Z itself, not by V(2π).
4. **Misleading terminology "non-separable."** The GNS space of Proposition 2.2 is spanned by {U(α)Ψ₀ : α ∈ ℝ} with ⟨Ψ₀, U(α⁻¹α′)Ψ₀⟩ orthogonal for α ≠ α′; that index set is uncountable, but the concrete completion used in Examples 2 and in Proposition 3.2 consists of formal Fourier sums with ℓ² coefficient sequences — a *separable* Hilbert space. The paper's claim that the classification "requires non-separable Hilbert spaces" is not borne out by its own construction.
5. **The two-body example is under-determined.** Declaring the CM position "irrelevant" and treating the Hamiltonian (H_rel + P²) as gauge invariant under CM translations is only consistent if P² is dropped from the dynamics; the paper asserts this without stating it, and the argument that non-normalizable |Ψ₀⟩ is worse than non-regularity, while defensible, is advocacy over analysis.
6. **GQFT motivation is programmatic, not demonstrated.** The abstract and introduction promise light shed on GQFT (including QCD instanton measure problems), but the field-theoretic content is entirely by analogy: no field-theoretic model is quantized or computed, reference [6] is "to be published," [7] partly "work in preparation," and the Zak-algebra classification [13] is only assumed to apply to GQFT. For a prestigious venue the promise/payload mismatch is significant.
7. **Minor technical gaps.** In the Ward-identity manipulation of §5 the interchange of lim_n and the time average, and the distributional interpretation of (5.2)/(5.3), are asserted ("holds also…") without proof; the "Goldstone-like state" of Theorem 5.1 is sketched in one line.

### Verdict

This is a competently written, coherent, and mathematically honest synthesis by a distinguished algebraic-QFT group, making a genuinely useful *re-framing* of a decade of non-regular Weyl quantization into a general superselection/gap mechanism, but the core ingredients are pre-publicated, the examples are standard physics re-described, the headline theorem establishes only non-derivation (not existence) of gaps, and there are fixable but real terminological and notational defects. It is acceptable for a specialized math-ph journal (J. Math. Phys. / L. Math. Phys. tier) after minor revision; as framed for a "prestigious journal," the conceptual advance is modest relative to the claims made, and the impact beyond the algebraic QFT/math-ph niche is limited — the framework (non-separable/doubly-discontinuous representations) has not become part of the standard toolkit, and polymer quantization absorbed much of the wider interest in non-regular Weyl structures.

**Recommendation:** Minor to major revision; acceptable for a specialized venue as is, with the terminology defects fixed and the "gap from the center spectrum" claims weakened to "gap compatible with, and naturally present in, these models."

## [1511.01757v1-mentor] Mentor


I've read the full paper (23 pp., arXiv:1511.01757v1, Heissenberg & Strocchi, "Gauge Invariance and Symmetry Breaking by Topology and Energy Gap"). Here is my review, in the form of feedback to you.

## Summary of what the paper claims

The paper generalizes the Weyl-polymer/non-regular-quantization program you've developed with collaborators: for a quantum system whose configuration manifold has nontrivial fundamental group, the observable C\*-algebra $\mathcal{A}$ sits inside a larger Weyl (field) algebra $\mathcal{F}_W$, has a nontrivial center $\mathcal{Z}$ of topological origin, and $\mathcal{Z}$ generates a gauge group. Gauge-invariant ground states give GNS representations that are non-regular (non-separable, direct sum over $\theta$-sectors, no generator for the non-regular one-parameter groups). The new content is Section 5:

- **Theorem 5.1**: a finite-system analog of the Goldstone theorem using the standard $Q_n \in \pi(\mathcal{A})''$ generator sequence and distributional Ward identity (5.2).
- **Theorem 5.2**: if a continuous symmetry $\beta^\lambda$ does not leave $\mathcal{Z}$ pointwise invariant (i.e. it acts nontrivially on the topological invariants), then it is spontaneously broken in every irreducible representation, and the Ward-identity premise of Goldstone *fails* precisely because $i[Q,V]=\delta V\neq 0$, $[Q,V]\in\mathcal{Z}$ makes the expectation $\omega_\theta(QA)$ undefined — hence an energy gap is *allowed*. Examples: particle on the circle (explicitly gapped spectrum $\sigma^\theta(H)=\frac{(n+\theta/2\pi)^2}{2m}$), the Bloch electron, the two-body problem.

## What is good

1. **The central mechanism is clean and, I think, correct.** The failure of the Goldstone theorem is not presented as a paradox to resolve but as a structural feature: the symmetry generator does not belong to the strong closure $\pi(\mathcal{A})''$, and the would-be Ward identity runs into an undefined term *because* $[Q,V]$ is a nontrivial center element. The one-line "the gap is the spectrum of the fundamental group/center" is a genuinely elegant slogan that unifies the examples.
2. **Theorem 5.1 is the right device.** Recasting the Goldstone proof in terms of the $Q_n$ sequence and requiring the tempered-distribution limit (5.2) makes the *actual hypothesis that fails* explicit, rather than asserting failure.
3. **The circle example is fully worked** (Eq. (5.6), order parameter, commutation with $T(\tau)$, discrete gapped spectrum), so the theorems are not hanging in the void.

## Substantive issues to address

These are the points that will decide the reception of the paper:

1. **"Finite-system SSB" needs to be defended, not asserted.** Your framing is that you've found symmetry breaking with a gap *in finite-dimensional systems*, evading Goldstone. But the Goldstone theorem is a theorem about *extended* (infinite) systems; a finite system is **trivially** gapped in its spectrum, so "the gap is allowed" is vacuous unless you make precise what your algebraic SSB *adds* over the ordinary fact that a finite spectrum is gapped. A skeptical reader (and there will be one) will say: you have *defined* SSB broadly enough (non-invariant state + non-derivable generator) that the theorem follows by construction. You must state explicitly that this is an *algebraic* notion of SSB, explain why it is physically meaningful in its own right, and say what it predicts in the infinite/extended limit. Right now the paper conflates "the Ward identity fails in this representation" with "Goldstone does not apply to this physics," and the second needs more than the first.

2. **The Bloch electron is doing the work you don't want it to.** In the crystal, the periodic potential *explicitly* breaks continuous translations, so a condensed-matter reader will say: the gap is just explicit breaking; the usual resolution already covers it, and no new mechanism is needed. Your re-encoding (lattice translations as *gauge*, all continuous $V(\beta)$ as observables, $\theta$-vacua as ground states) is a choice of observable algebra *made to fit the formalism* — and the paper should be candid about this. As written, the Bloch example reads as "we can re-encode known physics," which is fine as a consistency check but weak as evidence for the mechanism. Either (a) label it explicitly as such, or (b) strengthen it by exhibiting something nontrivial the formalism *predicts* that the explicit-breaking account doesn't give.

3. **The two-body example overstates its case.** You write that allowing a non-normalizable $\varphi_0$ (Jackiw) "would have catastrophic consequences... all matrix elements (including the ground-state expectations of gauge-invariant operators) would be divergent and one could not extract finite results." That is too strong: the standard delta-normalized center-of-mass treatment yields perfectly finite relative-state amplitudes and finite energy spectra. Your non-separable / ergodic-mean construction is a *cleaner* representation, not the *only* consistent one. Tone this down; otherwise a referee will reject the premise.

4. **Formal-level manipulations in the Theorem 5.2 proof.** You work with $Q\in\pi(\mathcal{F}_W)''$ defined as a strong-closure limit of $Q_n$, and then form products like $QA$, $QVAV^{-1}$, and take $\omega_\theta$ of them. These products are not a priori defined on the dense domain, and the step "hence $\omega_\theta(QA)$ cannot be defined" is an *indirection* argument (contradicting a term that was never well-defined) rather than a rigorous demonstration of non-existence of the Ward identity. At minimum: restrict to the cyclic vectors $\Psi_A = A\Psi_0$ of $A$, show the commutator expression blows up along that sequence, and then state the general claim. As it stands the proof is persuasive to an operator-algebraist but will not survive a careful functional-analysis referee.

5. **The connection to QGFT remains programmatic.** You say the toy models "shed light on the more difficult infinite-dimensional GQFT models" — but there is no explicit bridge: no lattice gauge model, no statement about how $\pi_1(\mathcal{M})$-type invariants of the *field* configuration space (as opposed to the *particle* manifold) enter. The introduction's critique of the QCD topological-sector / instanton literature is interesting but the paper doesn't actually engage with it. Pick one concrete QGFT or lattice model and state precisely what you claim follows from Theorem 5.2.

6. **Scope.** All examples are abelian, one-dimensional, and the center is assumed commutative throughout (a "commutative subgroup $\mathcal{G}"$). The non-abelian case — the physically interesting gauge case — is completely open. This limitation should be flagged in the abstract or conclusion, not left for the reader to discover.

## Smaller issues (fix before submission)

- Typo "superselcted charges" (p. 7) → "superselected".
- Eq. (2.2): "$\varphi$ is an eigenvector of $\pi_\omega(V(g))$, with eigenvalue $e^{i\bar p(g)}$" — $g$ here is an element of a group but $\bar p$ is a real number; either define the character $\bar p:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ or replace $g$ with the parameter.
- Theorem 3.1 is cited to [1] (your book). If the proof is there, say so and skip reproducing; if a *new* point here is the decomposition of $\pi_S$ of $\mathcal{F}_W$, state it as a remark.
- References [6] "to be published" and [7] "work in preparation" — for a 2015 arXiv preprint this is fine, but for a journal submission these must exist; [7] in particular is load-bearing (the claim that $\pi_1$ generates $\mathcal{Z}$).
- Notation: $\pi_\theta$ is used both for irreducible reps of $\mathcal{A}$ and (in the Remark after Th. 5.2) for the regular representation of $\mathcal{F}_W$. Distinguish them explicitly; the distinction is also *physically* important (in the regular rep the breaking appears only through the direct-integral decomposition; in the non-regular GNS rep there is no gauge-invariant vector at all), and this deserves a paragraph of its own, not a remark.
- The abstract's "Weyl-polymer quantization" keyword is only weakly justified in the body — you use Zak's / non-regular Zak-algebra classification rather than the polymer construction per se. Add one sentence connecting the two or drop "polymer."

## Bottom line

The core result (Th. 5.2 and the Ward-identity failure argument) is a clean, correct, and genuinely interesting reformulation: it replaces an implicit "Goldstone says there must be a gap, but there isn't" tension with a structural statement about when the Goldstone hypothesis is violated. That is worth publishing. But in the current form the paper asks the reader to believe that a collection of 1D abelian toy models with a *chosen* gauge structure constitutes an explanation of gapped symmetry breaking, and to accept a level of formalism in the main proof that won't hold under scrutiny. To get there, you need to: (i) make the "finite-system SSB" notion precise and defend why it's more than a definition; (ii) restrict the Bloch example to what it actually shows; (iii) tighten the Theorem 5.2 proof to the cyclic domain; (iv) either make a concrete QGFT claim or say candidly that the QGFT connection is a program. Do those four and the paper moves from interesting reformulation to a defensible result.
