## [1105.2027v1-general-review] General Review


# Review: Fleming et al., "Eclipsing Binary Science Via the Merging of Transit and Doppler Exoplanet Survey Data" (2011)

## What the paper argues

The paper's case rests on one efficiency argument and two case studies. The argument: exoplanet Doppler surveys already measure SB orbits (period, K1, T0, e) at high precision but have no photometry, while transit surveys have years of high-cadence photometry but no orbits. Since the eclipse probability is only (R1+R2)/a (a few percent to ~25%), targeted photometric follow-up of SBs is a poor bet — but querying *archival* transit-survey photometry at the known RV orbit is nearly free. Cross-referencing 24 short-period SBs from the MARVELS Pilot Project (MPP) with SuperWASP finds 2 eclipsing binaries among them: TYC 272 (an SB1: G primary + K-dwarf secondary) and TYC 1422 (a triple-lined SB: evolved primary + G-dwarf pair + a third component). The two systems then serve as demonstrations of a general framework (Fig. 14) for extracting masses and radii for both EB components from survey data alone.

The paper does what it sets out to do and is honest about where it falls short. The review below focuses on the argument's strength.

## Critique

**The strongest part of the paper is its framing, and it is well made.** The asymmetry of costs (archival photometry is cheap; targeted RV follow-up for SB2s is expensive) correctly predicts that cross-referencing is the efficient direction, and the observed yield (2/17 ≈ 12% of short-period SBs are EBs) is right in the expected band — so the numbers actually support the opening motivation rather than contradict it. This is the part a reader would retain.

**Weakness 1 — the headline science claim is not really tested.** The stated motivation is the inflation of K/M dwarfs (10–20% above model radii). But both systems are found merely "consistent (1σ) with model predictions." For TYC 272 the secondary radius is R2 = 0.559 ± 0.102 R☉ — an 18% error. A 10–20% model-vs-observation offset is not distinguishable at that precision, so the paper cannot actually speak to its own motivating problem. The result is a *methodological* demonstration, not a *conclusion* about mass-radius physics, and the "consistency" wording slightly oversells it. TYC 272's primary mass error (~12%) is dominated by the isochrone-based constraint, and that is the bottleneck the authors correctly identify but can't resolve with this data.

**Weakness 2 — the framework leans on model-dependent ages set by "visual inspection."** For TYC 272 the primary mass comes from Padova isochrones at an assumed age (they compare log t = 8.0 vs 9.95 and take the 3-σ EW Teff range). For TYC 1422 the age is "selected as the best-fit by visual inspection of tracks with differing ages" (Fig. 13 caption). This is the weakest epistemic step in an otherwise careful error treatment: the age is a free, poorly constrained parameter (a single-field system, no isochrone age is really possible), and choosing it by eye while simultaneously fitting the same diagram for the radius is close to circular. The mass/radius "agreement" is partly the result of an age chosen to make it agree.

**Weakness 3 — imprecise language about TYC 1422's primary.** §5.2.3 calls it an "evolved primary star on the main sequence with a mass of M1 = 1.163 M☉ and a radius of R1 = 2.063 R☉." A 1.16 M☉ star cannot have 2.06 R☉ on the main sequence; Fig. 13 places it at log t = 9.77, i.e. well off the turnoff/subgiant. "Evolved" and "on the main sequence" are contradictory here. This matters for exactly the population the paper is motivated to study (the radius anomaly), so the classification should be tightened.

**Weakness 4 — the triple case is nice but heavily compromised.** TYC 1422 is the more scientifically interesting system (only one where both K amplitudes are measured, giving 2.9–4.2% precision on masses/radii, genuinely in the paper's target regime). But 25% third light (L3/L1 = 0.25) contaminates the photometry and biases the eclipse model, the MPP RVs are unusable because of flux blending and could not be de-fringed (iodine cell), and the Component-1 linear trend (−75 ± 8 m/s/day) attributed entirely to "residual instrument drift" is a somewhat generous assignment — a 4th body or a real effect is not ruled out over a 3.5 km/s total drift. The authors are candid about all of these, but they collectively keep TYC 1422 from being the clean benchmark they hope it is.

**Weakness 5 — very small sample, and the framework is narrow.** Two systems, both requiring both a primary and a secondary eclipse (a stated limit in Fig. 14), does not establish that "much larger datasets" will be tractable. There is no demonstration that the SB1 pipeline (isochrone-constrained M1) is robust across a range of primaries — which is exactly the regime with no direct mass measurement. A larger MPP/SuperWASP cross-match or a validation on the existing De Vor / Fernández EB catalogues would have turned a plausible framework into a tested one.

**Minor methods notes.** The "Q" quality-factor scaling of RV errors is reasonable but field-dependent and untested per-object; dropping cross-terms in the σ_M2 propagation (Eq. 5) is justified by assuming M1 and C independent, fine, but the correlated (i, sin i) and (e cosω, e sin ω) uncertainties flowing into the radii deserve a one-line justification. The grazing secondary eclipse of TYC 272 forces the flux ratio to be assumed (8 ± 4%), and its effect on R1/R2 is only noted in passing — for a paper about radii, that assumption deserves more quantified sensitivity.

## Bottom line

A clean, honest, well-motivated pilot paper whose real contribution is the workflow (Fig. 14) and the demonstration that survey-grade data can already yield EB masses and radii — not new stellar-evolution constraints. The two case studies are executed with good error discipline but are, by the authors' own admission, too imprecise (TYC 272) or too contaminated (TYC 1422) to advance the scientific question they open with. The "consistent with models" framing slightly overstates the evidential value given the error bars, the age choices are the soft underbelly of the analysis, and a few classification phrases should be tightened. Useful foundation, modest incremental payoff.

I've read the full paper (main text, tables, figures). This is Fleming et al. (2011) — "Eclipsing Binary Science Via the Merging of Transit and Doppler Exoplanet Survey Data" (the MARVELS+SuperWASP cross-referencing paper, later published in ApJL). Here is my honest peer review.

## Major strengths

- **Timely and well-motivated.** The premise — that transit-survey and Doppler-survey catalogs can be cross-referenced to find eclipsing binaries (EBs) cheaply, before follow-up is committed — was a genuinely useful idea when this appeared. Starting from RV solutions (known P, e, T̄p) and searching archival photometry for eclipses is the right way to spend scarce spectroscopic time; the paper makes this argument clearly (the a-priori eclipse probability P(R1+R2)/a discussion) and demonstrates it end-to-end.
- **Methodologically thorough for the objects.** The TYC 272 (SB1) treatment is the strongest part: four independent T_eff/metallicity determinations of the primary (SME spectral synthesis, Fe I/Fe II equivalent widths, Hα profile, Monte Carlo infrared flux method), an RPM diagram to rule out giant status, and a SED plausibility check attributing the near-IR excess to a K-type companion. That redundancy is exactly what a reviewer wants to see when the primary mass is model-derived.
- **Honest about failure modes.** It is commendable that the authors (a) explicitly flag the iodine-cell problem that prevents de-fringing the MPP spectra of SBs, (b) discard the heavily contaminated MPP RVs for TYC 1422 and go to SMARTS instead, and (c) point out the grazing secondary eclipse and SB1 flux-ratio degeneracy as the reasons TYC 272 is *not* a benchmark system despite being a nice demo.

## Major weaknesses

1. **Very small statistical yield.** The central claim of the abstract is a "framework," but the deliverable is 2 new EBs out of a 24-body cross-match of a *pilot* data set. Five SBs had too little photometric coverage, two had blended stars — so 17 were usable and only 2 eclipsed. The yield/efficiency numbers that would make this a resource-allocation argument for MARVELS/K2 are not quantified. As a population claim it is essentially a proof-of-principle, and the paper understates this by not computing the expected discovery rate.
2. **The headline mass-radius numbers for TYC 272 are not benchmark quality, and the paper knows it.** M1 is isochrone-derived (log g > 4.0, metallicity Z=0.01, 100 Myr–9 Gyr age range), not measured. The authors give σM2 ≈ 6%, σR1 ≈ 8%, σR2 ≈ 18% and note these fall short of the <3% goal for constraining the K/M-dwarf radius-inflation problem. So the "mass-radius consistency with models" result for TYC 272 is nearly circular: the mass is *read off* the model isochrone, and then shown to agree with the model isochrone. The independent content is only that the RV K1, the T_eff spread, and the radii are mutually consistent. This is real but weaker than the abstract implies.
3. **Error propagation is approximate.** Eq. 5 treats P, K1, e, i as statistically independent (a_1 is the same quantity on both sides of the mass function), and the inclination/eccentricity are taken from the photometry at ~1-σ. For a system whose whole purpose is a <3% radius program, dropping correlations between the RV-derived and photometric-derived orbital parameters is the leading systematic and is not propagated. The authors do use the "prayer-bead" bootstrap for the lightcurve but state the RV side is analytic. A proper joint likelihood (RV+photometry, as Prša & Zwitter 2005 do — the paper cites it — but is deliberately not done) would tighten and properly correlate at least some of these.
4. **TYC 1422 "triple" claim is thin.** The third component's nature is asserted only from Na D in one APO spectrum (Fig. 8) and a linear RV trend (−75 m/s/day) that the authors themselves attribute to *residual instrument drift*, not to a real orbit. So "triple-component system" is really "eclipsing pair + an unidentified third flux/RV contributor." The paper would be stronger either demonstrating a wider-period orbit or explicitly renaming it "double-lined EB with an unresolved third light."
5. **Model dependence of the flux-ratio/limb-darkening treatment in jtkbo.** The grazing secondary makes J extremely sensitive; 8±4% flux-ratio adopted from SED rather than measured is a significant lever arm on the R2/ρ2 degeneracy. No sensitivity analysis to that 4% is shown.
6. Minor: the 9 Cet comparison (Fig. 6) is a *visual* detection-limit claim ("no secondary set of spectra can be seen") — fine, but worth stating as a formal upper limit; the Hα T_eff (5461 K) is noted to be affected by companion + activity contamination yet is still fed into the final T_eff band via the 3-σ EW interval and the SME choice, which is reasonable but the final "best" value (5785 K from the SED combination) deserves a more explicit uncertainty budget. The Q-scaling of RV errors (Q=3.44) is taken from the field's *other* 58 objects — appropriate given the single-line nature, but the assumption that the SB's own jitter doesn't inflate the field median is unexamined.

## Verdict

A competent, well-executed, candid proof-of-concept that will appeal to anyone building on MARVELS/K2/TESS-style catalogs, with a genuinely useful "analysis flow diagram" and an honest discussion of when the method fails (SB1 grazing EBs, iodine-cell DFdI, third light). It is not a benchmark result, and the two headline masses/radii carry ~6–18% errors that the authors themselves note are too large for the science the abstract promises. The statistical claim ("this is how we scale up") outstrips the n=2 evidence. I would accept with the minor revisions above, but would strongly encourage the authors to (i) add the expected-yield calculation and (ii) temper the abstract's mass-radius language to match the TYC 272 precision.

# Critique: Fleming et al. 2011, "Eclipsing Binary Science Via the Merging of Transit and Doppler Exoplanet Survey Data"

## What the paper does
Cross-matches 24 spectroscopic binaries (SBs, P<20 d) from the 2007 MARVELS Pilot Project (MPP, Keck ET/DFFI with I₂ cell) against SuperWASP photometry, discards 7 with too little photometry and 2 blended, and develops two of the remaining 17 as case-study eclipsing binaries: **TYC 0272** (SB1) and **TYC 1422** (SB3). The intent is to demonstrate that the *combination* of an exoplanet Doppler survey and a transit survey can yield EB masses and radii without dedicated follow-up photometry/spectroscopy — a prerequisite for the (then-emerging) F/G + K/M-dwarf mass–radius program.

## What works

- **Correct scientific framing.** The case for cross-referencing Doppler SBs and photometric EB candidates is laid out well (lines 2–9, p. 2–3) and matches the state of the field (Prša & Zwitter 2005 was cited; Torres/CoRoT EB studies are the comparator for the mass–radius relation science driver in §2).
- **Multiple mass-estimation techniques.** For the SB1 (TYC 272) where a dynamical M₁ is not possible, they genuinely try to triangulate M₁ through SME, EW, Hα, IRFM (Monte-Carlo) and an SED fit, plus an independent luminosity-class check (RPM diagram) to exclude a giant primary. The RPM validation against KIC (2% misclassification of verified giants) is a reasonable sanity check, even if the KIC regime is not exactly the TYC 272 one.
- **Honest error book-keeping.** They explicitly propagate σ_{M1} into σ_{M2} (Eq. 5), use a prayer-bead bootstrap for correlated super-epoch errors in the EOP fit, and they are candid that M₂ errors are dominated by M1 for the SB1.
- **Reproducibility of the method, at least.** The §6 "analysis flow diagram" and the explicit identification of the blocking issues in the MPP (I₂ cell, blending, insufficient secondary-flux ratio measurement) are genuinely useful documentation for people running MARVELS.

## Substantive problems

1. **The precision goal is not met for the system the paper argues is the interesting one.** §2 cites the "few percent" precision requirement of López-Morales/Torres as the science driver. The SB1 — which is precisely the class that has been missing from EB mass–radius catalogs (F/G + K/M, small flux ratio) — comes out at **M₂ = 0.610 ± 0.036 M⊙ (6 %)** and, worse, **R₂ = 0.559 ± 0.102 R⊙ (18 %)**. That is far off the target, and the R₂ is unreliable because the secondary eclipse is grazing (the well-known radius/brightness degeneracy, and the super-WASP photometry is ~30–50 mmag per point). The paper concedes this (p. 14) but then still calls TYC 272 "an excellent system to further study in the context of the mass-radius relationship," which is an overstatement.

2. **TYC 1422's "linear trend" is suspicious.** Component 1 shows an RV drift of **−75 m/s/day** over ~2–3 months (≈ 6 km/s over the SMARTS baseline) — the paper offers "residual instrument drift" as an explanation (p. 16). A real astrophysical linear trend (a wide fourth companion still on a much longer orbit, or a period super-period from a fourth body not yet visible) is at least as likely. The SMARTS pipeline (MPRVFIT2) has no explicit drift-removal, and calling it "drift" without an instrumental justification weakens the SB2 result. The 2.9% precision on M₁ they advertise is built on unmodeled drift.

3. **Eq. (5) is dimensionally inconsistent as printed.** Reading the PDF: σ_{M2} = [4σ_{M1}²C²(M₁+M₂)² + σ_C²(M₁+M₂)⁴ / (3 M₂² − 2C(M₁+M₂))]^{1/2}. Because C has dimensions of mass [M], the numerator inside the bracket is a sum of an [M⁶] term and an [M⁴] term (if the fraction divides only the second) — or [M⁴] after the full-division (if it divides everything). Either way the bracket is **not** [M²], so the square root does not have mass dimension, i.e. it cannot be σ_{M2}. The printed form is almost certainly a typesetting/bracketing error in the equation; the derivation as I re-derive it from f = M2³/(M1+M2)² = C gives a different structure (the (M1+M2) powers and the M₂² denominator factor in a different way from what Eq. 5 shows). Readers will not be able to reproduce σ_{M2} from Eq. (5) as written. (This is a real correctness concern for anyone re-implementing the framework.)

4. **Ad-hoc Q-factor.** The "quality factor" Q = RMS(X−X̄)/(MEDIAN σ_X) used to inflate RV uncertainties is borrowed from the group's 2010 paper and essentially just multiplies the *formal* errors by the field's median over-dispersion. For a field with Q = 3.44 (TYC 272) and 3.07 (TYC 1422), this implies the MPP formal RV errors are under-estimated by a factor of ~3, which is a significant systematic that propagates into a1 (and hence into masses) without an explicit statement about how that factor should be handled in σ_{a1} (and therefore in σ_{a} and σ_R).

5. **Tiny, visually curated sample.** The 24→17→2 funnel was performed by visual inspection of the super-WASP lightcurves. That workflow is not what would "apply this analysis to much larger datasets" (the abstract's promise); it is a manual proof-of-concept that would not scale to, say, the 10⁴ SBs in MARVELS or the hundreds of EBs in the KIC. The paper has no quantitative yield-per-unit-time estimate, no simulation study, and no algorithmic EB-identification — the "framework" of §6 is really a flow chart, not a method.

6. **M₁ of the SB1 is fully model-dependent.** M₁ = 0.92 ± 0.1 M⊙ comes from the Padova isochrones (two age tracks, Z = 0.01) over the wide 4-σ T_Eff range 5483–5957 K from four discrepant techniques (5461, 5720, 5905, 5459 K — the spread is ~450 K, larger than any individual error). No TODCOR-style independent mass check from a larger telescope/high-SN spectrum is attempted; the authors acknowledge this and recommend it, but the headline "M₂ = 0.61" is really an isochrone result wearing a dynamical-analysis coat.

7. **The I₂-cell problem is a genuine limitation, but the paper's response is too thin.** For a 2011 paper positioning "this is how MPP/MARVELS will produce EBs," the SMARTS-only follow-up for TYC 1422 (because the MPP I₂ line could not be removed) means the *entire* mass/radius result for the SB2 comes from a single instrument at ~20/res-element SN. No independent instrument cross-check is reported for the SMARTS RVs.

8. **Super-WASP precision is being stretched.** 1003 points over 10 nights for TYC 272 and 649 over 12 nights for TYC 1422, with super-WASP's known ~30–50 mmag scatter, are being fitted to EOP models to get percent-level R₁ for an eclipsing system. The paper doesn't quantify what the photometric point-to-point precision actually was, or how sensitive the radii are to the per-night offset model; the prayer-bead is used for correlated errors, but the super-WASP's long-term systematics are not characterized.

## Minor
- "The secondary eclipse of TYC 272 is grazing" → the R₂ error should scale more strongly with grazing depth; the error budget isn't broken down to show that.
- The flux-ratio estimate for TYC 272, K_V < 20 %, is from a non-detection in ARCES; the *adopted* flux ratio of 8 ± 4 % is not from spectroscopy but "consistency with SED," which is not an independent constraint and is not clearly separated from the mass/radius analysis.
- The 24 SBs have P < 20 d, but the paper does not discuss how the sample is biased by the MPP cadence (short-period, RV-amplitude-limited), i.e. the "found 2 EBs / 24 SBs" rate is not a general cross-match yield for future samples.
- Several figures are cited that I do not see (Fig. 10, Fig. 14); the text is fine without them.

## Bottom line

This is a competent, careful, and *useful* demonstration that combining a multi-object Doppler survey and a transit survey can close the EB mass/radius loop, and it does so with the right attitude about what is model-dependent vs. dynamical. But the case-study yield is small (two systems from 24 candidates, one of which is a triple with a suspicious 75 m/s/d drift, and the SB1 — the one system of scientific interest for the mass–radius program — comes out at 18 % in R₂, well short of the "few per cent" goal the paper sets itself). The promised scalable framework is a flow chart, not a method. The mass-error-propagation formula (Eq. 5) is also dimensionally inconsistent as printed, which is an actual bug for anyone re-implementing the paper.

**Originality**: the underlying idea (merge photometric and Doppler EB catalogs) is not new (Prša & Zwitter 2005); the MPP+SuperWASP execution and the SB1 mass-constraint workflow are incremental.

**Overall soundness**: solid analysis, honest uncertainties, one dimensional-incorrect equation, and a case-study precision that falls below the paper's own stated motivation.

**Impact**: a good proof of concept for the MARVELS program that fed a handful of follow-up papers, but the mass–relation field was driven by CoRoT, Kepler KIC EBs, and Torres/OGLE — not by this paper.

## Stated Contribution (in my own words)

The paper presents a **methodology/framework for turning eclipsing binary (EB) stars incidentally found in two independently-operated exoplanet surveys — a Doppler (radial velocity) survey (the MARVELS Pilot Project) and a photometric transit survey (SuperWASP) — into a source of precise stellar masses and radii**, for use in studying the K/M-dwarf mass-radius relationship and testing stellar-evolution models (in particular the "inflated-radius" anomaly of short-period, magnetically-active low-mass stars). The authors cross-match 24 short-period spectroscopic binaries from the RV survey against SuperWASP light curves, identify the eclipsing ones, and develop the approach on two case studies: **TYC 272** (an SB1, i.e. only the primary's RVs are measurable — so the primary mass must be pinned down from photometry/stellar models, and the secondary mass then falls out of the mass function plus eclipse geometry) and **TYC 1422** (an EB that turns out to be an SB3 triple, for which both components of the eclipsing pair can be measured directly). The deliverable is a general, replicable "analysis flow diagram" that says: given SBs from a Doppler survey plus archival transit-survey photometry, here is how to derive M1, M2, R1, R2 without costly dedicated follow-up, with future improvements (TODCOR on faint companions, better graze-EB photometry) outlined.

## Novelty Assessment

**What is genuinely new:**

1. **The specific cross-referencing strategy itself.** Merging a *Doppler* planet survey's spectroscopic binaries with a *transit* survey's photometry to find eclipsing binaries at F/G primaries — and thereby getting the RV amplitudes (from the RV survey, which has the epoch count and cadence) plus the eclipse photometry (from the transit survey, which has near-continuous phase coverage) without a matched pair of dedicated programs. The paper makes a reasonable and useful point that this is more efficient than either of the two one-sided approaches (photometric follow-up of SBs to find EBS — mostly null detections because typical a-priori eclipse probabilities are only a few percent to ~25% — or getting expensive high-precision RV follow-up of photometric EBs to pin down masses). The F/G-primary + K/M-secondary focus also sidesteps the mass-ratio-near-unity degeneracies of K/M-K/M EBs. This framing — using exoplanet survey byproducts for stellar-astrophysics mass/radius work — is the core novelty and is well motivated.

2. **The SB1 treatment via "mass-from-photometry" for a flux-ratio-asymmetric EB.** For TYC 272, the secondary's K-line signature is too faint for the survey spectrum, so the primary mass is derived from color/SN/SED/PFM/isochrone consistency and then propagated through the mass function. The careful multi-technique T_eff determination (SME, Fe-line EW, Hα core, IRFM, SED) feeding into the isochrone mass constraint is a reasonable if laborious recipe.

3. **The "analysis flow diagram" as a reusable procedure** (Section 6, Fig. 14), explicitly targeting larger datasets (e.g., MARVELS proper, CoRoT, later TESS).

**What is NOT new (already in the cited/existing literature, and the paper itself concedes this):**

1. **The core physics and equations.** The mass function (Eq. 3), the RV-semi-amplitude → semi-major-axis relation (Eq. 4), the Kepler's-third-law mass determination for SB2s (Eq. 7), and the radius-from-geometric-lightcurve-parameters approach are all standard EB analysis. The paper explicitly cites Prša & Zwitter (2005) for simultaneous RV+photometry modeling and says "many software packages exist to simultaneously model all observational data." Nothing in the orbital/photometric analysis (jkteop EBOP fits, mass function propagation) is new.

2. **The individual stellar-characterization techniques** are all borrowed: SME spectral synthesis (Valenti & Fischer 2005), the EW/excitation ionization method (Porto de Mello et al. 2008; Gonzalez et al. 2010 — actually "Ghezzi et al. 2010"), Hα line profiles (Lyra & Porto de Mello 2003), the infrared flux method (Casagrande et al. 2010), reduced-proper-motion diagram (Collier Cameron et al. 2007b), TODCOR (Zuckerman & Mazeh 1994; Zuckerman et al. 2003), and the isochrone mass constraint (Marigo et al. 2008). The SME pipeline is explicitly said to be "a modification of some of [Valenti & Fischer 2005]."

3. **The broader motivation — using exoplanet-survey byproducts for binary/variability science — is already well-established and partly acknowledged.** The paper's own citations (Collier Cameron et al. 2007/2010 on SuperWASP variable stars/EBs; the statement that Kepler/Coughlin/Prša are uncovering thousands of EBs from transit surveys) show the "planet survey → EB sample" idea was already active. What the paper adds is specifically the *doppler+transit merge* for *masses* of *F/G+K/M* pairs, not the general concept.

4. **The mass-radius anomaly / magnetic inflation discussion** (Section 2) — that K/M dwarfs in short-period binaries have 10–20% inflated radii, plausibly magnetic — is a synthesis of prior work (López-Morales 2007, Torres 2010, Fabrycky et al. not cited, Gaudet; Chabrier 2007, Morales 2010), not an original result.

## Summary of Novelty Verdict

The paper is primarily a **methodology/case-study paper** rather than a discovery paper. Its genuine contribution is the *specific strategy* of merging Doppler- and transit-exoplanet-survey byproducts to mass/radius-calibrate F/G+K/M eclipsing binaries at low cost, plus a worked demonstration that this is feasible on two objects (one of which, TYC 1422, is itself a new triple-lined system) and a clean, reusable analysis workflow. The physics, equations, and every individual stellar-analysis technique it uses are all standard and well-cited. It is **not** a new physical result (no new EBs of special type, no new mass-radius finding, no new model), and the "exoplanet-survey byproduct" idea is not by itself new.

So: **modestly novel strategy, applied to a small (n=2) sample, with no independent physical discovery.** The case-study objects themselves (TYC 272, TYC 1422) are new EBs, but each is described as a *poor* benchmark (grazing eclipse in TYC 272; triply-blended in TYC 1422) and the precisions achieved (6% on the SB1 secondary mass; 3–4% radii on the SB2) are acceptable but not the headline. The work is sound, carefully executed, and useful as a proof-of-concept and pipeline, but the incremental nature of the strategy and the small/awkward sample limit its weight.

## [1105.2027v1-methodology] Methodology reproducibility


# Methodology Summary — "Eclipsing Binary Science Via the Merging of Transit and Doppler Exoplanet Survey Data" (Fleming et al. 2011, arXiv:1105.2027)

## 1. Overall goal and pipeline

The paper uses exoplanet survey data (Doppler RV + transit photometry), which incidentally catch large-amplitude binary stars, to characterize eclipsing binaries (EBs) without dedicated follow-up. The "central experiment" is a **cross-matching**: 24 single-lined spectroscopic binaries (SBs) with P < 20 d from the MARVELS Pilot Project (MPP, 2007, Keck ET/D-FDI on the SDSS 2.5-m; R ~ 5100) were visually overlaid on SuperWASP lightcurves (WASP band, 400–700 nm, ~49″ aperture; 1003 points in 10 nights for TYC 272, 649 in 12 nights for TYC 1422). Five had insufficient photometry, two blended with other stars; two EBs survived: TYC 0272-00458-1 (SB1) and TYC 1422-01328-1 (SB3).

## 2. Reproducing the central experiment (TYC 272 in detail)

**(a) RV orbit.** The 14 MPP epochs were scaled by a field "quality factor" Q = RMS(residuals)/MEDIAN(σ_X) computed over the other 58 stars in the same field (Q = 3.44; assumption that field objects are not RV-variable). Best fit from RVSIMF (Kane et al. 2007): P = 5.7282 d, K₁ ≈ 54.6 km/s, e = 0.001, χ²/dof = 2.62.

**(b) Primary stellar characterization** (five independent estimates of T_eff):
- SME spectral synthesis (Valenti & Fisher 2005; forward χ² grid → 100 L-B iterations), fixed line abundances, at ARCES resolution: T_eff = 5905±177 K, log g = 4.83±0.19, [Fe/H] = −0.28±0.12.
- Fe I/Fe II equivalent-width method (57 I + 3 II lines, 2σ clipping; Porto de Mello et al. 2008 / Gezzo et al. 2010) with an EW correction factor ≈ 1.1 to remove ~10% secondary flux: T_eff = 5720±79 K, [Fe/H] = −0.37±0.10.
- Hα profile fit (Lyra & Porto de Mello 2005): T_eff = 5461±118 K (noted as systematically cool).
- Infrared Flux Method (Casagrande et al. 2010) Monte Carlo sampling V, J, Ks with uniform [Fe/H] ∈ (−1, 0.5) and A_V ∈ (0, 0.075, from Schlegel dust maps): T_eff = 5459 (+233/−208) K.
- An HAO absolute photometry + 2MASS + GALEX SED fit with NextGen models fixed at T_eff = 5785 K, log g = 4.5, [Fe/H] = −0.3, free distance and extinction; it independently found a near-IR excess reproducible by a 4400 K, 0.7 R☉ companion → flux ratio F₂/F₁ ≈ 0.1.
- Luminosity class checked with the RPM-J diagram (proper motions from Tycho-2, NIR from 2MASS).
- Adopted: T_eff range = the Fe I EW 3σ interval, 5483 < T_eff < 5957 K.

**(c) Primary mass from isochrones.** Padova tracks at Z = 0.01 (M/H = −0.28), ages 100 Myr and ~9 Gyr, with the T_eff range as a horizontal bar → M₁ = 0.92 ± 0.1 M☉ (log g > 4.0 and 8.0 < log(age) < 9.95).

**(d) Light curve model.** jktbop (EPOP model) with per-night normalizations as free parameters; limb-darkening γ₁ = 0.6 (G band), γ₂ = 0.7 (K band), σ = 0.1 each; e cos ω and e sin ω constrained from the RV solution (without them best fit gives e cos ω = 0.2). Grazing secondary eclipse → flux ratio F₂/F₁ pinned to 8 ± 4% from the SED. Error propagation via "prayer-bead" bootstrap on the photometric residuals. Outputs in Table 4: R₁/a = 0.060, R₂/a = 0.036, i = 86.6°.

**(e) Mass and radii.** Mass function (Eq. 3) with M₁ fixed, a₁ from K₁P sin i (Eq. 4): M₂ = 0.610 ± 0.036 M☉. Radii R_j = (R_j/a)·a with a = a₁ + a₂: R₁ = 0.932 ± 0.076, R₂ = 0.559 ± 0.102 R☉. Uncertainties via implicit partial differentiation (Eq. 5), treating orbital parameters as independent.

**TYC 1422** (the clean demonstration case): MPP RVs were rejected (blended by a third star, RMS ~ 6 km/s); SMARTS (CTIO 1.5-m, R = 42000; 7 epochs, 10-min exposures, 2010) resolved the triple (Na D), giving individual RVs with an iterative 3σ-clip CCF (template = HD 108510); MPFVIFIT2 (periodogram + Levenberg–Marquardt, simultaneous two-component fit sharing parameters between the bound pair) yielded P = 6.2005 d, e = 0.16, q = 0.78, with a linear-drift term (−48 ± 18 m/s/d). Lightcurve fit with jktbop adding third light L₃ = 0.25 L₁; then direct SB2 mass/radii formulas (Eqs. 7, center of mass): M₁ = 1.163 ± 0.034 M☉, R₁ = 2.063 ± 0.058 R☉, M₂ = 0.905 ± 0.067 M☉, R₂ = 0.887 ± 0.037 R☉.

## 3. Assumptions the methodology papers over

1. **M₁ is not a direct measurement for SB1s.** The entire M₂, R₁, R₂ derivation for TYC 272 hangs on an isochrone mass (0.92 ± 0.1 M☉ ≈ 11%), whose validity requires: the system age to be one of the two Padova tracks bracketing it; metallicity Z = 0.01 exact; a single main-sequence star (no binarity among the components); no significant rotational/vrot corrections; standard Padova physics. This is the dominant error (5.9% on M₂ is "dominated by the large uncertainty in M₁").
2. **Adopted T_eff selection is circular.** They use the Fe I EW 3σ interval (5483–5957 K) as the "least biased," yet that EW estimate itself had the secondary flux correction applied (a factor of ~1.1) and the five methods disagree by up to ~3σ; Hα and IRFM are acknowledged as biased cool.
3. **Grazing-eclipse flux-ratio assumption.** F₂/F₁ = 8 ± 4% was chosen to "reproduce the observed SED well," not measured spectroscopically (only a <20% upper limit exists); the radius/depth solution inherits that modeling assumption, and the paper admits this is "a well-known problem" with radius-to-surface-brightness degeneracy.
4. **Limb-darkening choices.** γ = 0.6 (G) and 0.7 (K) were "decided" from tables with an arbitrary ±0.1 error; the WASP band is broad (400–700 nm) where linear law is a poor approximation.
5. **Error propagation assumptions.** Eqs. 3–6 assume all orbital parameters independent and drop cross terms; chromospheric jitter (tens of m/s, Santos et al. 2000) and RV systematic floors are folded into a single field Q factor assuming the majority of the 58 field stars are single/quiescent.
6. **Photometry.** Prayer-bead bootstrap assumes residuals are white noise (no red noise in the 10–12-epoch WASP series); extinction free in the SED fit; Schlegel A_V ceiling (0.075) assumed in IRFM; the IRFM result is stated as only a lower bound because of NIR flux fraction from the companion.
7. **Model atmosphere/abundance assumptions.** SME analysis fixes individual line abundances to those of Valentini & Fisher 2005; the EW method applies a uniform contaminant correction factor derived from assuming a specific companion type (mid-K).
8. **TYC 1422 specific:** the MPP data discarded as "systematic" — the third linear trend (−75 m/s/d) is unmodeled physically (no test whether it is stellar or instrumental); e = 0.16 for a 6.2-day orbit is inconsistent with the circularization expectation invoked for TYC 272 and is not discussed; the third-star T_eff is unconstrained and is needed for the disputed Na I EW/flux check; only 7 SMARTS epochs (1 dof) means the e, T_p, γ correlations are weakly constrained.
9. **Framework-level (Sec. 6):** the "framework" requires *both* primary and secondary eclipses to be measurable, excludes SB1s with grazing/vanishing secondary eclipses (like TYC 272, which is called a "poor benchmark"), assumes no third light or requires it be cleanly modeled, and presumes the RV survey cadence already samples the orbit fully — none of which is validated statistically across the 24-star sample (the two "successes" were selected by eye from 24 visually inspected lightcurves, so the pipeline's yield is an N = 2 anecdote, not a measured efficiency).
10. **Statistical robustness.** No formal goodness-of-fit for the isochrone mass (just "consistent within 1σ"), no MCMC joint fit of orbit + lightcurve + stellar parameters (they state packages exist but "analyzed separately"), no validation that the isochrone M₁ matches a dynamical value in either system (TYC 1422's SB2 masses are consistent with Padova but that is the only cross-check).
11. **Generalization.** The mass-radius science goal (M < 0.8 M☉ component, P > 5 d) is not actually met by either case study — both secondaries are ~0.6–0.9 M☉ G/K dwarfs with the *primary* the star of interest — so the motivating sample-size claim ("increase by factors of several") is asserted, not demonstrated.

## Scores

This is a solid, transparent applied astronomy paper but methodologically thin: the central result (EBs from cross-matching) is demonstrated on two visually selected systems, the SB1 mass/radius chain is dominated by an unvalidated isochrone prior, error treatment is approximate, and the proposed "framework" is a recipe list rather than a tested, reproducible pipeline.

## [1105.2027v1-logical-fallacies] Logical fallacies


I've read the full paper (Fleming et al. 2011, *Eclipsing Binary Science Via the Merging of Transit and Doppler Exoplanet Survey Data* — MARVELS Pilot Project + SuperWASP case study). Here is my reconstruction of its argument, followed by assessment.

## My understanding of the paper's logical argument

**Thesis.** Exoplanet surveys produce a large "waste stream" of binary stars. Those binaries are useful for ancillary stellar science (the mass–radius relation, testing evolution models), but they are under-used because each survey alone gives you only half the picture. The paper's central claim is that by *cross-referencing* two already-collected data sets — spectroscopic binaries (SBs) discovered by a Doppler/radial-velocity (RV) survey and eclipsing binaries (EBs) identified in the *photometry* of a transit survey — you can recover EBs with masses *and* radii for a small fraction of the cost of a dedicated follow-up program.

The argument proceeds through a complementarity analysis (Section 1):
- Start from an SB → you already have a precise orbit (period), so you can check *archival* photometry for eclipses instead of scheduling new observations (the eclipse a priori probability (R1+R2)/a is only a few–20%).
- Start from an EB → the favorable eclipse geometry is known, but high-precision RV follow-up to get the orbit is the expensive part, which the Doppler survey has already captured.

So merging gives both. Sections 2–3 set up the specific science driver: the mass–radius relation of K/M dwarfs (M < 0.8 M⊙), which model-vs-observation work (Torres 2010, Morales 2010) suggested can be inflated 10–20% by magnetic effects, and which needs a larger sample than the ~13 systems available (López-Morales 2007).

**The method has two branches (Section 6, the "framework"):**
- *SB2* (both RV amplitudes measurable → e.g. TYC 1422): masses and radii come *directly* from observables (RV semi-amplitudes + light-curve geometry) via the full mass function / Kepler's third law. No model mass needed.
- *SB1 / low-flux-ratio* (only one RV amplitude → e.g. TYC 272): the secondary mass and both radii need the *primary* mass. Because the secondary is too faint to Doppler in the survey spectra, M1 is constrained indirectly from stellar characterization (spectral synthesis, Fe I/II equivalent widths, Hα, IR flux method, SED, and a reduced proper-motion diagram), then propagated through the mass function to M2, R1, R2. TYC 272 demonstrates this multi-method primary-mass estimation; the authors acknowledge TODCOR on larger-aperture data would eventually make it direct.

**The case studies deliver:**
- **TYC 272**: a 5.73-day, circular, SB1 eclipsing binary. Five or six independent stellar-temperature/gravities methods plus an SED (revealing a NIR excess that *requires* a K-dwarf companion) pin the primary at M1 = 0.92 ± 0.1 M⊙, from which M2 = 0.610 ± 0.036, R1 = 0.932 ± 0.076, R2 = 0.559 ± 0.102 R⊙ — quoted as consistent with Padova models within 1σ. The grazing secondary eclipse causes a radius/flux degeneracy, handled by capping the flux ratio (<20%, V-band ratio 8 ± 4%).
- **TYC 1422**: a *triple* system. The MPP data is corrupted by blend/contamination (RMS ~6 km s⁻¹), so SMARTS high-resolution follow-up resolves the Na-D triplet; components 2 & 3 are in anti-phase (the eclipsing SB2), component 1 is a third source with a linear trend (attributed to drift). An improved orbital fit (De Lee's MPRVFT2) gives P = 6.2005 d, e = 0.16, and direct masses/radii: evolved primary M1 = 1.163 ± 0.034, R1 = 2.063 ± 0.058 R⊙; G-dwarf secondary M2 = 0.905 ± 0.067, R2 = 0.887 ± 0.037 R⊙ — relative errors of 2.8% and 4.2%, near the 3% precision goal.

Conclusion: the survey-merge pipeline is demonstrated end-to-end for both the "clean" SB2 and the "hard" SB1/triple cases, and packaged as a reusable framework (analysis-flow diagram, Fig. 14).

## Feedback

**Overall the paper is competent and internally consistent, but it reads as an application/pipeline demonstration more than a methodological breakthrough.**

**Strengths**
1. **The resource-efficiency framing is real and correctly argued.** The insight that a *known orbit* from the RV data lets you exploit the *archival* photometry — rather than the reverse (new RV follow-up) — is the genuinely useful part, and it's stated clearly. This is the paper's best idea.
2. **Careful, honest multi-method stellar characterization of the TYC 272 primary.** Running five–six independent probes (SME, EW, Hα, RPMD, IRFM, SED) and *discussing* their systematics — notably that the IRFM temperature is only a lower bound because of companion flux contamination, and that the NIR SED excess independently *requires* a cooler companion — shows the authors understand the degeneracies rather than averaging blindly.
3. **Explicit error budgeting** (Section 5.1.4) through the mass function, and honest reporting of limits (the weak, ±101° photometric ω; the abandoned MPP data for TYC 1422).
4. **TYC 1422 is a legitimate, fully *measured* SB2 result** with ~3% radius precision — a real (if small) science return, not just a claim.
5. Handling of the genuinely messy cases (blending, iodine-cell de-fringing, third light) models what a future large-scale pipeline will actually face.

**Weaknesses**
1. **Novelty is in the packaging, not the methods.** As the authors concede (p. 5), "many software packages exist to simultaneously model all observational data" (PRSL/Prša & Zwitter). The mass function, RV+lightcurve geometry, IRFM, SME, and TODCOR are all standard. The originality is the *merging concept and resource argument*, which is legitimate but does not rise to a new technique. This caps the impact.
2. **The yield doesn't back the ambition.** Section 2 argues the mass–radius problem needs "factors of several" more systems, yet the demonstrated return is **2 EBs from 24 candidates** (17 usable). The "we provide the framework for much larger data sets" claim is never quantified — no estimate of how many EBs this pipeline would recover from a full MARVELS or Kepler-like sample, no success-rate scaling. There is a real gap between the stated goal and the demonstrated output.
3. **The SB1 headline is partly circular for its stated purpose.** The whole point of Section 2 is to test model predictions, yet the M1 = 0.92 M⊙ used for TYC 272 comes *itself* from isochrones (Padova, an assumed 100 Myr–9 Gyr band, Z = 0.01, [M/H] ≈ −0.28). You then demonstrate "consistency with models." Using the models being tested to constrain the unknown mass weakens the benchmark claim. The authors flag this and point to TODCOR as the cure, but the *presented* numbers are therefore not model-independent.
4. **Age/metallicity sensitivity is under-quantified.** The primary mass rides on an assumed log(t) < 9.5 cut with log(g) > 4.0, not on an age measurement. That systematic isn't propagated beyond "the two tracks agree."
5. **Error-bar validation is thin.** The "prayer-bead" bootstrap captures only correlated *photometric* red noise, not RV systematics or parameter degeneracies; and TYC 1422's final orbit rests on just seven SMARTS epochs (one DOF before exploiting shared parameters). Sparse-N systematics could dominate the quoted ~3% precisions, and there is no independent cross-check (e.g. a full MCMC) of the uncertainties.
6. **The third component of TYC 1422 is handled somewhat hand-wavily.** Attributing a −75 m s⁻¹ day⁻¹ linear trend to "instrument drift" is asserted; a real outer companion would also trend, so the interpretation isn't firmly distinguished.

**Contextual note (impact).** Written for the 2011 survey era, the practical pipeline it proposes has largely been superseded by the dedicated EB surveys of Kepler/TESS, which carry their own targeted RVfollow-up (e.g. PRaDL, TESS-Keck, AOS). Its durable contribution is the *argument* and the two benchmarks, not the pipeline itself.

**Bottom line.** A sound, well-executed, honest case study that demonstrates a sensible and clearly-motivated methodology and delivers two new EBs (one a genuinely measured SB2). It loses points on (a) incremental rather than original methodology, (b) a demonstrated yield far smaller than its motivating case, and (c) the model-dependence of the SB1 mass that undercuts the very science driver the paper sets up. Competent and internally consistent, but not a step change.

## Audit

**Paper:** Fleming et al. (2011), "Eclipsing Binary Science Via the Merging of Transit and Doppler Exoplanet Survey Data" (arXiv:1105.2027v1), 40 pages, MARVELS Pilot Project + SuperWASP.

**Strongest quantitative claim:** The abstract and §5.2 claim that the two components of TYC 0272-00458-1 have "masses and radii consistent (1-σ) with model predictions" — presented as direct evidence that the data can test theoretical stellar evolution (the paper's stated broader motivation). Specifically: M₁ = 0.92 ± 0.1 M⊙ (assumed), M₂ = 0.610 ± 0.036 M⊙, R₁ = 0.932 ± 0.076 R⊙, R₂ = 0.559 ± 0.102 R⊙.

**Audit of the evidence:**

1. **Is the comparison fair? No — it's a one-sided consistency check, not a differential test.** Both components are compared only against Padova isochrones (Marigo et al. 2008) in overlaid mass–radius diagrams (Figs. 7, 13). There is no head-to-head comparison with competing models (Geneva, BaSTI, etc.), no goodness-of-fit statistic for the isochrone match, and no way the reader can tell whether the agreement would also hold (or fail) for alternatives. With R₂'s error of 18%, essentially any reasonable model family would pass this "test."

2. **Are baselines current? Yes, as of 2011.** Marigo et al. 2008 (Padova) was the current model set when this was written; SME (Valenti & Fischer 2005) and the RVSIM pipeline were standard. No staleness issue.

3. **Error bars / statistical significance?**
   - 1-σ bars are reported on all derived quantities, and the authors explicitly frame the claim as "consistent (1-σ)" — but "within 1σ" is *not* a significance test and is a weak consistency statement by design (it excludes ~68% nothing; any model within 1σ "passes").
   - The authors themselves concede the errors are inadequate for the intended use: "the relative errors … are larger than typically used to study the mass-radius relationship (∼ 6% for M₂, 8% and 18% for R₁ and R₂ respectively)" vs. the "a few percent" needed.
   - They also self-flag that RV fit residuals show "the formal uncertainties are underestimated" (χ²/dof = 2.62, 8 dof, scaled by Q; TYC 1422 fit χ²/dof = 4.135). Scaled uncertainties were used, but no systematic error floor was added to the mass/radius budgets, so the quoted 1-σ ranges likely understate the true scatter.
   - Critically for TYC 272, the whole result rests on an **adopted, not measured, primary mass** (M₁ = 0.92 ± 0.1 M⊙ from isochrone fitting, which is degenerate with age and metallicity). M₂, R₁, R₂ are all derived from that assumed value, and its systematic component is not fully propagated into the "consistency" claim.

**Verdict:** The comparison is not a fair model test in any strong sense: single-model, no significance statistic, self-admittedly oversized error bars (18% on the secondary radius), a possibly underestimated RV scatter, and a key input (primary mass) that is an assumption. The claim "consistent with model predictions" is defensible only as a null result for one 2011 model family. Note, however, that the paper is honestly scoped — its primary deliverable is a reusable cross-referencing *framework*, and the two case studies (24 binaries cross-referenced, 2 new EBs) are ancillary, with limitations openly acknowledged.

**Broader assessment:** Modestly original (systematic merging of transit + Doppler survey data for eclipsing-binary science was a genuinely useful idea in 2011, though the idea itself was motivated by prior work, e.g., Kane et al. 2009, and was soon superseded by Kepler's huge yield); solid but unremarkable methodology; limited lasting impact since the same niche became unnecessary once TESS/Kepler data made EBs abundant.

## [1105.2027v1-weak-point] Weakest link in the argument


# Weakest-Link Review: Fleming et al. (2011), "Eclipsing Binary Science Via the Merging of Transit and Doppler Exoplanet Survey Data" (1105.2027v1)

## The argument chain

1. Transit + Doppler surveys both produce large reservoirs of SBs/EBs; cross-referencing them is efficient (§1).
2. Cross-referencing 24 MPP SBs with SuperWASP yields two EBs, TYC 272 and TYC 1422, as case studies (§3–5).
3. From these, masses and radii are derived — directly for TYC 1422, and for TYC 272 via a model-constrained primary mass (§5.1.4).
4. Both systems are "consistent with model predictions," so the framework demonstrably yields precision EBs (M/R to a few percent) for studying the K/M-dwarf radius-inflation problem in the 0.3–0.8 M⊙ regime (§2, §7, Abstract).

## The single weakest link

**Step 3→4 for TYC 272:** inserting the isochrone-derived primary mass M1 = 0.92 ± 0.1 M⊙ into the mass function and flux-ratio constraint, then concluding that the "measured" secondary mass and both radii (M2 = 0.610 ± 0.036, R1 = 0.932 ± 0.076, R2 = 0.559 ± 0.102 R⊙) are "consistent with model predictions" and that this pipeline produces the precision data §2 says the mass–radius problem requires.

Exactly why the data do not entail this conclusion:

1. **The anchor isn't a measurement — it's the model under test.** M1 comes from Padova isochrones fit over an unconstrained age range of 10 Gyr (8.0 < log age < 9.95) and Teff estimates from four methods that span 5459–5905 K. That is the very class of stellar model the EB study exists to *independently* check (§2). The "dynamical" M2, R1, R2 are therefore isochrone outputs repackaged through Kepler's third law, so "both stars are consistent with models" (Abstract) is close to circular: it is partly a demonstration that the models reproduce their own inputs.
2. **The grazing-eclipse degeneracy is broken circularly.** §5.1.3 concedes the secondary eclipse is grazing (R2/R1 = 0.60 ± 0.12, a 20% uncertainty, Table 4) and that the required flux ratio (8 ± 4%) was *estimated from the model-derived M1 range and K1* — the same M1 that then flows through Eq. 3 into M2 and through a = a1 + a2 into R1 and R2. The dwarf's radius, i.e., the exact quantity relevant to the inflation problem, is the most model-dependent number in the paper.
3. **The precision bar set by the paper is missed where it matters.** §2 demands "masses and radii measured to precisions of a few percent" and at least one component at 0.3 < M < 0.8 M⊙. TYC 272 is the only case study in that regime, and it delivers 6 % (M2), 8 % (R1), **18 % (R2)** — the secondary radius error bar (±0.102 R⊙) is larger than the 10–20 % inflation under investigation, so it cannot falsify any model. A conclusion of model *consistency* drawn from such an error bar carries zero testable information. The paper itself admits "TYC 272 is not an ideal benchmark system" (§5.1.4, §6) and defers "a few percent" to future TODCOR/high-precision observations that are not in the paper.
4. **The precision is achieved outside the problem's parameter space.** TYC 1422 does reach 2.8–4.2 % on radii — but the eclipsing pair is an evolved 1.16 M⊙, 2.06 R⊙ primary and a 0.91 M⊙ G dwarf, i.e., above the 0.8 M⊙ inflation regime, and in a triple where a contaminating third component (L3 = 0.25 ± 0.08) degrades the photometry. Worse, the only result that meets the precision goal is a fit with 7 epochs, 14 RV points, and **χ²/dof = 4.14** (Table 8), meaning the quoted 2.8–2.9 % uncertainties are likely over-optimistic; the fit residuals argue against them.

So on a 2/24 (or 2/17) success sample, each case study fails one of the two requirements in an orthogonal way: the in-range system lacks the precision and is model-anchored; the in-precision system is out of range and contaminated. The extrapolation to "a rich source of EBs" for mass–radius science of F/G + K/M pairs is the step where the conclusion outruns the data. Fixable with the promised K2 measurements (TODCOR) and deeper secondary-eclipse photometry — but those are precisely what the conclusion currently rests on as promise rather than evidence.

## Paper at a glance

Fleming et al. (2011, arXiv:1105.2027) is an observational case study: they cross-reference 24 spectroscopic binaries from the MARVELS Pilot Project (MPP, Keck/ET, 2007) with SuperWASP photometry, and characterize two newly discovered eclipsing binaries — TYC 272 (single-lined EB: a G primary + K M-dwarf secondary) and TYC 1422 (triple: an evolved primary + eclipsing G-dwarf pair). The headline deliverable is masses/radii for both systems, plus a flow-diagram "framework" for doing this to all survey EBs ("§6"). It is an early-2011 paper, so I judge it against the norms of that era but name gaps that would block an independent re-implementation today.

## What is adequately specified

The paper is unusually transparent for its year in *method narrative*:

- **Named, citable analysis tools**: RVSIMFIT (Kane et al. 2007) for RV fitting; `jktebop` (Popper & Etzel 1981 EOP model; link to the public Keele mirror) for lightcurves, with concrete settings given — limb-darkening coefficients (u=0.6/0.7 for 272, 0.55/0.55 for 1422, ±0.11 uncertainty), per-night normalizations added as free parameters, and the "prayer-bead" bootstrap for correlated photometric noise.
- **Named data sources**: MPP/Keck ET RVs (R~5100, 495–585 nm, quality factors Q=3.44 and 3.07 per Table 1), WASP photometry (1003 points/10 nights and 649/12 nights), their own HAO SDSS-band photometry, SMARTS R~42000 (7 epochs), APO/ARCES R~31500 (2 exposures each, with SNR~105/75 given).
- **Named models**: Padova isochrones (Marigo et al. 2008) at log(t)=8.0/9.77, Z=0.01/0.0019, and NextGen/HAUSCHILD (1999) model spectra for the SED; the "bplot" IRAF EW task after 2σ clipping of 57 Fe I + 3 Fe II lines.
- **Key numbers in the body**: T_eff determinations by four methods and their agreement, the flux-ratio constraint 8±4%, the M1=0.92±0.1 M⊙ adopted mass constraining the SB1 solution, and the explicit mass/radius error-propagation equations (3)–(6), which are themselves re-implementable.
- **Tables of measured RVs** (Tables 4, 5, 7) so the orbital fits at least can be refit from published data.
- **The flow diagram (Fig. 14)** that is the paper's stated contribution, making the pipeline steps explicit.

This is a real, well-written analysis, but as a reproducibility artifact it is a *recipe*, not *reproducible results*.

## What is missing (reprovision blocks for independent re-implementation)

Ordered roughly by how badly they block a from-scratch re-derivation:

1. **No published raw data for the two key lightcurves.** The WASP points are said to be "extracted from the WASP archive," but the exact per-night reduced mags, aperture choices, filtering/quality criteria, and the full lightcurve tables are not given (only plots in Figs. 5/12). The HAO absolute photometry is fully theirs and only summarized (Table 3 SED inputs). An independent team would have to redo the full photometric reduction from raw frames and get different numbers. No data DOI or archive is cited.
2. **No code release at all.** For 2011 this was the norm, but it remains the single biggest obstacle:
   - **MPRVFFIT2**, the IDL procedure fit to the SMARTS RVs of the triple, is explicitly "written by N. De Lee" with **no public availability noted anywhere** (p. 15). Its non-standard 7-parameter constrained fit (identical *P, e, T_p, γ*, anti-phase ω, linear term) cannot be reproduced without it or its exact implementation.
   - Their **SME spectral-synthesis implementation** is described only as "we follow the exact procedure in Valenti & Fischer (2005) but include some changes… a forward-modeling grid of parameter space … 100 different initial parameter sets" (p. 9). **The grid ranges, the "changes" from VF05, and the line/continuum setup beyond "same as VF05" are unspecified**, so T_eff=5905±177 K, log g=4.83±0.19, [Fe/H]=−0.28±0.12 cannot be independently reproduced.
   - The **Monte-Carlo IRFM** (p. 10, sampling [Fe/H]∈[−1.0,0.5], A_V∈[0,0.075], T_eff=(V−Ks)) has no code and no release of the resulting distributions.
   - **Their own photometric reduction (HAO)** and the **APO/ARCES "IRAF script"** (p. 7–8) are not released.
   - The **MPP/DFFI de-fringing pipeline** is deferred to Fleming et al. (2010) and the iodine-cell limitation is explained qualitatively (p. 14–15) but the pipeline itself (which stores fringe-fit information to reconstruct a "traditional" de-frired spectrum) is not described or released in enough detail to rebuild.
3. **No random seeds / stochastic reproducibility.** Two Monte-Carlo procedures are central (the IRFM T_eff sampling, the prayer-bead lightcurve error bootstrap, and "100 different initial parameter values" for SME). No seeds, no code; the *median of the asymmetric* IRFM distribution is reported as the central value but not its width, so a re-run could easily yield a shifted T_eff and propagate into M1.
4. **No version pinning.** No software versions, no git/hash, no Python/IDL environment, no model-atmosphere file versions (NextGen vs. updated grids), no exact isochrone file versions (the "2008" Marigo set has many revisions), no IRAF version. The `jktebop` mirror link is a 2011-era URL with no version tag.
5. **Numerical details only in figure captions or prose:** the exact initial-grid values for LC fitting, the specific Fe I/Fe II line list ("57 Fe I and 3 Fe II lines" — which ones, from which library?), and the *exact* "3-σ confidence interval (5483 < T_eff < 5957 K)" used to bound the M1 tracks are reported in body text but the underlying distribution data are in no table. An independent team must guess at the clipping and line-selection to match the EW T_eff=5720±79 K.
6. **MPP RV data provenance.** Table 4 gives the 14 TYC 272 RVs and Table 5 the 16 TYC 1422 RVs, which is good — but the scaling quality-factor Q (Eq. 2) is defined in terms of "the other 58 objects in the same field of view"; the field definition and the RMS of those 58 are not tabulated, so Q can't be independently recomputed.
7. **Compute / provenance.** The paper mentions Vanderbilt's ACCRE parallel facility for the SME grid but gives no node count, run count, or wall-time; the "100 initial parameter values" is the only quantification. The APO 3.5m and SMARTS 1.5m exposure scripts (flat sets, ThAr calibration choice) are described at an operational level but not released as scripts.
8. **Third light (L3) in TYC 1422.** The lightcurve fit adds L3 as a free parameter (Table 9), but the starting range, its correlation with the EB solution, and how its value feeds into the radii are not quantified beyond the table entry.

## What would unblock re-implementation (minimum bar)

- Release the reduced WASP and HAO lightcurves (per-point photometry, filter, SNR flags, and the exact jktebop input files).
- Release MPRVFFIT2 (or a modern equivalent) with a worked fit on the Table 7 RVs.
- Release the modified-SME code with the explicit grid bounds used, and publish the line list (57 Fe I + 3 Fe II) with the continuum window definitions.
- Release the Monte-Carlo IRFM script with seeds, *and* report the mean/median/**σ** (not just median) of the T_eff distribution.
- Publish the prayer-bead bootstrap script and seed.
- Publish the full MPP RV list (all 708 stars) with the field-grouping used for Q, or at least the TYC 272 and TYC 1422 field members.
- Pin software versions (IRAF, RVSIMFIT, jktebop, the ARCS pipeline, the isochrone file hash).

## Verdict

The paper is honest, internally consistent, and unusually detailed in prose about *which* methods were used. But it is not reproducible in the modern sense: no code, no data tables for the two key lightcurves, no seeds, no version pinning, and three named-but-unreleased software pieces (MPRVFFIT2, modified-SME, MC-IRFM) that are each load-bearing for a headline number. An independent group with the same archive access and a willingness to re-derive the SME and IRFM pieces would probably land on *broadly consistent* masses and radii (the four T_eff methods agree within ~1σ, and the mass errors are large enough that small T_eff shifts don't flip the physics), but they could not **reproduce** the stated values.

This is a "re-derivable with substantial effort and guesswork, but not independently re-creatable from the paper alone" paper — a fairly typical 2011 observational result. I rate it mid-range: the *science* is sound and the *prose* is transparent, but the *artifact* is far from complete for modern reproducibility standards.

# Critique of Fleming et al. 2011 (arXiv:1105.2027v1)

## Writing and framing: overclaims beyond the evidence

The paper's central promise is that cross-referencing Doppler exoplanet SBs with transit-survey photometry makes eclipsing-binary (EB) science cheap, precise, and generically reproducible from survey data alone. Several specific sentences outrun what the two case studies actually demonstrate.

**Title and abstract — scale vs. substance.**
- Title, *"Eclipsing Binary Science Via the Merging of Transit and Doppler Exoplanet Survey Data,"* is a universal claim built on a cross-reference of 24 SBs, of which 7 are unusable (5 with too little photometry, 2 blended; p. 5), leaving 17 that yield 2 EBs. The honest denominator is 2 of 17 usable (~12 %), not 2 of 24 — and the 12 % is essentially the a‑priori eclipse probability the authors themselves quote a page earlier (p. 2, "a few percent up to ~25 %"), so the method buys almost nothing over the geometric prior.
- Abstract: *"we use as case studies to develop a general approach to eclipsing binaries in survey data"* and *"We provide the framework necessary to apply this analysis to much larger datasets."* The framework is *stated*, not *validated at scale*. Two objects — one SB1 that needs isochrone-anchored masses, one triple whose data required a 1.5 m follow-up because the MPP instrument put light through an iodine cell (pp. 13–14) — do not constitute a "general approach"; they are a demonstration of the recipe at its two hardest limits, not its easy core.
- Abstract: *"both stars have masses and radii consistent with model predictions."* For a paper whose motivation (Sec. 2) is precisely that K/M dwarf radii deviate 10–20 % from theory, "consistent at 1 σ" given 6–18 % uncertainties (p. 14) is a very weak test, and the anchor on the M1 of TYC 272 is *itself taken from the same isochrones* (p. 11), so the "consistency" is semi-circular.

**Section 1 (Introduction).**
- *"The amplitudes of the photometric or Doppler variability from such objects are often several orders of magnitude larger than exoplanet signals, making them comparatively simpler to identify and characterize."* The "identify" half is right; the "characterize" half is contradicted by the paper's own §5.1, where deriving (M1, M2, R1, R2) for a single SB1 takes a five-method T_eff campaign whose values span ~450 K, an isochrone constraint, an IRFM Monte Carlo, an SEM fit, and a Hα fit that is acknowledged as biased cool (p. 10).
- *"Cross-referencing … can minimize the amount of follow-up data required."* "Minimize" is a strong word, and the paper's own TYC 1422 refutes it: the MPP RVs were dropped entirely because of the iodine cell (p. 14: *"We do not use the MPP data in our analysis because it is so heavily affected by systematic errors"*), and the final masses/radii rest on SMARTS data taken years later. The paper's most successful case is also its most refutational case for the efficiency claim, and the text does not say this plainly.

**Section 2 (Motivation).**
- *"In order to place constraints on the interaction of magnetic fields and the stellar radius, a large sample of EBs with masses and radii measured to precisions of a few percent is required."* Fine as a goal; but the paper immediately (p. 14) reports "the relative errors for the masses and radii in the specific case of TYC 272 are larger than typically used to study the mass-radius relationship (~6 % for M2, 8 % and 18 % for R1 and R2, respectively)." The paper's own SB1 case study therefore does *not* meet the stated precision floor, and the "few percent" goal is silently relaxed to "1-σ consistent."
- *"The Kepler mission is uncovering thousands of EBs with exquisite photometric precision (Coughlin et al. 2010; Prša et al. 2010)."* This sentence is not paid off: no Kepler EB appears in the 24-object sample, and the paper's whole point about ground-based Doppler follow-up does not apply to Kepler anyway. It reads as motivation pasted in rather than as part of the argument.

**Section 5.1 (TYC 272).**
- End of §5.1.1: *"making this EB an excellent system to further study in the context of the mass-radius relationship."* "Excellent" is asserted before the rest of the section shows that R1 carries an 18 % error, that four independent T_eff determinations disagree by ~450 K, and that the flux-ratio correction is "~10%" (p. 9), which the authors immediately wave away as "small and well within the internal errors."
- End of §5.1.2: *"We therefore conclude that a flux ratio … of ~10% … would not significantly affect our measured values based on the EW method."* A 10 % companion-flux correction to the EW-based T_eff, log(g) and [Fe/H] is not "small" in the same spirit as the "few percent precision" the paper demands of its output radii; the sentence is doing rhetorical work.
- p. 11: *"all four T_eff are consistent to within 1-σ … Based on this T_eff range, we find the Padova models constrain M1 = 0.92 ± 0.1 M☉ …"* The "T_eff range" they adopt is the EW method's *3-σ* interval (5483 < T_eff < 5957 K), which is what swallows the Hα (5461 K) and IRFM (5459 K) values that fall *outside* the SME/EW 1-σ bands. Quoting 3-σ for the spread rather than 1-σ to declare the four methods "consistent" is the sort of framing a reviewer will flag.

**Section 5.2 (TYC 1422).**
- p. 16: *"The primary star is evolved onto the main sequence [sic] and has a mass of M1 = 1.163 ± 0.034 M☉ …"* This sentence contains a typo ("evolved onto" → presumably "has evolved off the main sequence or is on the main sequence"), but more substantively: the identification of the system as a triple rests on the triple Na D pattern in a *single* ARCES order (Fig. 8), and the third star's T_eff is admittedly unconstrained (p. 16). Precise masses and radii are quoted for the eclipsing pair while the third light is an uncharacterized flux term, which softens the "triple-component system" claim in the abstract.
- p. 17: *"Relative uncertainties for R1 and R2 are 2.8 % and 4.2 %, very close to the desired precision goal of < 3 %."* 4.2 % is not "very close to < 3 %"; it is missed, and the phrasing papers over the shortfall.

**Section 6 (Framework) and Conclusion.**
- Sec. 6: *"This framework applies only to those EBs that have both a measurable primary and secondary eclipse."* The paper's most important caveat is buried as an offhand restriction in the methods section; a well-framed paper would put this in the abstract and conclusion.
- Sec. 6 / Conclusion: *"We have shown that the combination of survey data can be a rich source of EBs, for which many physical parameters can be directly measured or constrained from the survey data itself."* For TYC 272, M1 is not directly measured — it is isochrone-constrained. For TYC 1422, the MPP survey data was *not* usable for the final masses (dropped over systematic errors, p. 14). "Directly" is doing significant work in both halves.
- Conclusion: *"We have found two new EBs around F/G primaries … ideal benchmark systems for studying the mass-radius relationship."* "Ideal" is a strong word for a pair where one is a grazing EB requiring model flux ratios to break the R1–I_degeneracy (p. 12: *"a well-known problem for grazing EBs"*), and the other is a triple with an unconstrained third component. "Promising" would be honest.

A summary of the recurring framing problem: the paper is structured around the word "general" — general approach (abstract), general framework (Sec. 6), general framework in the title — but the evidence is two systems that together sit at the extremes of what the method can do (hard SB1 + hard triple), not its sweet spot. A reviewer will note this.

# Peer Review: "Eclipsing Binary Science Via the Merging of Transit and Doppler Exoplanet Survey Data — A Case Study With the MARVELS Pilot Project and SuperWASP" (Fleming et al., 2011; arXiv:1105.2027)

## 1. Summary of the paper

The paper cross-references 24 spectroscopic binaries (SBs, P < 20 d) from the MARVELS Pilot Project (MPP, an SDSS 2.5-m / Keck ET Doppler pilot survey) with SuperWASP wide-field photometry to identify eclipsing binaries (EBs). From 17 SBs with usable photometry, the authors identify two: **TYC 272** (TYC 0272-00458-1), a single-lined SB (SB1) around a G-type primary with a K/M dwarf secondary, and **TYC 1422** (TYC 1422-01328-1), a triple-lined SB containing an evolved primary plus an eclipsing G+G pair. For TYC 272 they constrain the primary mass via Padova evolutionary isochrones (M₁ = 0.92 ± 0.1 M☉), then derive the secondary mass (0.610 ± 0.036 M☉) and both radii (0.932 ± 0.076, 0.559 ± 0.102 R☉) using the mass function and light-curve geometry. For TYC 1422, both EB components have Doppler measurements, so the masses and radii follow directly (M₁ = 1.163 ± 0.034, M₂ = 0.905 ± 0.067 M☉; R₁ = 2.063 ± 0.058, R₂ = 0.887 ± 0.037 R☉). The broader message is that combining transit (photometric) and Doppler survey archives can harvest EB science — in particular the mass–radius relationship — cheaply, and a general "analysis flow diagram" is proposed to scale this to larger samples.

## 2. Strengths

- **Well-motivated and timely.** The rationale — using the epoch certainty of long-baseline photometric surveys to confirm eclipses predicted by short-baseline RV surveys, minimizing null detections of photometric follow-up, is sound and an efficient use of already-acquired data. The motivation for F/G + K/M systems to constrain the K/M-dwarf radius inflation is clear and the connection to the magnetic/tide literature is laid out properly.
- **Genuinely independent, multi-probe mass determination for the SB1 primary.** For TYC 272 the authors do not rely on a single mass estimate: they use (a) spectral synthesis (SME), (b) Fe I/II excitation/ionization-equilibrium EW analysis, (c) Hα core, (d) the reduced-proper-motion (RPM) diagram for luminosity class, (e) the Monte Carlo IRFM, (f) a full SED construction, and (g) Padova isochrones. The agreement within ~1σ (T_eff ≈ 5700–5785 K) is reassuring, and the flux-ratio correction in the EW analysis is a thoughtful touch.
- **Rigorous handling of correlated photometric errors.** The "prayer-bead" bootstrap Monte Carlo to account for red/noise in the light curve (a standard but often-skipped technique) is applied consistently, as are explicit propagation-of-error derivations for the masses and radii (Eqs. 5–6).
- **The triple system (TYC 1422).** Discovering and characterizing a triple with all three spectra resolved, noting the linear drift in Component 1 (−48 ± 18 m s⁻¹ d⁻¹) as possible residual instrument drift, and honestly flagging that it is *not* a good benchmark because of the third flux component, shows good judgment and transparency.
- **Honest error budgeting.** The 5.9% error on M₂ for TYC 272 is explicitly attributed to the 1-σ uncertainty on M₁, and the grazing secondary eclipse is acknowledged as a source of degeneracy.

## 3. Weaknesses and concerns

- **Limited statistical significance; the two systems are not ideal benchmarks.** The central goal — a sample large enough to study the K/M mass–radius relationship over a range of periods (a "wide range of orbital periods to sample a range of magnetic field strengths") — is *not* achieved. Two systems, both short-period, cannot test any period/magnetic-field trend. The authors concede this (e.g., "neither of these two specific binaries are ideal benchmark systems"); the paper is essentially a proof of concept. For a "prestigious" venue this is the biggest weakness.
- **Sample selection and false-alarm control are thin.** Of 24 SBs, 5 have too few photometric epochs, 2 are blended, leaving 17 examined "visually." There is no formal screening statistic, no false-alarm-rate estimate, and no statement of how complete/representative the sample is or whether additional EBs were missed in the 17. Relying on visual inspection for discovery, with 24 objects, leaves the reader uneasy about completeness.
- **Primary-mass uncertainty is the dominant, somewhat arbitrary term.** For TYC 272, M₁ = 0.92 ± 0.1 M☉ (≈11%) drives most of the error in M₂. The adopted T_eff range is the EW 3σ interval, and the ±0.1 on M₁ comes from an isochrone scan. The authors don't fully explore the *model-systematics* on the isochrones (age, opacity, convection) beyond noting metallicity is a minor effect — an important caveat for a paper whose stated aim is to test stellar-evolution models.
- **The RV systematics treatment.** Q-factors of 3.3–3.4 for both fields mean the formal RV errors are nearly quadrupled. This is a red flag that the MPP pipeline has substantial unmodeled systematics; it is handled by scaling, but the underlying cause is not diagnosed. For an MPP data set this is perhaps expected, but it degrades the "survey data alone can yield high precision" claim.
- **TYC 1422 required follow-up.** Only 7 SMARTS epochs (one degree of freedom for a full Keplerian fit) were used for the improved orbit. The claim that the MARVELS instrument (no iodine cell) can do this "without additional follow-up" is forward-looking and not demonstrated here; the demonstrated result rests on the CTIO 1.5-m follow-up. ## Overall impression

This is a competent, honest observational case study. Fleming et al. cross-reference 24 spectroscopic binaries from the MARVELS Pilot Project (Doppler) with SuperWASP transit photometry, identify two eclipsing binaries, and walk through the full derivation of component masses and radii for each — one an SB1 (TYC 272), the other a triple-lined SB2 (TYC 1422). That tension is the main thing to fix conceptually.

## Specific feedback

1. **Precision falls short of the stated goal, and you should say so more plainly.** Section 2 and the conclusion frame these as benchmark systems for the mass–radius relationship, but the target is "masses and radii to precisions of a few percent (§2)." For TYC 272 you get ~6% on M₂, 8% and 18% on R₁/R₂. For TYC 1422 the masses are 2.9% and 7.4% and radii 2.8%/4.2% — that one is closer to goal, undermined by the third spectrum and low SNR. The paper is honest in §5.1.4 that the errors are "larger than typically used," but I'd pull the "benchmark" framing forward into the abstract/intro, or scale it back, so a reader isn't led to expect 3%-level calibrations and then find 18% radii.

2. **TYC 272's M₂ is really just an M₁ problem.** You're transparent that M₂'s 5.9% error is dominated by the M₁ = 0.92 ± 0.1 M☉ assumption. The good news is you bracket M₁ with four largely independent methods (SME, Fe-I EW, Hα profile, IRFM) plus the isochrone/color approach, and they agree to within ~1σ — that's genuinely good practice and worth emphasizing more prominently, since it's the backbone of the result. The "independence" claim deserves one sentence on the shared assumptions (extinction, flux-ratio correction) that actually do leak between methods.

3. **TYC 1422's orbital solution is essentially under-determined.** Seven epochs, parameters {P, e, ω, T_p, K₁, K₂, γ} reduced to six by the shared-eccentricity/ω+π constraint — so you have ~one degree of freedom before the linear trend. Calling this a "best-fit" over-states it; it's closer to an interpolation with the FAP giving a false sense of statistical security. The FAP derivation (Baluev 2008 analytical) is the right call for such a small N, but please acknowledge that with so little redundancy the "slightly eccentric, e = 0.16" claim has weak statistical grounding, and that the linear trend (−48 ± 18 m s⁻¹ d⁻¹) being attributed to drift is an assumption, not a measurement.

4. **The "framework" (§6) is a workflow, not a validated method.** It's applied to exactly two systems, one of which (TYC 1422) you yourself flag as a poor benchmark (third spectrum, iodine cell, blending). So the general claim that "many EBs from surveys can yield masses+radial" rests on one clean SB1. That's fine as a proof of concept, but the figure-11 flowchart should be honestly labeled as a *proposed* pipeline whose general yield/precision hasn't yet been demonstrated at scale.

5. **Error-propagation assumptions (Eq. 5).** You drop cross terms assuming M₁ and C are independent, and "P, K₁, e, i all independent." e comes jointly from the RV fit and the lightcurv's e·cosω, and i/R come from the same lightcurve that supplies the flux ratio — these correlations aren't fully independent. This is a standard approximation and defensible, but a sentence acknowledging that the quoted σ's are likely mildly optimistic (rather than conservative) would strengthen the rigor. For the radius errors specifically, the limb-darkening ±0.1 and the grazing-secondary-eclipse degeneracy (which you correctly note for TYC 272) are worth folding into the discussion of whether R₂ is really at the quoted precision.

## Minor points
- log(g) = 4.83 ± 0.19 is "larger than expected" per §5.1.2 — this directly feeds the isochrone age/mass, so its tension with the EW-method 4.20 and the Hα-based picture deserves one more line on how much it pulls M₁.
- The Hα T_eff is known to be systematically cooler (contamination from K-dwarf wings); you handle this sensibly but it's worth being explicit that it's excluded from the adopted T_eff range for that reason.
- The quality-factor scaling (Q = 3.44/3.07) inflating RV errors is reasonable but field-dependent; a brief note that this is the dominant systematic for the MPP data would help.

## Verdict

Solid, methodical work done carefully and reported honestly. Its real value is the *methodology and pipeline* for extracting masses/radii from low-flux-ratio SB1s in survey data and the demonstration that Doppler+photometry cross-referencing yields new EBs cheaply. Its limitation is scale and precision: two systems, one of which is a weak benchmark, and precision below the paper's own stated standard. As a foundations/pilot paper it works; as a claim about deliverable benchmark mass–radius data, it doesn't quite yet hold up. Tighten the "benchmark" framing, be more candid about the TYC 1422 degrees of freedom, and the paper reads much more credibly.
