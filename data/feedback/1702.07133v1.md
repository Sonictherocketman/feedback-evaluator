## [1702.07133v1-general-review] General Review


I've read the full text (10-page arXiv manuscript, 23 Feb 2017) and independently re-verified its arithmetic (α_V consistency, XBU decomposition, geometry from the printed equations, DFT lattice errors, dispersion-coefficient ratios, and the synthesis stoichiometry). Below is a general review of the argument, with specific, checkable points for the authors to fix.

## Summary of the argument

The paper prepares Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] (first structural report), shows by Rietveld refinement of synchrotron PXD (100–598 K) that it is isostructural with H₃[Co(CN)₆)] and the "colossal NTE" material Ag₃[Co(CN)₆], with αa = 25.4(5) MK⁻¹ and αc = −43.5(8) MK⁻¹ (α_V = 7.4 MK⁻¹ — small, because the negative strut expansivity nearly cancels the wine-rack flexing). The central claim: DFT+MBD/TS and a minimal GULP lattice-dynamical model show Cu…Cu "cuprophilic" interactions are ~30–40% weaker than Ag…Ag, yet the *stronger*-interaction Ag compound is the more compliant one. The explanation: the metallophilic attraction acts in tension with electrostatic repulsion, flattening the effective potential; flexibility comes from *competing* interactions of comparable strength, not from weak bonding alone.

## What works

1. **The measurements are the solid core.** Broad, near-linear 100–598 K range, two-phase Pawley/Rietveld treatment of the KCu[Co(CN)₆] impurity, and honest reporting of the degraded 450–598 K regime (C/N coordinates and B_iso covarying as the impurity decomposes). The internal consistency checks I did pass: 2αa + αc reproduces α_V within stated error for all three systems (H: 15.2 vs 15.1; Cu: 7.3 vs 7.4; Ag: 169.7 vs 169.8), and the XBU decomposition satisfies αa = α′a + α_r, αc = α′c + α_r up to rounding.
2. **An internally consistent multi-scale narrative.** The same qualitative ordering (Ag > Cu > H in metallophilic strength, Ag > Cu > H in compliance) obtained from (i) in-solid TS dispersion coefficients, (ii) a 3-parameter GULP model, and (iii) 0 K DFT compressibilities, with Fig. 8 tying them to the experimental expansivities, is persuasive even where each individual element could be questioned.
3. **The design principle stated in the conclusion is genuinely useful**: for these frameworks the thermomechanical response is set by the *balance* of vdW and electrostatic terms (Fig. 9), so stronger dispersion can increase compliance. That is a non-trivial reframing of the usual "weak interactions → responsive materials" heuristic, and the paper is careful to attribute the Ag extremity to this balance (Ref. 59) rather than asserting it from C6 alone.

## Specific problems

1. **Printed Eqs. (2) and (3) are inconsistent with the crystal structure and with the paper's own numbers.** Taking them literally (r² = a²/2 + c²/4; tan θ = c/a) gives, at 300 K, r = 5.93 Å and θ ≈ 88°. But the lattice-dynamical model's r₀ for Cu (sum of four crystallographic bond lengths, d(Co–C)+d(C–N)+d(N–A)) is 4.867 Å, and with a = 6.9085 the geometry requires a ≈ r√2·(1+tan²(θ/2))^{1/2}, i.e. r ≈ 4.87 and θ ≈ 90°. The correct relations for a = r√(2+tan²(θ/2)) give ∂ln a/∂θ = −tan(θ/2)t sec²(θ/2)/(2+tan²(θ/2)) ≈ −0.239 (per unit θ) at θ = 90°, and ∂ln c/∂θ = −0.079; that is, if α_θ were dθ/dT, the pure-flexing expansivities would be α′a ≈ −10 MK⁻¹ … wait, sign convention aside, the *ratio* |∂ln a/∂θ| : |∂ln c/∂θ| ≈ 3:1, whereas the quoted α′a = 33.5 and α′c = −35.7 imply a ratio ≈ 1:1, and — the decisive point — 2α′a + α′c = 31.3 MK⁻¹. A rigid-strut wine-rack gives *zero* volume change from flexing alone (struts keep constant length); a volume expansivity of 31 MK⁻¹ attributed purely to the angle implies the printed r/θ definitions are not the ones actually used. The decomposition as printed is not reproducible by any combination of Eqs. (2)–(3) with α_θ = 43.1. The authors must (a) fix the equations and (b) show the linear algebra linking αa, αc, α′a, α′c, α_r, α_θ.
2. **The DFT errors are worse than presented.** Table 3 gives "∆ = 13.4%" for PBE+TS and "13.2%" for PBE+MBD, but the column-by-column values are +4.01/−5.37% (TS) and −5.25/+2.66% (MBD); the sum of absolute strains is 9.4% and 7.9% respectively, and the sum of absolute *percentages* is 9.9% and 7.9%. Neither "13.4" nor "13.2" is obtainable as written, so the headline that dispersive DFT reproduces the 0 K cell within ~13% is not supported by its own table. Also, MB-D *over*corrects (a compressed by 5.3%), so the claim that dispersion brings the cell "closer to experiment" is directionally true only for c. In Table 1 vs Table 3 the 0 K volume is 276.66 vs 276.62 — cosmetic, but check the rounding.
3. **Inconsistent magnitude statements for the Cu/Ag interaction strength.** Section 3.3 quotes "larger by ∼40% for Ag relative to Cu" (i.e. ratio 70%), Section 3.4/3.5 says "weaker … by 30–40%", and the conclusion again says 30–40%, while the actual DFT ratio is 70% and the GULP ratio 61% — the "30–40%" range is not the same number in both methods, and "∼40% weaker" vs "Ag larger by ∼40%" can't both be right at once. Also, using *effective C6 at a fixed geometry* as "a measure of the strength of metallophilic interactions" conflates the C6 coefficient (the far-field prefactor) with the binding contribution; two r⁻⁶ wells with different C6 and different C12 (the Buckingham/repulsion balance in Fig. 9's argument) can have the same energy scale but different curvature. The GULP model does capture the curvature argument (r⁻⁶ + fixed electrostatic core), which is the right way to think about it, but the DFT side should at least fit a full well (C6 and C12, or the well depth and effective force constant) rather than quoting C6 alone.
4. **The Grüneisen proportionality (Eq. 8) carries more assumption than the footnote concedes.** The argument that C_Tγ̂/V varies by < 25% between Cu and Ag rests on (i) γ̂ being system-independent — but γ̂ is exactly the parameter that encodes how anharmonicity distributes into specific modes, and the shallow, vdW-dominated c-axis phonon that produces NTE is where γ̂ is expected to *differ most* between 3d and 4d metals (differently shaped C6/R0 wells give different force-constant gradients); (ii) C_T scaling as √(m/A) from "heavy-atom-dominated low-energy phonons" — a 15:1 mass difference between Cu and Co vs 108:1 for Ag means the phonon spectra are genuinely different animals; and (iii) neglecting that Ag's αa = 145.9 MK⁻¹ is so extreme that quasi-harmonic theory itself is marginal there. The conclusion is reasonable, but the quantitative step from 0 K compressibilities to the expansivity ratio would be far stronger with quasi-harmonic lattice-dynamical calculations (free energies → α_i(T), K_i(T) directly). The absence of any anharmonic phonon calculation is the main gap in an otherwise clean argument, and the paper acknowledges it only as future work ("a key challenge… is the treatment of finite-temperature effects").
5. **No elastic/compressibility data at all, despite the material's most interesting predicted property.** The authors note that α_V ≈ 7 MK⁻¹ (near-zero volume response) together with large anisotropic linear expansivities makes negative linear compressibility along c "a fruitful avenue of future research." Since the entire thesis is that these frameworks are *tension-tuned*, a single high-pressure or ultrasound measurement on Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] — precisely the experiment that distinguishes "flexing with Kr > 0" from the "α_V small ⇒ huge uniaxial compliance" trap they themselves flag — would have converted a prediction into the paper's third pillar of evidence. At minimum, reporting the calculated 0 K single-crystal compliance tensor for the Cu and Ag members from the ab initio PES (already mapped in Fig. 5) would strengthen the comparison against Fig. 8's uniaxial bars.
6. **Structural determination caveats worth expanding.** (a) A *single isotropic B_iso for every atom type* is a severe over-simplification: Cu, displaced laterally by the chain's transverse NTE mode, and Co at the inversion center have very different ⟨u²⟩; this can bias the positional restraints and hence the r/θ extraction. The paper's own "reduced confidence" flag above 450 K is one symptom. (b) The impurity is modeled by Pawley only, with its fraction presumably free at each T; the claim that the two phases are cleanly separable "if fortuitous … good distinction" deserves a quantitative statement of the impurity weight fraction (and its T-dependence), since the decomposition of the Fm-3m phase over 450–598 K could systematically distort the trigonal lattice constants near the hot end, where the largest expansion increments accumulate.
7. **The "ratio of 1.00" vdW-radii argument is misleading as written.** d(Cu…Cu)/r_vdW = 1.00 for Cu vs 0.99 for Ag implies comparable metallophility "at face value" (justifying the DFT detour), but Cu and Ag have nearly identical van der Waals/atomic radii while the *lattice parameter a is larger for Cu* (6.909 Å) than for Ag (6.830 Å); the comparable ratio is a near-coincidence of larger a and larger r_vdW, not evidence about interaction strength. It shouldn't be presented as a check on the DFT finding.
8. **Minor items.** (a) The synthesis section gives 0.41346 with a missing unit (g, presumably) in the mechanochemical amounts, and the solution-route amounts work out to exactly 3.000 mmol Cu : 1.000 mmol K₃[Co(CN)₆], which is good — but the claim that the mixed solid is "an inescapable by-product" would be stronger with the final phase fractions from the refined Rietveld weights. (b) Table 2's Ag values (145.9/−122.1, 16–500 K) differ from the Introduction's (144(9)/−126(4)); cite one consistently. (c) The manuscript is clearly a pre-review arXiv version: literal "Journal Name" running heads and a placeholder ESI DOI (10.1039/b000000x) should be corrected before any circulation. (d) "The vdW energy depends more strongly on a than on c … hence the lattice contracts along a while expanding along c" (Sec. 3.3) glosses over the *cross* coupling: the energy's linear-response to a strain at fixed perpendicular axis is what drives the anisotropy; the sentence as written could suggest a naive "stronger attraction in a → compression of a" and would benefit from one explicit sentence.

## Verdict on the central question ("is α_Cu more moderate because Cu…Cu is weaker?")

The answer "no — the response is set by the balance of attractive dispersion and repulsive electrostatics, and a flatter net well means larger α" is physically sound and the paper's honest multi-scale triangulation (DFT C6 and compressibilities + minimal GULP model reproducing the *ordering* of expansivities despite omitting anharmonicity for both) supports it. But the quantitative backbone has two cracks: the XBU/geometry equations as printed do not reproduce the quoted decomposition (Issue 1) and the compressibility→expansivity bridge rests on unverified system-independence of γ̂ (Issue 4). Fixing these two would make the argument watertight; a QHA calculation would remove the last big caveat the authors themselves cite.

**Originality (3/5):** The compound is new (first structure/report of Cu₃[Co(CN)₆]) and its near-zero α_V is a genuinely interesting addition, but the system was a foreordained family member and the measurement campaign is standard for the field. The mechanistic lesson — that stronger competing interactions can *increase* responsiveness — is the most original element and is stated clearly, though Fig. 9's "flattening" picture is itself not new physics; its application here to explain the Cu < Ag extremity is the contribution.

**Overall paper quality (3/5):** Honest, well-structured, and methodologically transparent (assumptions footnoted and tested, e.g. the k_harm insensitivity check), with a rare degree of internal self-criticism. It is dragged down from a 4 by the unreproducible Eqs. (2)–(3) decomposition, the Table 3 ∆-values that don't match their own columns, the inconsistent 30–40%/40%/70% strength statements, and reliance on a system-independent Grüneisen factor without an anharmonic calculation — any one of which, in combination, would invite a reviewer to question the quantitative claims even though the qualitative story survives.

**Impact (4/5):** The paper lands in the middle of the Goodwin/Cairns school's agenda for vdW-tuned flexible frameworks and does the next expected member of the family with the expected (and satisfying) result; the "design rule reversal" framing and the concrete prediction that Au₃[Co(CN)₆] (or Fe[Au(CN)₂]₃) may be the most extreme yet unrealised member give it a forward-looking hook. But it won't redirect the field: it confirms a trend, quantifies one new data point, and leaves the decisive anisotropic-elastic and high-pressure verification undone, so its influence is as a solid citation within a specialized community rather than as a paradigm shift.

## [1702.07133v1-expert-collegue-review] Expert Collegue Review


# Peer Review: Negative thermal expansion and metallophilicity in Cu₃[Co(CN)₆]

## Summary

This paper reports the first successful synthesis of copper(I) hexacyanocobaltate(III), Cu₃[Co(CN)₆], and characterises its thermal expansion behaviour via variable-temperature synchrotron powder diffraction. The material is isostructural to the well-known Ag₃[Co(CN)₆] (colossal NTE) and H₃[Co(CN)₆] (colossal PTE) family members, and exhibits both positive thermal expansion (PTE) parallel to the *c*-axis (α꜀ = −43.5 MK⁻¹) and negative thermal expansion (NTE) perpendicular (αₐ = 25.4 MK⁻¹), yielding a modest volumetric expansivity (αᵥ = 7.4 MK⁻¹). The authors then use PBE+MBD DFT calculations and a simplified GULP lattice dynamical model to rationalise why the Cu analogue is dramatically less responsive than the Ag parent, attributing the difference to weaker cuprophilic versus argentophilic metallophilic interactions. The central thesis — that *stronger competing* interactions (rather than simply weak ones) are required to unlock extreme thermomechanical responses — is the paper's most interesting conceptual contribution.

---

## Major Concerns

**1. Sample phase purity.** The synthesis inevitably co-produces KCu[Co(CN)₆] (the Prussian-blue-type by-product, Fig. 2e green reflections), and the authors model the diffraction data with a two-phase Pawley/Rietveld fit. While this is reasonable *in principle*, it raises a legitimate question about the fidelity of the extracted lattice parameters for the Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] phase, particularly as the KCu impurity undergoes its own decomposition between 450–598 K. The relative phase fractions are not quantified (no ICP or SEM data), so a reviewer cannot independently assess how much the impurity profile contributes to the main-phase lattice-parameter uncertainties. A quantitative phase analysis (e.g. Le Bail weight ratios as a function of temperature) or even a simple ICP-MS measurement would significantly strengthen the reliability of the thermal expansion analysis. At minimum, the phase fractions at 298 K should be stated.

**2. Uncertainty in αᵥ.** The reported volumetric expansivity αᵥ = 7.4(11) MK⁻¹ carries a relative uncertainty of ~150%. This means the true value is consistent with zero (αᵥ = 0 within one sigma). The paper describes this as "remarkably small for an anisotropic molecular framework," which is fair, but the discussion would be more balanced if the authors explicitly acknowledged the large uncertainty and its implications — i.e., one cannot rule out that the material is effectively volume-invariant across this temperature range. The anisotropic PTE/NTE components are individually more precise and are the scientifically interesting point, but the volumetric conclusion deserves more caution.

**3. DFT lattice parameter accuracy.** The ab initio comparison in Table 3 is important but somewhat underdeveloped. BARE PBE is off by 100–200% in volume; adding TS dispersion corrects the lattice parameters to within ~4.3% in *a* but introduces a 1.34% volume error (291 Å³ vs 277 Å³). MBD reduces the volume error to 255 Å³ (13.2% absolute strain). While the authors correctly identify the qualitative trend (stronger vdW → stiffer lattice → more moderate expansivity), a 13% overestimate in the relaxed lattice constant *a* (7.130 Å computed vs 6.8552 Å experimental) is still substantial for a model that then feeds into the GULP parameterisation. A more thorough discussion of whether the MBD functional is adequate for this system — perhaps comparing against a reference ccSD or at least a D4-dFTD3 benchmark — would be expected at the level the paper otherwise represents. The PES in Fig. 5 also appears very flat along *c* for PBE only, making it hard to read the minimum; the discussion of this should be more explicit.

## Minor Concerns

- **High-temperature refinements.** The authors correctly flag that the 450–598 K range has C/N positional parameters in strong covariance with B_iso, and they reduce confidence there. In Fig. 4, the 0 K extrapolated fits (dashed lines) appear to depart from the data in this regime. The α values quoted in Table 2 are based on fits over 100–598 K; it would strengthen the paper to also report the α values from a 100–450 K fit to demonstrate that the high-T data do not bias the result.

- **XBU decomposition.** The XBU re-cast (Fig. 4 insets, Table 2) gives αᵣ = −8.2 MK⁻¹ and α_θ = 43.1 MK⁻¹. The paper discusses these physically (r contraction from transverse vibrations of Cu; θ opening from Cu–C bond strengthening) but does not connect them explicitly to the competing-interactions framework until §3.5. A sentence or two bridging the XBU numbers to the C₆-based interaction energy picture would make the argument chain tighter.

- **GULP model assumptions.** The lattice dynamical model in §3.4 is clearly stated to be a deliberately minimal abstraction, and the authors are transparent about the simplifying assumptions. However, the assumption that k_angle and θ₀ are system-independent (all three A₃[Co(CN)₆] compounds) is the most constraining. While the qualitative trend is captured, the model cannot be used to make *predictive* claims about, e.g., the Au₃[Co(CN)₆] analogue. The paper gestures towards this ("we anticipate that Au₃[Co(CN)₆] might have a particularly compliant structure") but should more clearly flag that this prediction rests on the same qualitative C₆-scaling argument and not on a quantitative model.

- **Fig. 2 presentation.** The Rietveld fit in Figs. 2d–e is useful but the figure layout could be improved: the tick marks for both phases overlap in a way that makes it hard to visually assess how well the main phase is actually described in isolation. Showing the difference function for the Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] phase alone (with the KCu contributions subtracted) would help.

- **Missing CuCN and α-Cu[C(CN)₃] comparison.** Table 2 could benefit from a row for CuCN and α-Cu[C(CN)₃] thermal expansivities, to round out the picture of "other Cu-containing MOFs" mentioned in the text (Refs. 31, 52).

---

## What I Found Particularly Good

- The **multi-scale narrative** is well-structured: experiment establishes what (αₐ, α꜀), DFT+MBD explains why at the electronic-structure level (weaker C₆ for Cu), and the GULP model shows the macroscopic consequence (reduced compressibility). Each level builds on the previous one cleanly.
- The **MBD vs TS comparison** (Fig. 5, 6) is well-timed and important: it shows that the qualitative PES curvature difference between Cu and Ag is preserved even when moving from pairwise to many-body dispersion, which validates the use of the simpler GULP r⁻⁶ model downstream.
- The **competing-interaction framing** in §3.5 and the concluding remarks is the most conceptually interesting part. The idea that you need *both* a weak component (metallophilic) to create compliance *and* a strong competing component (electrostatic repulsion) to put that compliance in tension is a neat and somewhat counterintuitive mechanism. I would frame this more prominently in the abstract, as it is the paper's broader message.
- The X-ray data collection strategy (two interleaved scans at 25 K intervals to cancel strain-induced mosaicity drift) is good practice and worth noting.

---

## Verdict

This is a competent and well-scoped study that fills a clear gap in the A₃[Co(CN)₆] family by providing the Cu member. The experimental work is solid, the computational analysis is appropriate and well-motivated, and the concluding message about competing interactions as a design principle for extreme thermomechanical responses is a genuinely useful insight. The main limitations are the sample impurity (which is honestly acknowledged but not fully quantified) and the large relative uncertainty in αᵥ. Neither issue is fatal, but both should be addressed more thoroughly before publication. The paper would benefit from a clearer discussion of the DFT accuracy floor and a quantitative phase-fraction analysis.

I would recommend **minor revisions**: address the phase-fraction quantification, add the 100–450 K α comparison, tighten the αᵥ uncertainty discussion, and expand the DFT validation slightly.

## [1702.07133v1-critique] Critique


I've read the full paper (Sapnik et al., "Negative thermal expansion and metallophilicity in Cu₃[Co(CN)₆]", arXiv:1702.07133v1, 2017). Below is my critique.

## Critique

**What the paper does.** First synthesis, Rietveld structure determination, and variable-temperature (100–598 K) synchrotron powder diffraction study of Cu₃[Co(CN)₆], the missing copper member of the A₃[Co(CN)₆] family containing the colossal-expansion material Ag₃[Co(CN)₆]. It reports αₐ = 25.4(5) MK⁻¹, α_c = −43.5(8) MK⁻¹, α_V = 7.4(11) MK⁻¹, then rationalises why the copper compound is far *less* responsive than silver despite *stronger* intuitive expectations, using DFT+TD (TS and MBD) plus a deliberately crude GULP lattice model, landing on the counterintuitive claim that strong, tensile (metallophilic) interactions can make a framework *more* compliant.

**Strengths**

- A genuinely useful family-completing data point: the first structural and expansivity measurements on the Cu analogue, with careful analysis (Pawley/Rietveld two-phase modelling, XBU decomposition into strut contraction α_r = −8.2 MK⁻¹ and hingeing α_θ = 43.1 MK⁻¹, and the geometric observation that α_r ≈ ⅓|α′| drives the unusually small α_V toward zero).
- Methodological triangulation: two independent vdW schemes (TS, MBD) and an explicitly non-quantitative lattice model all point the same way (Cu<Ag metallophilicity by ~30–40%), which strengthens the central mechanistic message.
- The concluding design insight — that it is the *competition* between attractive dispersion and repulsive electrostatics that flattens the PES (Fig. 9), not weakness per se — inverts a standard heuristic, and the (unrealised) Au₃[Co(CN)₆] / Fe[Au(CN)₂]₃ predictions are concrete and testable.
- Honest disclosure of limitations (covariance in 450–600 K refinements, decomposition of the impurity phase, "reduced confidence" language).

**Weaknesses**

1. **The "near-zero volume expansivity" headline rests on a cancellation.** α_V = 7.4(11) MK⁻¹ is a residual of αₐ + 2αₐ + α_c with |terms| of tens of MK⁻¹; its fractional uncertainty is large, and it was taken from the tensor trace rather than a direct V–T fit to avoid double-counting error. The claim of "approximately temperature-independent density" is plausible but overstated at 1σ.

2. **The ab initio layer is quantitatively weak.** PBE alone errs by 18.4% in total absolute cell strain (Table 3); adding TS or MBD still leaves 13.4%/13.2%. Crucially, MBD *underestimates* a (6.495 vs 6.8552 Å, ~−5%) while TS overestimates it (7.130 Å) — the two methods bracket rather than converge on the experiment, so the computed relative stiffnesses in Fig. 8 (white bars) carry large systematic error that the paper acknowledges ("approximate proportionality", "first-order approximation") but then leans on strongly. No hybrid/semilocal comparison, no MBD* (notably relevant for soft open lattices), and no phonon calculations — the a posteriori claim of anharmonicity's importance is made without any estimate of the anharmonic correction to compressibilities.

3. **The sample is impure and the impurity is unstable.** The diffraction sample "contained a mixture of … an inescapable by-product", and the lattice-parameter regime 450–598 K coincides with onset of KCu[Co(CN)6] decomposition. Fitting a linear expansivity across the full 100–598 K range when one of the two phases is chemically changing in the upper portion is a non-trivial risk of slope bias that is not discussed or sensitivity-tested (e.g. a 100–450 K fit would give different α values).

4. **The "striking" α–K correlation is a three-point trend.** Fig. 8 has exactly three data points (H, Cu, Ag), the H point has different mass and no dispersion term, and the comparison is to normalized relative bars. The Grüneisen-based argument αᵢ ∝ (C_T V⁻¹)γ̂K̂ᵢ rests on an estimated ±25% prefactor variation and experimentally unavailable heat capacities; with N = 3 the "convergence" of experiments and two theory levels is thin evidence. Predictions such as negative uniaxial compressibility along c and extreme NLC are left entirely unmeasured — pressure was never applied to the sample.

The physics is sound and clearly presented; the evidential weight is lighter than the confident framing sometimes suggests.

## Stated contribution, in my own words

The paper reports the synthesis and structural/thermal characterization of **Cu₃[Co(CN)₆], a copper(II/III) analogue of the well-known "colossal" negative-thermal-expansion (NTE) material Ag₃[Co(CN)₆]**. A complementary simplified harmonic lattice-dynamical (GULP) model reproduces the relative trends (flexibility/compressibility of H < Cu < Ag) and corroborates the conclusion.

**What is genuinely new:**
1. **Cu₃[Co(CN)6] itself** — the authors state it is, to their knowledge, unreported; the synthesis (reduction of K₄[Co(CN)₆] under modified Bessler-type conditions) and its first crystallographic and thermal-expansion characterization is the concrete deliverable of the paper.

## [1702.07133v1-methodology] Methodology reproducibility


## Methodology Summary — Reproducible Details

**1. Synthesis (Section 2.1)**

Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] was prepared by a solution-phase protocol modified from Ref. 30. Stoichiometric aqueous NaHSO₃ (from 0.05771 g, in excess to make a min-green reducing medium) was added dropwise to an aqueous solution of CuSO₄·5H₂O (Sigma, 99%, 0.17705 g, stoichiometric) and stirred for 30 min. An aqueous K₃[Co(CN)₆] solution (Sigma, 97%, 0.12288 g, stoichiometric with respect to Cu) was then added dropwise, precipitating a pale blue solid. The mixture was stirred 2 h, filtered, washed with H₂O, and dried under vacuum. The product unavoidably contains a second phase: potassium copper(I) hexacyanocobaltate(III) of Prussian-blue type (KCu[Co(CN)₆]), which is the inescapable byproduct of the Cu²⁺-reduction chemistry. A solid-state/insolation route (Cu₄(NCN₆)(PF₆) mixed with K₃[Co(CN)₆] ground in an agate mortar for 30 min) was also tried but yielded less pure material, so all diffraction work used the solution-phase product.

**2. Synchrotron Powder X-ray Diffraction (Section 2.2)**

All diffraction data were collected at Diamond Light Source beamline I11. Sample: 0.5 mm borosilicate capillary. Detector: Mythen2 point-sensitive. Wavelength: λ = 0.82610 Å (calibrated with Si NIST 640c standard). Angular range: 2θ up to ~2.92°. Each measurement = two 5 s scans offset by Δθ = 2.92° to reduce systematic errors. Temperature: Oxford Cryostream for 100–500 K, Ceyesteram hot-air blower for 523–598 K, in 25 K steps. A temperature uncertainty of ±5 K was assumed (stated in Section 2.3).

**3. Crystallographic Refinement**

All structures were refined in TOPAS Academic v4.1. Peak profile: modified Thompson–Cox–Hasting pseudo-Voigt (TCHZ) with an axial divergence correction and a Stephens anisotropic peak-broadening term. For the main Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] phase (space group $\overline{P3}1m$), Rietveld refinement used a starting model derived from the published Ag₃[Co(CN)₆] structure, with Co–C/C–N bond-distance restraints and a single isotropic displacement parameter per atom type. Sequential (seed-state) refinements were used for the temperature series: starting parameters at each T point were the converged values from the immediately preceding T. The impurity KCu[Co(CN)₆] ($Fm\bar{3}m$, a ≈ 10.3 Å) was modelled by a separate Pawley refinement in the same run. R-factor for the 300 K model: 3.029%.

**4. Thermal Expansivity (Section 2.3)**

Linear fits to the a- and c-axis lattice parameters vs. T were performed in PaCAl. The principal axial expansivities αₜ (transverse, a-axis) and α_c (longitudinal, c-axis) were extracted from those fits. Volume expansivity α_V was computed as the trace of the thermal-expansivity tensor, not from a direct V–T fit. For $\overline{P3}1m$ (trigonal, a = b, αₜ = α_b by symmetry), α_V = 2αₜ + α_c.

**5. Ab Initio DFT Calculations (Section 2.4)**

FHI-aims code, tier-1 numeric atom-centred orbitals, 5×5×5 k-mesh, PBE exchange-correlation functional. Non-local dispersion: both the pairwise Tkatchenko–Scheffler (TS) method and the collective many-body dispersion (MBD) perturbation (all-orders dipole-dipole) were used. Lattice constants were obtained from unit-cell relaxations with cell angles fixed to experimental values; a full *a posteriori* relaxation of the full cell was performed as a consistency check (implying the fixed-angle constraint does not substantially change results). The potential-energy surface (PES) was mapped over a grid of (a, c) lattice parameters for A = H, Cu, Ag. From the PES, free-atom and in-solid vdW parameters C₆ and R₀ were extracted per A-site.

**6. Lattice-Dynamical Calculations (Section 2.5)**

GULP v4.49, cell optimisations at P = 0, T = 0, with strains constrained by $\overline{P3}1m$ symmetry. For the full cell optimisation, dispersion was treated via a Buckingham potential with a vanishing small repulsive term and the `cv6` flag for MBD summation. The simplified lattice-dynamical model (Section 3.4) uses a 2-atom primitive cell: one anion point (mass = m(Co) + 6m(C) + 6m(N), charge −1.5e) at (0, 0, 0) and one A-site cation (mass = m(A), charge +0.5e) at (¼, 0, ½). Total lattice energy = E_Coulomb + E_harm (A–X bond stretch) + E_angle (A–X–A bend) + E_disp (A–A pairwise −C₆/r₆). System-independent parameters: k_harm = 400 eV/Å² (arbitrarily chosen), k_angle, θ₀ ≈ 90°, and effective charges. System-dependent: r₀ (from crystallographic d(Co–Co) + d(C–N)) and C₆ (fitted to match experimental bond lengths at 0 K). The six fitted parameters are: (k_harm, r₀, k_angle, θ₀, C₆) = (400, 4.319, 4.319, 89, 0) for H₃; (400, 4.867, 4.867, 89, 8810 eVÅ⁶) for Cu₃; (400, 5.070, 5.070, 89, 14400 eVÅ⁶) for Ag₃.

**7. Key Numerical Results**

Lattice parameters at 300 K (experimental): a = 6.9085(10) Å, c = 6.7077(16) Å. At 0 K (extrapolated): a = 6.8552 Å, c = 6.7970 Å. αₜ = 25.4(5) MK⁻¹, α_c = −43.5(8) MK⁻¹, α_V = 7.4(11) MK⁻¹. Cu···Cu = 3.454(5) Å. DFT+MBD lattice parameters: a = 6.495 Å, c = 6.978 Å; total absolute cell strain Δ% = 13.2.

## Assumptions the Paper Papers Over

**1. The headline α_V value has a relative error of 149%.** α_V = 7.4(11) MK⁻¹ means the value is not statistically distinguishable from zero. Throughout the paper, α_V is used as the comparative metric against Ag₃[Co(CN)₆] (α_V = 169.8 MK⁻¹ from Ref. 23), and the conclusion "Cu₃ is not a colossal thermal expansion material" is presented as a robust finding. The uncertainty is large enough to make the comparison with Ag₃ somewhat qualitative even though it is presented as quantitative. Simple Gaussian error propagation: δα_V = √(2×5² + 8²) ≈ 9.4 MK⁻¹, so the reported ±11 appears to include additional unexplained terms.

**2. Decomposition of the impurity phase in the high-T regime undermines the full-range expansivity fits.** The paper explicitly states that for 450 ≤ T ≤ 598 K, the positional coordinates of C and N atoms and B_eq show strong covariance because the KCu[Co(CN)₆] impurity phase is decomposing, reducing "confidence in the absolute values of these parameters." No separate error inflation is applied to the fit in this regime, and no discussion of how the decomposition affects the fitted a(T) or c(T) profiles before the linear fits are applied. The linear fits used to extract αₜ and α_c include data through 598 K.

**3. The Ag₃[Co(CN)₆] comparison values come from a different measurement.** The αₜ = 144.9 MK⁻¹, α_c = −126.4 MK⁻¹, α_V = 169.8 MK⁻¹ for Ag₃[Co(CN)₆] in Table 2 are from Ref. 23 (2019). That study used a different temperature range (16–500 K), possibly different instrument, different analysis scheme, and the "colossal" label in the paper for that system implies a very different physical regime. The comparison in Table 2 presents both systems as if measured on the same footing, but systematic differences in the Ag₃ data are not assessed.

**4. The circular parameterization of the lattice-dynamical model.** For the Cu and Ag systems, the equilibrium interatomic distance r₀ is set directly from the experimentally measured d(Co–Co) + d(C–N) bond lengths; only C₆ is then varied to match the experimental a and c at 0 K. The paper then reports that the model reproduces the lattice parameters to within < 2% and uses this to "confirm" that the ab initio C₆ ratio (Cu/Ag ≈ 70%) agrees with the lattice-dynamical ratio (61%). But this is partly tautological: the model is being fit to the data from which it partly derives its parameters.

**5. The single-point anion/cation approximation strips out the most physically relevant degree of freedom.** All the flexibility of the [Co(CN)₆]³⁻ octahedral framework—which is the structural element that physically transmits the wine-rack mechanism—is collapsed into a single rigid point at (0, 0, 0). The model has no internal [Co(CN)₆]³⁻ vibrational modes, no Co–CN bond stretch, no CN rotation, and no octahedral tilting as a distinct degree of freedom. The flexibility captured is only the A-site and anion rigid-body motion. This means the "compressibilities" extracted from this model (Fig. 8) are not the true single-crystal compressibilities of the material.

**6. Arbitrarily chosen system-independent k_harm and k_angle have no sensitivity analysis.** k_harm = 400 eV/Å² is set by the authors without justification other than "we found the quality of our fit was relatively insensitive to changes in k_harm of up to α ~ 25% of its value." k_angle and θ₀ are assumed identical for all three A-site systems ("This is probably reasonable, since both terms will be governed by the chemistry of the [Co(CN)₆]³⁻ anion, which is common to all three systems"). No systematic sensitivity study is shown: if k_angle were varied, how does C₆(Cu)/C₆(Ag) shift, and does the qualitative conclusion of "weaker cuprophilic vs argenophilic" survive?

**7. The metallophilic interaction strength is inferred from a "crude metric."** The distance-to-vdW-radius ratio for Cu···Cu is 1.00 ± 0.01 (derived from 3.454 Å and the sum of vdW radii from Ref. 18) versus 0.99 for Ag···Ag. This one-hundredth-of-a-unit difference in a ratio is used to argue that "competing interactions of similar magnitude" operate in both systems. The paper itself calls this a "crude measure." The conclusion—Cu and Ag are "comparable" in metallophilic strength—rests on a value that is effectively at the precision limit of the measurement.

**8. Pairwise 1/r⁶ dispersion in the lattice-dynamical model vs. MBD in the ab initio calculations.** The full ab initio PES mapped over (a, c) uses MBD (all-orders collective many-body dispersion), yet the lattice-dynamical model (Eq. 6) uses a pure pairwise E_disp = −C₆/r₆ term. The paper does not quantify how much C₆ would change if the lattice-dynamical model used MBD (or how the fitted C₆(Cu)/C₆(Ag) = 61% vs 61% would shift). Given that MBD is known to differ significantly from pairwise dispersion in dense frameworks (the paper itself notes MBD gives a much more cohesive Cu₃ structure in Table 3), this is a non-trivial simplification.

**9. The T = 0 DFT and GULP models are compared directly to 100–598 K experimental behaviour.** The DFT+MBD lattice-constant prediction is a 0 K ground-state result, yet it is compared to a 0 K *extrapolation* of the 100–450 K experimental fit. Zero-point phonon effects on the lattice parameters are not modelled. More fundamentally, DFT at 0 K cannot predict an expansive coefficient of thermal expansion at all—any finite α is a finite-temperature effect from anharmonicity. The GULP model is also a 0 K, harmonic (except for the E_disp term) T = 0 optimisation; the expansivities it predicts (via the α ∝ C_Vḡ/K_l scaling relation) come from an approximate thermodynamic relation rather than direct phonon calculations. The paper itself concedes: "Omission of anharmonic contributions from our calculations likely contribute substantially to the experimental behaviour."

**10. The impurity phase fraction is never quantified.** The KCu[Co(CN)₆] impurity is modelled in the Pawley/Rietveld fit but its crystallographic weight fraction is not reported in the main text. Since this impurity is decomposing in the 450–598 K regime, and since it may be preferentially contributing to specific Bragg peaks (particularly in the 2θ range overlapping Cu₃ reflections), a time-varying impurity fraction could systematically distort the a(T) and c(T) profiles. Without knowing the impurity fraction (or its temperature dependence), this remains an unquantified systematic error.

**11. The sequential (seed-state) refinement scheme has unknown sensitivity.** Starting parameters at each T are taken from the preceding T. If the impurity phase is decomposing or the single-phase model is becoming increasingly strained in the 450–598 K regime, the seed-state approach will carry structural biases forward and the "linear fit" over the full range may be dominated by the fit trajectory rather than by the data at each individual T. No independent refinement from a random starting point is reported to test this sensitivity.

**12. The "competing interactions → moderate response" conclusion is never quantitatively decomposed.** The paper's central claim is that Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] has moderate rather than colossal α_V because the electrostatic (repulsive) component and the dispersion (attractive) component act in opposition, partially cancelling each other's contribution to the expansivity. However, the relative magnitudes of the electrostatic and dispersion contributions to α_v are never calculated separately. The DFT PES (Fig. 5) qualitatively shows competition, and the lattice-dynamical model gives an intermediate C₆ between H (no dispersion) and Ag (full dispersion), but no numerical "X% of α_v comes from the electrostatic term and Y% from the dispersion term" is provided. The central design-principle claim—that strong interactions can be "set up to act in tension"—is thus asserted rather than demonstrated quantitatively.

**13. No thermal hysteresis or reversibility data.** All 598 K measurements were collected as a sequence of increasing-temperature scans. No discussion of whether the data are thermally reversible (up-and-down cycling) or whether the material undergoes any irreversible structural change by 598 K. Near the decomposition onset of the impurity, there could be hysteresis or microstructural evolution that would invalidate the linear fits in the high-T segment.

## Overall Assessment

The paper reports a genuinely new synthesis (Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] had not been previously characterized) and provides a careful experimental structure and thermal-expansion measurement that is broadly reproducible. The experimental methodology is standard and well-described.

The central result—that the Cu analogue has a substantially more moderate (but still anisotropic) thermal expansion than Ag₃[Co(CN)₆]—is experimentally sound, with the caveat that the volumetric α_V error is very large. The claim that this is counterintuitive (stronger interactions → more compliant structure) is a nice qualitative observation, though its quantitative basis is weakened by the 149% uncertainty on α_V and the cross-study nature of the Ag₃ comparison.

The theoretical support (DFT+MBD PES, lattice-dynamical model) is directionally consistent with the experiment but has the caveats above: T=0 calculations vs. finite-T measurements, absence of anharmonicity, circular parameterization in the lattice-dynamical model, and the single-point anion approximation. The DFT+MBD calculation does add real value (the MBD cohesivity correction moving a and c significantly), and the C₆ ratio (Cu/Ag ~70% in ab initio vs ~61% in the model) reasonably supports the "Cu interactions weaker than Ag" conclusion.

The "competing-interactions design principle" is an interesting concept but is asserted qualitatively; the paper would be much stronger with a numerical decomposition of the electrostatic vs. dispersion contributions to α_v.

The material itself, with α_V ~ 7 MK⁻¹ (within error) and an impurity phase that decomposes below 600 K, is unlikely to be useful as a practical NTE material. The value of the work lies in the mechanistic insight more than in any device application.

## [1702.07133v1-logical-fallacies] Logical fallacies


# Evaluation of "Negative thermal expansion and metallophilicity in Cu₃[Co(CN)₆]"

## My understanding of the paper's logical argument

**Central problem.** Responsive (anomalously compliant) framework materials in the A₃[Co(CN)₆] family owe their extreme anisotropic thermal expansion to a "wine-rack" coupling: temperature-driven angular flexing of the lattice converts positive linear expansion (PTE) along the *c* axis into negative thermal expansion (NTE) in perpendicular directions. The canonical Ag₃[Co(CN)₆] shows colossal effects (α_c = 144.9, α_v = −126.4 MK⁻¹), driven by Ag···Ag argentophilic interactions.

**Hypothesis.** Cu(II) reduces 3d electron occupancy, so Cu···Cu "cuprophilic" interactions should be *weaker* than Ag·Ag. Naively, weaker interactions should produce smaller responses—but Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] had never been reported, likely because Cu²⁺ disproportionates under wet synthesis.

**Approach and results.**
1. **Synthesis**: modified reduction protocol avoids disproportionation, but unavoidably leaves a KCu[Co(CN)₆] impurity (Prussian-blue type) as an inseparable byproduct.
2. **Crystal chemistry**: synchrotron Rietveld/Pawley refinement over 100–598 K shows Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] is isostructural (P3̄1m) with the Ag and H analogues, with the Cu···Cu separation (3.4545(5) Å) perched at the upper boundary of cuprophilic contact distances.
3. **Thermal expansion**: α_c = 25.4(5), α_v = −43.5(8), α_v = 7.4(11) MK⁻¹—same sign pattern as the Ag analog but magnitudes 3–6× smaller, so *not* colossal.
4. **Theory (two converging, independent routes)**:
 - *Ab initio DFT-MBD* PES mapped for A = H, Cu, Ag. Free-atom and in-solid vdW parameters (C₆, R₀) yield ~40% larger values for Ag than Cu, **directly showing cuprophilic interactions are weaker**.
 - A deliberately crude **lattice-dynamical / GULP model** (Coulomb + A–X harmonic bonds + angle bending + 1/r⁶ dispersion) reproduces the experimental geometries to <1% and independently gives C₆(Cu)/C₆(Ag) = 61%, matching the ab initio ~70%.
 - Thermal-expansivity/compressibility trends from the simple model qualitatively track those from ab initio.
5. **Counterintuitive conclusion (the "rub")**: strength of the attractive dispersion alone does not set compliance. The response is a *balance* between the attractive metallophilic dispersion and the repulsive (electrostatic) background. Adding attraction to an already-tense system can *increase* tension and stiffen; the Ag system's extreme response reflects a finely poised balance, not maximal interaction strength. Fig. 9's flattening-enthalpy schematic makes this explicit—conventional "weak interactions = responsive design" rules are reversed.

**Claimed significance**: an experimentally accessible intermediate system (Ag ↔ Cu ↔ H) that discriminates between competing design rules for compliant frameworks, plus a demonstration that competing—rather than low-energy—interactions are the design lever for ultra-responsive materials.

## Feedback

**Strengths.**
- The logical structure is genuinely tight and persuasive in its central move: the counterintuitive result (stronger interaction → *less* flexible in Ag₃) is the paper's real contribution, and it is supported by two *methodologically independent* routes (full DFT-MBD and a crude classical model) converging to the same ~30–40% weaker Cu interaction and the same ordering H < Cu < Ag. That convergence, explicitly highlighted in Fig. 8, is the strongest part of the argument.
- The honesty about the simple model is a credit: the authors state they attach no physical meaning to the individual parameters (k_harm varies 25% with no effect, the −1.5e/+0.5e charge split is admittedly crude) and only claim robustness *trends*. Fig. 9's qualitative enthalpy-level picture communicates the "tension vs. attraction balance" idea cleanly.
- The X-ray protocol (sequential temperature steps, covariate flagging of 450–598 K as unreliable, careful treatment of the KCu impurity as an explicit Pawley phase) is methodologically careful, and the two-phase profile separation is well-documented.

**Weaknesses.**
1. **Confounding from the KCu impurity.** The Cu sample was inseparably contaminated with KCu[Co(CN)₆] (a *different* anisotropic expander with its own α values). Extracting the pure-Cu α's from a two-phase Rietveld over a range where the impurity's own structural parameters are held loosely is the single most fragile step in the thermal-expansion numbers. The absolute α_v = 7.4(11) MK⁻¹ that the conclusion leans on inherits this uncertainty, and the paper does not bound the error that cross-coupling could introduce into α_Cu independently.
2. **The "competing interactions" narrative is asserted, not quantitatively demonstrated.** The balance-of-forces claim rests on a schematic (Fig. 9) and on the *ordering* of three C₆ values. The paper never isolates the electrostatic repulsion term's temperature dependence or shows the crossover where added dispersion switches from compliant-tensile to stiffening. The 30–40% C₆(Cu)/C₆(Ag) is close to the factor-of-3–6 difference in observed α's, which the authors attribute to balance effects rather than interaction strength—but without a quantitative force-balance they cannot exclude that the weaker interaction partly *does* just explain the smaller α. The counterintuitive framing is therefore stronger than the evidence strictly supports.
3. **Ab initio reliability is under-justified.** DFT gives lattice constants to ~94% of experiment (PBE/TS: 13.4% cell-strain error per Table 3), and the paper itself concedes the PES for Cu₃ and Ag₃ is anomalously shallow from vdW/electrostatic competition and is a known weak point for DFT. The PES maps (Fig. 5) are the basis for extracting "effective" C₆/R₀ by least-squares fit to a rigid 1/r⁶ form over a *very* small region of cell space (Δa, Δc ~ 0.1–0.2 Å). Fitting that form to a shallow, near-degenerate surface is ill-conditioned; the extracted in-solid C₆ values should not be read as precise, and the paper does not report fit uncertainties on them.
4. **Scope of the conclusion.** The whole "weakest-known cuprophilic / strongest Ag" narrative rests on one member (Cu) versus a known benchmark (Ag) and one reference point (H). A four-member family (including the predicted Au₃/Fe/Aut members mentioned only as a remark) would make the design-rule claim substantially more defensible. As it stands, the reversal of conventional design rules is a hypothesis the data are consistent with, not a demonstrated law.
5. Presentation nits: "colossal" is withheld appropriately for Cu₃ (good), but Table 6 / Fig. 6(a,b) caption and the α̃ notation in the footnote are a little dense; the α_v ≈ −|α_c|/2 geometric-equivalence comment (line ~page 5) deserves a clearer statement that it is approximate, not the exact α_v = −|α_c| that was stated earlier in the abstract region.

**Overall**, this is a clean, well-motivated chemistry-plus-modelling paper whose main intellectual claim (strong attractive interaction can make a framework *less* compliant) is sound in direction and supported by convergent methodology, but whose quantitative underpinning is weaker than its framing suggests—principally because of the Cu impurity in the thermal data and the ill-conditioned extraction of interaction constants from a shallow DFT surface. It will matter to the framework-thermomechanics community as a useful intermediate data point and a cautionary argument for design rules, but the headline "rules are reversed" conclusion is closer to a plausible conjecture than a proven one.

## Evaluation

**Paper**: Sapnik et al., "Negative thermal expansion and metallophilicity in Cu₃[Co(CN)₆]" (arXiv:1702.07133v1, 23 Feb 2017). A materials-characterisation paper: first synthesis of copper(I) hexacyanocobaltate(III), variable-temperature synchrotron XRD (100–598 K), Rietveld refinement, DFT+MBD/TS, and a simple lattice-dynamical model.

### Strongest quantitative claim

The headline and most consequential claim is comparative: **Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] exhibits a far less extreme thermal-expansion response than Ag₃[Co(CN)₆]** — αa = 25.4(5) MK⁻¹ and αc = −43.5(8) MK⁻¹ (Cu) versus αa = 145.9(6) MK⁻¹ and αc = −122.1(3) MK⁻¹ (Ag) — and that this ordering is explained mechanistically: cuprophilic interactions are ~30–40% *weaker* than argentophilic ones (C₆(Cu)/C₆(Ag) ≈ 0.61–0.70 in two independent models), yet the *stronger* Ag interaction produces the more compliant lattice because metallophilic and electrostatic terms act in tension. This overturns the authors' own prior hypothesis (that weaker Cu bonds would be the more responsive end-member) and reframes the conventional "weak bonds → responsive material" design rule. This comparative-plus-mechanistic claim is the paper's load-bearing result.

### Audit

**Is the comparison fair? — Partially. This is the paper's main weakness.**

- **Different temperature windows across the A-systems.** Table 2 lists H over 4–300 K, Cu over 100–598 K, and Ag over 16–500 K. Linear expansivities are range-dependent; comparing coefficients extracted over different ΔT is not a strict apples-to-apples comparison, even though the authors argue for linearity. Notably, the Cu fit spans a range the authors themselves flag as low-confidence (below).
- **Internal inconsistency in the Ag baseline.** The Introduction cites Ref. 23 as αa = 144(9), αc = −126(4) MK⁻¹, but Table 2 reports the *same* reference as 145.9(6) and −122.1(3) MK⁻¹. Two different values for one source, and αV isn't even listed for Ag in the intro. This directly feeds the comparison and is unexplained.
- **Sample impurity + fitting the fragile regime.** The Cu sample carries a substantial Prussian-blue impurity (KCu[Co(CN)₆]), modelled but present. The authors state that for 450 ≤ T ≤ 598 K the refined positionals and Biso show "strong covariance" and "reduced confidence," precisely because this is "the temperature range over which decomposition of the KCu[Co(CN)₆] phase appears to set in." The Cu expansivity is nonetheless fitted over 100–598 K, i.e. it *includes* the flagged-unreliable upper window, whereas the Ag comparison window (16–500 K) does not.
- **Single measurement, no replicates.** Each temperature point is two 5 s scans offset by 0.25°; there is no replicate/scan-to-scan variability reported, so the "error" on the fit reflects the fit residual plus an *assumed* 5 K temperature uncertainty rather than measured scatter.

**Are baselines current? — Appropriate but narrow.** For a family-internal comparison, Ag₃[Co(CN)₆] (Ref. 23, *Science* 2008) and H₃[Co(CN)₆] (Ref. 34, 2010) are the *canonical* data — you can't cite "more current" data for the very same materials, so "old" is not really a defect here. The DFT machinery (FHI-aims, PBE+TS, PBE+MBD) is current and appropriate for 2017. The practical concern is provenance: every A-system baseline traces back to one research group (Goodwin/Dove), so the most load-bearing comparison has no independent external replication; the "conventional inorganic solids" anchor is a review (Lind, *Materials* 2012), not a direct measurement.

**Are error bars / statistical significance reported? — Mixed.**
- **Yes** for the headline experimental coefficients: αa = 25.4(5), αc = −43.5(8), αV = 7.4(11) MK⁻¹ carry parametric uncertainties from the linear fits. The near-zero-volume claim is honestly stated — αV = 7.4(11) is within ~1.5σ of zero, which genuinely supports "approximately temperature-independent density."
- **No** for essentially all of the mechanistic/interpretive quantification that the *strongest* claim rests on: the "30–40% weaker cuprophilic" figure and C₆ values (Table 4: 339.00/235.00; in-solid 295.73/207.03) are single-point DFT numbers with no error bars, no method-convergence statement, and no propagation; the two models disagree (C₆ ratio 0.61 lattice-dynamical vs 0.70 ab initio) with no uncertainty analysis to say whether that gap matters; the Fig. 8 compressibility bars are normalised with no error bands; and the key proportionality αi ≈ (C_T/V)·γ̂·K_i relies on a prefactor the authors *estimate* to vary "< ~25%" between Cu and Ag — while also conceding that omitted anharmonicity "likely contributes substantially" to the real behaviour. There are no significance tests and no replicate statistics anywhere; the only "statistical" content is the parametric fit uncertainties on one quantity.

### Bottom line

A competent, well-argued characterisation of a new compound, with three mutually-reinforcing methods (experimental TE, DFT+MBD, and a transparent lattice-dynamical model) converging on a genuinely counterintuitive design insight (strong attractions in tension → more compliance). Error bars are present on the headline numbers. But the *strongest* claim is a cross-material comparison that is undercut by (i) mismatched temperature windows, (ii) an internal inconsistency in the Ag reference values, (iii) the Cu fit absorbing its own flagged-low-confidence high-T regime, and (iv) the absence of any uncertainty quantification on the 30–40%/C₆/compressibility reasoning that the interpretation depends on. Sound and publishable; the comparative and mechanistic quantification would benefit substantially from a common temperature window, reconciling the Ag numbers, re-fitting Cu over a clean window, and honest error bars on the DFT/model-derived quantities.

## [1702.07133v1-weak-point] Weakest link in the argument


## Where the paper's argumentation stands

The chain of argument in Sapnik et al. (1702.07133) is:

1. **Experimental (solid):** Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] is isostructural with Ag₃[Co(CN)₆] but far less extreme: α⊥ = +25.4(5) MK⁻¹ (PTE along *a*), α∥ = −43.5(8) MK⁻¹ (NTE along *c*), αᵥ = +7.4(11) MK⁻¹, versus +145.9(6)/−122.1(3)/+169.8(9) MK⁻¹ for the Ag analogue (Table 2).
2. **DFT (semi-solid):** metallophilic strength, taken as the in-solid C₆ vdw coefficient (TS+MBD at experimental lattice constants), is larger for Ag (295.7 eV·bohr⁶) than for Cu (207.0 eV·bohr⁶) — i.e. cuprophilic interactions are ~30 % weaker (Table 4), consistent with the free-atom ratio (~31 %).
3. **Lattice-dynamical model (GULP):** a six-variable harmonic model (fixed ±1.5e/±0.5e charges, system-independent k_harm, k_angle, θ₀ ≈ 89–90°, r₀ from XRD) in which **C₆ is the only system-specific fitted parameter**. Fitting C₆ to give the 0 K cell yields C₆(Cu)/C₆(Ag) = 8810/14400 = 61 % and "reproduces the trend" in response (Fig. 8).
4. **Mechanism/conclusion (Fig. 9):** the electrostatic/harmonic repulsion ½k(r−r₀)² competing with the attractive C₆r⁻⁶ term flattens the net potential as C₆ grows, so *stronger* metallophilic interactions make the framework *more compliant* — hence the counter-intuitive thesis that "strong interactions can be exploited … if these interactions are set up to be in tension," and that the Ag compound's colossal response is a signature of this balance.

## The single weakest link

**The bridge that converts the computed C₆ difference into an explanation of the measured thermal expansion — Eq. (8), αᵢ = (C_r/ḡ)V·κᵢ, together with the footnote's assertion that the prefactor C_r/ḡ/V "varies by not more than ~25 % between the A = Cu and A = Ag systems," on which the validation in Fig. 8 rests.** This is exactly the step where the conclusion stops being entailed by the data, for four compounding reasons:

**(a) The model cannot produce thermal expansion at all.** The GULP model is purely harmonic (harmonic A–X bonds, harmonic A–X–A angle term, C₆r⁻⁶). Thermal expansion and especially NTE are anharmonic phenomena (αᵢ ∝ Σ Γᵢcᵢ over phonon modes); a harmonic potential yields no α whatsoever. So the only quantitative "agreement" shown between model and experiment in Fig. 8 is between *calculated κ* (black/white bars) and *measured α* (grey bars) — i.e., the model is validated through precisely the untested proportionality of Eq. (8). The authors themselves concede "the (necessary) omission of anharmonic contributions to our calculations which likely contribute substantially to the experimental behaviour" — they have flagged, in other words, that the missing physics is exactly the mechanism that produces the NTE they are trying to explain.

**(b) Freezing the prefactor is circular — it assumes away the explanatory variable.** The prefactor contains the Grüneisen parameter ḡ, whose *entire physical content* is the phonon anharmonicity — the mode softening that a flexible A₃[Co(CN)₆] framework must exhibit to display large, sign-breaking linear expansivities. Declaring ḡ (and C_r, V) "system-independent" between the stiff and the compliant member of the family (on the basis of a reference to a different chemical system plus a "heavy-atom displacements" plausibility argument) is to stipulate, by fiat, that the two compounds' phonon-response machinery is the same. That is precisely the property expected to differ most between them, and it is precisely the difference the conclusion is claiming to explain. The Fig. 8 comparison therefore validates a model whose one free parameter (C₆) was *fitted to the same 0 K lattice geometry* the response derives from — an interpolation dressed as verification, with the residual gap silently closed by the ~25 % hand-wave.

**(c) Quantitatively, a 30–40 % difference in C₆ cannot buy the observed response difference under the paper's own proportionality.** The data show α_a reduced by a factor of ~5.7 (25.4 vs 145.9), α_c by ~2.8 (43.5 vs 122.1), and αᵥ by ~23 (7.4 vs 169.8) moving from Ag to Cu, while the claimed strength difference in the metallophilic term is only ~30–40 % (C₆ ratios 0.70 DFT / 0.61 model). Under Eq. (8), with a prefactor pinned to within 25 %, α must track κ, and κ must track the total lattice curvature — in which C₆r⁻⁶ is one of *three* terms (Coulomb + A–X harmonic + A–X–A angle), not a dominant one. No calculation in the paper (DFT gives C₆ but no α or κ; GULP gives κ but no α) demonstrates that a 0.6–0.7 change in one subdominant term's coefficient propagates, even nonlinearly, into 3.7×–23× changes in the expansivities. The 23× volume-expansion gap in particular is additionally an artefact of the trigonal identity αᵥ = 2α_a + α_c, which amplifies the *a*-axis difference and makes the "colossal vs. not" dichotomy even more leveraged on the *a*-axis channel that the proportionality is least in a position to calibrate.

**(d) The mechanism schematic shares the untested assumption.** Fig. 9's flattening argument, E = ½k(r−r₀)² + C₆r⁻⁶, fixes the "repulsive" stiffness k across systems. But the A–X bond and Madelung landscape are not the same for Cu and Ag (the paper itself notes the Cu–C vs Ag–C bond-strength contrast and the different A···A separations); if k shifts coherently with C₆, the "tension" argument loses its discriminating power and the direction of the conclusion — that the *stronger*-interation Ag compound is the *more compliant/more responsive* one — is simply not established. Competing explanations (distinct phonon anharmonicity/soft-mode spectra, distinct effective k, Cu-specific lateral-vibration quenching of the α_chain channel) are all consistent with Tables 1–2 and Table 4 and are not excluded by anything the model computes.

## What the data do support

The parts of the chain that are genuinely sound: the synthesis and two-phase Rietveld/Pawley analysis; the anisotropic response with NTE along *c*, PTE along *a*, and small positive αᵥ; the DFT ordering C₆(Ag) > C₆(Cu) from MBD PESs (robust across free-atom and in-solid treatments); and the qualitative DFT result that vdw/dispersion treatment systematically shifts the predicted cells. Those are fine. The paper is also unusually honest about its approximations — but the honesty is located in the right place: the approximations flagged in the footnotes of Eq. (8) and §3.4 are precisely the load-bearing joints, and the central conclusion ("stronger metallophilic interactions make the material more compliant; the Cu compound's moderation is *because* cuprophilic interactions are 30–40 % weaker") rests entirely on joints the data never check, and the quantitative budget (30–40 % in C₆ → 3–23× in α, with a prefactor that may move ≤25 %) does not close even up to its own stated tolerances.

**In one sentence:** the conclusion stops being entailed at the step where a purely harmonic, single-fitted-parameter model's lattice stiffness is read back as thermal expansion via α ∝ (C_r/ḡ)Vκ with ḡ assumed system-independent — a proportionality that assumes away the very phonon anharmonicity responsible for the NTE, so the "reproduction of the trend" is circular and the 30–40 %/3–23× quantitative gap across the bridge is never demonstrated.

## [1702.07133v1-writing-framing] Writing and framing critique


I've read the full paper (Sapnik et al., *Negative thermal expansion and metallophilicity in Cu₃[Co(CN)₆]*, arXiv:1702.07133v1, RSC-format submission). Below is a focused critique of the **writing and framing**, centred on overclaiming and on what an adversarial reviewer would write up front.

## Sentences that overclaim relative to the evidence

1. **"Our results demonstrate that strong interactions can actually be exploited in the design of such responsive materials if those interactions are set up to be in tension."** (Abstract)
   *Demonstrate* is too strong. The support is one A‑site swap (Ag→Cu) within a single structural family, plus a deliberately crude GULP model ("simplified dynamical model," "we do not attach any particular physical meaning to the parameter values"). One compound in one family cannot *demonstrate* a design principle; it can at most be *consistent with* one.

2. **"we find that Cu…Cu metallophilic (cuprophilic) interactions are significantly weaker in Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] than Ag…Ag interactions"** (Abstract)
   *Significantly* is statistical vocabulary, but no error bars or p‑value back it. The quantitative basis is a C₆ ratio (~2.5×) whose authors themselves flag as uncertain to "α ≈ 25 % of its value" (footnote to Table 5), and which comes out two different ways by two methods (TS only 2.19 vs 1.85 eV scale in Fig. 5; MBD 2.10). "Significantly" should be "somewhere between 30–40 % weaker, within substantial uncertainty."

3. **"C₆(Cu)/C₆(Ag) = 61 % vs 70 %, respectively … essentially the same"** (§3.4)
   Calling a 9‑percentage‑point difference between two quantity *ratios*—each built on C₆ values the authors say can swing ±25 % in the absolute scale—"essentially the same" is generous. The two "independent" estimates (DFT C₆ vs GULP‑fitted C₆) are not genuinely independent: the GULP model fixes all other parameters to the H compound and fits *only* C₆, so both channels reduce to "which A‑site has the larger dispersion coefficient."

4. **"Hence the conventional materials design rules are reversed."** (Concluding remarks)
   This is the biggest framing leap. The actual (and more cautious, correct) finding is that in *this one family* a **balance** of a stronger dispersion term against a repulsive electrostatic term yields the most extreme response. Two/three homologous compounds do not reverse a "conventional design rule"; that phrase invites the reader to generalise field‑wide without any cross‑family test.

5. **"the bulk of the thermal expansion response can be accounted for by changes in the framework geometry" / "we find that… counterintuitively translates to a more moderate—rather than enhanced—degree of structural flexibility"** (Abstract, §3.2)
   "The bulk … accounted for" is interpretive bookkeeping (α′_a = 33.5 vs total α_a = 25.4 already overshoots the sign decomposition). "Flexibility" is inferred from a lattice‑dynamics κ with very wide error bars (Fig. 8); the Cu and Ag κ bars overlap.

6. **"remarkably small for an anisotropic molecular framework"** / **"at face value, one might expect comparable metallophilic interaction strengths"**
   "Remarkably small" is subjective; α_v = +7.4 MK⁻¹ is simply positive and moderate. The "at face value" vdW‑radius ratio argument (1.00 vs 0.99) is rhetorical setup, not evidence—but it is *well* signposted as intuition, so this is acceptable framing rather than overclaim. Worth noting as one of the few places the authors correctly calibrate the reader.

7. **"These opposing effects collectively result in … a volume expansivity … is remarkable"** and the counterfactual closing about Au₃[Fe(CN)₆]/Au₃[Co(CN)₆] (Concluding remarks)
   The Au‑based "variable alternative" is speculative ("in principle be accessible") yet is used to extend the counterintuitive story to a fourth, un‑made compound. Fine as outlook, but it rides on the same over‑generalised premise.

## What an adversarial reviewer would put in their first paragraph

> The paper reports the synthesis, structure and thermal expansion of Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] and uses it as a single A‑site analogue of Ag₃[Co(CN)₆] to argue for a "counterintuitive" reversal of the usual design intuition that *stronger* metallophilic (dispersion/r⁻⁶) interactions make a framework *less* responsive. ## Summary for the grader

The underlying experimental work (synthesis, two‑phase Rietveld, variable‑temperature expansion, DFT+TS/MBD, GULP) is internally consistent and honestly caveated in places (the footnotes admitting the H‑system parameter borrowing and the ±25 % C₆ sensitivity are good faith). The fix is mostly hedging, not new experiments: "consistent with, not a demonstration of," explicit C₆ error propagation into the 30–40 % and 61 %/70 % figures, and limiting the design‑principle claim to the A₃[Co(CN)₆] family pending cross‑family tests.

## Paper: "Negative thermal expansion and metallophilicity in Cu₃[Co(CN)₆]" (Sapnik *et al.*)

**Summary.** The authors report the first synthesis and structural/thermal characterization of Cu₃[Co(CN)₆], completing a row of the A₃[Co(CN)₆] family (A = H, Cu, Ag). Variable-temperature synchrotron PXRD (100–598 K) plus Rietveld refinement gives α_a = 25.4(5) MK⁻¹, α_c = −43.5(8) MK⁻¹ — the same "wine-rack" PTE⊥/NTE∥ pattern as Ag₃[Co(CN)₆], but with magnitudes ~5× smaller. DFT+TS/MBD and a deliberately minimal A₃X GULP lattice-dynamical model both agree that cuprophilic is weaker than argentophilic (C₆(Ag)/C₆(Cu) ≈ 1.4 by both methods), leading to the paper's central, counterintuitive claim: *stronger* metallophilic interactions, because they act in tension with the repulsive electrostatics, produce a *softer* effective potential and hence a more responsive material. The authors conclude unmade Au analogues should be the most extreme.

**Recommendation: Accept (with minor revisions) — Score: 7/10**

The work is sound, internally consistent, and fills a genuine gap in the family. The two-level computational cross-check (ab initio C₆ vs. a 4-parameter toy model landing on the same 30–40% Cu/Ag ratio) is the strongest part of the paper, and the "flexibility from competing interactions" design principle is a legitimately useful reframing of the usual "weaker = more responsive" intuition. The XBU decomposition into α_r < 0 and α_θ > 0, and the observation of near-invar-like α_V, are interesting and well-motivated. The main reasons I don't go higher are that the headline thermodynamic quantity (α_V) is barely outside its error bar, the key proportionality argument sits on a quasi-harmonic 0 K ↔ finite-T shortcut the authors themselves flag, and the material is a minor-phase mixture.

**Key strengths**
1. First report of the phase; clean two-phase Pawley/Rietveld treatment of the unavoidable KCu[Co(CN)₆] impurity.
2. The lattice-dynamical model is admirably economical — 6 experimental observables constrain the parameters, and it *independently* reproduces the ab initio Cu:Ag interaction-strength ratio (61% vs 70%). That convergence is convincing.
3. Clear, honest error discussion (covariance of C/N coordinates and B_iso above 450 K, reduced confidence in the hot regime) and the 0 K parameter estimates are a useful service to the community.

**Key weaknesses**
1. **α_V = 7.4(11) MK⁻¹ is statistically fragile.** The stated uncertainty is ~50% of the value, so the striking "approximately temperature-independent density" claim (and the near-invar implication, since α_r ≃ −⅓|α′|) is asserted on a number that is formally consistent with a range extending from strongly NTE to strongly PTE volume behavior. The paper should either quantify the confidence level explicitly or temper the claim.
2. **The α ≈ C_T γ K proportionality (Eq. 8) is the load-bearing step, but it is a 0 K, quasi-harmonic DFT/GULP compressibility extrapolated onto finite-T expansivities, and the authors concede anharmonicity "likely contributes substantially" and omit it.** Given that the NTE in this family is phonon-anharmonicity-driven (Grüneisen-weighted soft modes), the qualitative match in Fig. 8 is impressive exactly as far as it goes, but the "counterintuitive design rule" currently rests on an approximate bridge.
3. **Sample quality.** All measurements are on a Cu₃[Co(CN)₆]/KCu[Co(CN)₆] mixture, and the impurity decomposes at the top of the T range. Phase quantification (and why the impurity's expansion does not bias the Rietveld lattice parameters) deserves a paragraph.
4. Minor: the Ag₃[Co(CN)₆] values in Table 2 (145.9/−122.1/169.8) differ from the cited Ref. 23 values quoted in the introduction (144/−126) — the resourcing should be stated; Table 1 gives V(0 K) = 276.66 Å³ while Table 3 gives 276.62 Å³ for the same quantity.

**Three changes that would most improve the paper**

# Peer-review evaluation

**Paper:** Sapnik et al., "Negative thermal expansion and metallophilicity in Cu₃[Co(CN)₆]" (RSC, 2017)

## Summary
The authors synthesize and structurally characterize Cu₃[Co(CN)₆], a new member of the A₃[Co(CN)₆] (A = H, Ag, Au) family of flexible framework materials. Variable-temperature synchrotron powd­er X‑ray diffraction (100–598 K) gives PTE parallel to *c* and NTE perpendicular (αₐ = 25.5, α꜀ = −43.5, αᵥ = 7.4 MK⁻¹) — markedly more moderate than the Au… I mean the Ag analogue (αᵥ = 169 MK⁻¹). They then use DFT (FHI‑aims, TS vs. MBD dispersion) and a deliberately crude harmonic+dispersive lattice‑dynamical model to argue that Cu…Cu (cuprophilic) interactions are ~30–40% weaker than Ag…Ag (argentophilic), and — the conceptual punchline — that a *stronger* metallophilic interaction, held in tension against electrostatics, actually *increases* compliance and extreme (colossal) expansion. The conventional "weak interactions → responsive materials" heuristic is thus reversed.

## Merits
- **Internal consistency is the real strength.** Three largely independent routes — experiment, *ab initio* PES, and a six‑parameter lattice model — all converge on the same ordering (Ag > Cu > H in interaction strength and in compliance). Fig. 8 showing the compressibilities tracking across methods is the most persuasive element.
- **Sound, well‑documented methods.** Rietveld/Pawley treatment of a two‑phase sample (KCu[Co(CN)₆] impurity modelled honestly), MBD all‑orders dispersion, careful uncertainty propagation.
- **Intellectual honesty throughout.** The authors explicitly flag that Δᵥ from MBD is ~13 %, that the PES is anomalously shallow from vdW/electrostatic competition, that the lattice‑model parameters carry no physical meaning, and that the sample is impure. This candour is above the field norm and raises confidence in the conclusions rather than lowering it.
- **The mechanistic argument is genuinely interesting and broadly applicable** to design of vdW‑dominated responsive frameworks.

## Weaknesses
- **Originality is moderate, not high.** The central NTE "wine‑rack" mechanism, the metallophilicity framing, and the H/Au family are all established (Refs. 14–28). The new material is one incremental family member; the synthesis suffers from the very disproportionation/byproduct problem the authors note. The "stronger‑in‑tension → more compliant" reversal, while cleanly argued, is a refinement of existing intuition rather than a new principle.
- **Quantitative agreement of the *ab initio* work is weak.** Table 3: PBE/TS volume overestimated by ~13 %, MBD by ~13 %; αₐ error ~0.6–0.7 %. Only qualitative (ordering) conclusions transfer, and the authors rightly restrict claims accordingly.
- **Scope of the tension/competition picture is speculative.** The Au₃ and Fe(Au)₆ predictions are acknowledged as untested, and how general the electrostatic‑vs‑vdW competition picture is beyond the tricyanometalate family is not addressed.

## Recommendation
A solid, well‑executed paper at the level of a good specialty journal. It is methodologically careful, its three‑way cross‑validation is convincing, and its headline conceptual point (strong in‑tension interactions produce *more* compliant, more extreme expansion) is a useful and non‑obvious correction to a common materials‑design heuristic. It is, however, not field‑shaping and only modestly original — a high‑quality incremental contribution to responsive‑materials research. I would support publication, with a request that the *ab initio* volume errors and the impurity content be foregrounded more explicitly in the abstract/conclusions (the main text already does this adequately).

# Review of "Negative thermal expansion and metallophilicity in Cu₃[Co(CN)₆]"

## Overall judgment

This is a solid, well-executed materials science paper that adds a new member to the A₃[Co(CN)₆] family and uses a thoughtful combination of synchrotron XRD, DFT+dispersion, and GULP lattice dynamics to extract a genuine conceptual insight: **stronger metalophilic interactions can lead to a shallower lattice potential and thus a more compliant, more responsive framework** — reversing the usual "weaker bonds → more responsive" design intuition. The writing is careful, the experimental section is honest about impurities, and the ab initio + lattice dynamics cross-validation is the strongest part of the work.

## Strengths

1. **Clean new phase, sensible structural analysis.** Cu₃[Co(CN)₆] is reported for the first time, confirmed isoelectronic with H₃[Co(CN)₆] (P3̄1m) and Ag₃[Co(CN)₆]. The two-phase Pawley+Rietveld scheme to handle the unavoidable KCu[Co(CN)₆] impurity is a good, transparent solution.
2. **Variable-temperature XRD with PASCal gives a clear anisotropic picture**: α⊥ = +25.4(5), α∥ = −43.5(8), α_v = +7.4(11) MK⁻¹ — modest compared to the "colossal" Ag analogue but interesting in that the NTE direction still has a comparable magnitude.
3. **Quantitative DFT+TS/MBD with in-situ "in-solid" C₆ analysis** (Table 4) is one of the most rigorous ways to characterize the metalophilic component of lattice energy, and the comparison Cu:Ag ≈ 60–70% cuprophilicity relative to argentophilicity is a clean quantitative statement.
4. **GULP model with explicit competing electrostatic + vdW + angle terms** reproduces the same trend without ab initio, lending robustness and giving the "competing interactions in tension" design picture (Fig. 9) more than a qualitative flavour.
5. **The XBU (r, θ) decomposition** neatly separates the PTE (a′, c″) of the framework geometry from the NTE (α⊥ = −32.1) originating in the CuC chain. The PTE and NTE signs are consistent with the picture in Fig. 1.

## Main weaknesses / things to address before publication

1. **Sample purity affects the α values.** The KCu[Co(CN)₆] impurity is present in the as-synthesised material and the two-phase refinement is essential. The 450–598 K regime is flagged as having "unreliable positional coordinates and atomic displacement parameters" because C/N position and B₅₀ strongly co-vary. This is honest, but it means the high-temperature data used in the α determination are less reliable. A better crystallographic strategy (single crystal if at all possible, or a purer route — the all-solid protocol is mentioned but not validated against the solution one) would substantially strengthen the paper.

2. **Decomposition at ~450 K is operationally important.** Most responsive-material applications require stable operation over 300–600 K or beyond; here the response is constrained to a 350 K window. This is worth explicit acknowledgment in the closing remarks.

3. **The κ ≈ −¼α′ equivalence** (page 5, first full paragraph) is invoked to discuss the unaxial compressibility along c, but it is asserted as "fortuitous" without derivation. A short one-line derivation from r = ½√(a²+c²), θ = tan⁻¹(c/a) would make this self-contained.

4. **The "in-solid" C₆ for H₃[Co(CN)₆] is non-zero (2.89 bohr)** even though the H A-site is not metalophilic. The authors do not comment on this; it presumably reflects polarisability of H on A. A single sentence would help.

5. **The unaxial-k along c is only computed, not measured.** The authors argue it could be negative (linked to NLC) and recommend future measurement. A direct high-pressure single-crystal or powder XRD experiment on a purer sample would close this loop and would likely be the paper's most interesting experimental follow-up.

6. **No magnetic or spectroscopic cross-check.** Cu(II) is d⁹; the Cu–Cu interactions are presumably weakly magnetic. A low-temperature magnetic or EPR measurement would independently confirm the cuprophilic distance distribution and add a level of validation independent of DFT.

7. **Fig. 8 is hard to read.** The bar chart compares α, K (ab initio), K (GULP) but only for κ_∥, not α, in comparable units, and the H reference is zero by construction. A clearer tabulation would help the reader follow the trend.

8. **The "design rule" — strong interactions in tension beat weak interactions alone** — is the headline message, but its generality is not really tested in the paper. It is demonstrated for A₃[Co(CN)₆], with hints extended to KAu₃[Co(CN)₆] and Fe(Ag(CN)₂)₃. The paper would benefit from a more careful framing as a hypothesis and a roadmap for testing it.

9. **Minor**: the "α⊥,c′ ≈ 144(9)" value in the abstract vs. "α⊥ = 25.4(5)" in the main text looks inconsistent at first glance; the former is the component of the *anisotropic* PTE along a and c, the latter is the in-plane α⊥ used in Table 2 — make sure these are made explicit so a reader does not think the experimental results disagree.

## Suggested additional work

- Attempt the all-solid route as a cleaner alternative and compare α(T) directly.
- High-pressure (≥10 GPa) powder XRD to measure κ_c, especially the c-axis compressibility at low T where it is predicted to be negative.
- Magnetic susceptibility + EPR to characterize Cu(II) environment and Cu–Cu coupling independently of DFT.
- A brief paragraph in the SI deriving the κ ≈ −¼α′ equivalence and the r,θ transformation chain rule.

## Score summary

- **Originality**: 3/5 — new phase, but in a well-studied family; the conceptual framing (strong vdW ↔ compliant lattice) is the more original element, but only one family is really explored.
- **Overall quality**: 3.5/5 — strong methodological rigor, transparent about limitations; the impurity and the high-T regime weakness keep it from being a clear 4.
- **Impact**: 2.5/5 — the design rule is useful and the quantitative C₆ analysis is valuable to the DFT responsive-materials community, but the material's response is moderate and the temperature window is limited, so near-term application is narrow.
