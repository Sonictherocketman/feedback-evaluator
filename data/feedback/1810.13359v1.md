## [1810.13359v1-general-review] General Review


# Review: Abramo, D'Angelo & Viel (2013) — *Selecting Competent Referees to Assess Research Project Proposals: A Study of Referees' Registers*

**Research Evaluation, 22(1), 41–51.**

---

## Summary of the argument

The paper examines the adequacy of Italy's MIUR referees' register — a self-declared, bottom-up list of experts used to select peer reviewers for competitively-funded research proposals (PRIN/FIRB) under the Ministry of Education, Universities and Research. The authors ask two questions:

1. **Coverage**: Does the register adequately span the full spectrum of 205 scientific-disciplinary sectors (SDS) in the MIUR classification, relative to the actual demand for referee judgments?
2. **Performance**: How does the bibliometric productivity (measured by their own *Fractional Scientific Strength*, FSS, index) of the 956 academics in the register compare to the national population in the same SDS?

A third, field-validated analysis applies the same methods to a real 2008 call for proposals in the Region of Sardinia, where the region drew 75 referees from the MIUR register (all non-residents) to evaluate 467 submitted projects.

**Key findings:**
- 18 of 205 hard-science SDS have no registered referee at all; 25 more are covered by only one person. The most severely affected UDA is Medicine, where 27 of 50 SDS have no referee or only one.
- 46% of registered academics come from Industrial & Information Engineering (concentration index 3.30); Medicine and Earth Science are underweight (0.29 and 0.37).
- 5% of the 956 academics show zero publication or citation activity over 2004–2008; a further 34% fall below the national median; 20% are in the bottom quartile.
- In the Sardinia case, 40% of the selected referees had FSS below the national median, and 58% of applicants had projects evaluated by referees from a different SDS than the applicants' own.

**Conclusion**: The bottom-up, self-declared approach is inadequate in both coverage and quality. The authors recommend replacing it with a top-down, bibliometrically-driven, continuously updated system for identifying referees.

---

## Strengths

1. **Direct policy relevance.** The paper identifies a concrete, measurable weakness in a real, currently-operating peer-review infrastructure. This is rare in research-evaluation literature, which often stays at the theoretical level.

2. **Transparent, reproducible methodology.** The FSS metric is well specified (fractional citation credits, life-science author-position weights, standardization to year-by-subject-category national medians). The data source (ORP/WoS) is described, and the reconciliation error rate (<5%) is reported.

3. **Sensible analytical choices.** Using the median rather than the mean for citation standardization is correct given the skewed distribution. The fractional-weighting scheme for co-authorship, with the specific Italian 40-30-15-10 split derived from expert advice, is a reasonable adaptation to the field's norms.

4. **The "cross-colonization" observation is interesting.** The finding that 37 SDS were claimed by academics whose *official* SDS is different — e.g., six fluid-dynamics professors all coming from the aerospace-propulsion sector registering for ING-IND/05 — is a concrete and somewhat unexpected insight about the incentives created by the self-declared competence procedure.

5. **The Sardinia case study grounds the analysis in a real decision event**, showing that the theoretical coverage gaps had tangible consequences in a specific funding round.

---

## Critical Weaknesses

### 1. Conflation of research productivity with referee competence (fundamental)

This is the paper's most serious conceptual limitation. The register is a list of people selected to *judge* proposals, not to *produce* research. The paper uses FSS — a measure of cited publication output over 2004–2008 — as the proxy for "competence to evaluate" without justifying why this is the right proxy. Consider a plausible but omitted scenario: a senior professor who held a dean's office from 2005–2010, publishing very little but with 30 years of deep domain expertise, would be scored as "inactive" by the FSS measure yet would be an excellent referee. The register includes full professors and research directors, many of whom have administrative roles that suppress output.

The paper briefly touches on this ("the more the competence of the referee concerning the core topic, the more their judgment is trustworthy") but never resolves the tension. The recommendation to use bibliometric FSS to select referees inherits this problem: it would systematically disadvantage exactly the senior, established experts whose deep domain knowledge — not their recent publication count — makes them competent to evaluate proposals. The paper cites Glänzel (2008) on the known distortions of citation indicators but applies FSS as if those distortions are fully handled by subject-category standardization, without addressing the career-stage issue.

**Recommendation for the authors:** At minimum, a sensitivity analysis separating full professors from associate professors; or using a longer observation window (10 years) to reduce the penalty on those whose peak productivity fell before 2004.

### 2. No statistical testing anywhere

Tables 2–7 are entirely descriptive: counts, percentages, ratios. Key findings — e.g., "43% of civil engineering referees were inactive" (n = 40) or "40% of Sardinia referees below the national median" (n = 55) — are reported as point estimates with no confidence intervals, no binomial or chi-square tests, and no multiple-comparison correction. Given the number of UDA-level comparisons (9 UDA × several performance cells), several of the individual "concerning" findings in Tables 4 and 7 are likely attributable to sampling variation. The Sardinia case is especially fragile: the Physics and Civil engineering subsamples have 2 and 3 referees respectively; any inference at that level is meaningless.

### 3. The "top-down" alternative is asserted, not demonstrated

The conclusion's central recommendation — replace the bottom-up register with a top-down, bibliometrically-driven referee-identification system — is supported by exactly nothing in the paper. The authors:

- Do not propose or test the algorithm.
- Do not demonstrate that top-down bibliometric selection produces referee pools that are *actually better at evaluating proposals* (no outcome comparison, no blinded review-quality comparison).
- Do not address the risk that a top-down bibliometric system would itself carry the "famous researcher bias" they cite in the Introduction (Glanz & Bero 1994; Wessely 1998) — if you select referees by citation count, you select the already-famous, which is precisely the distortion the peer-review bias literature warns about.

The paper would be much stronger with even a pilot demonstration: "If we had selected referees for the Sardinia call using a top-down FSS-based query over the ORP/WoS, what would the coverage and performance profile have been?"

### 4. The concentration index rests on an unvalidated assumption

The concentration index (column 5, Table 3) compares the share of each UDA among registered academics to the share of that UDA among national academic staff, implicitly assuming that evaluation demand is proportional to staff size — that is, that researchers in all UDA submit grant applications (and thus require referee judgments) at rates proportional to their UDA's size. This is stated but never tested. In practice, some fields apply at higher rates than others. If Engineering applicants submit more frequently per head than Biology applicants, then the Engineering overweight in the register might be closer to warranted than the index suggests.

### 5. Temporal obsolescence, not design failure

The register was created in 2001. By the measurement window (2004–2008), it was already four to seven years old. The paper does not distinguish between: (a) the register was structurally inadequate from its design in 2001; and (b) it became outdated as careers advanced, people retired or left, and new researchers matured into the system. If the latter dominates, the practical remedy is "run the register update cycle more frequently," not "abandon registers for a different mechanism." The authors' strong conclusion against bottom-up design may overstate what the longitudinal evidence (which they don't have) would support.

### 6. The Sardinia "coverage" metric is circular

Table 6 measures "coverage" as the share of applicant *coordinators* from SDS that are not matched to a referee's SDS. But the Sardinia region only selected 75 referees from a register of 1,545, and specifically excluded Sardinia residents. The low coverage is a direct consequence of the region's decision to use a very small, externally-sourced subset, not a reflection of the MIUR register's overall quality. The Sardinia case therefore confounds register composition with the region's specific sampling choices.

### 7. Minor issues

- **Missing references on register design.** The paper cites Wessely (1998) for general peer-review bias but does not engage with the broader literature on how different national peer-review systems have handled referee selection (e.g., the UK REF, the Dutch NWO system, the German DFG panel system). A brief comparison would have situated the Italian case better.
- **The footnote 10 claim** that the Italian system (one official SDS per professor) is "unique in the world" is unsupported and likely too strong.
- **Table 1** (competencies for one academic) serves as a qualitative illustration but is oddly placed in the methods section and is not analyzed.

---

## Overall Assessment

This is a competent, clearly written, and genuinely useful empirical case study. It demonstrates, with real data from a real system, that a national referees' register — built through a self-declared, public-call process and not updated for several years — contains measurable and potentially consequential gaps in both disciplinary coverage and the current productivity of its members. These findings are likely to have been influential in Italian research-policy circles, and the Sardinia case provides the concrete grounding that such a policy paper needs.

However, the paper has a fundamental unresolved gap: it uses bibliometric productivity as its quality measure without adequately justifying why "cited publications over 2004–2008" is the right proxy for *referee competence*, and then builds a conclusion (adopt top-down bibliometric referee selection) on the foundation of that unvalidated assumption. The alternative it proposes would itself be vulnerable to the exact bias it cites in the introduction. The statistical basis for the UDA-level findings is thin, and no part of the "top-down" recommendation is operationalized or tested.

The paper would be substantially stronger with: (a) a career-stage decomposition; (b) confidence intervals or significance tests on all cell-level percentages; (c) a pilot demonstration showing the Sardinia outcome would have been different under a top-down selection; and (d) explicit engagement with the risk of bias in any bibliometric-driven selection, including its own.

**Review: "Selecting competent referees to assess research project proposals: a study of referees' registers" (Abramo, D'Angelo & Viel, *Research Evaluation*, 2013)**

**Summary of the work.** The paper examines the Italian MIUR register (a "bottom-up," self-declared list of 1,545 experts, 956 academics) used to select referees for competitively-funded research proposals. It (i) measures the register's *coverage* against the spectrum of competencies (205 hard-science "scientific disciplinary sectors," SDSs) and against national academic-staff supply; (ii) computes a *bibliometric performance* metric (Fractional Scientific Strength, FSS: median-standardized fractional citations) for each referee and ranks them as percentiles against their national colleagues in the same SDS; and (iii) applies both to a 2008 Sardinia regional call (55 referees). Headline findings: coverage is badly non-uniform (43/205 SDSs with zero or one referee, medicine worst), the register is over-weighted toward industrial/information engineering (concentration index 3.30) and under-weighted toward medicine (0.29) and earth science (0.37), and 34% of academic referees sit below the national median, 20% in the last quartile, 5% "inactive."

**Strengths**

1. **Genuinely useful policy question, empirically answered.** Assessing the adequacy of expert registers is a real gap (the authors document the absence of prior work), and the findings are concrete and immediately actionable for funding agencies and register operators. The coverage problem and the "cross-colonization" of 37 SDSs by out-of-field referees are well evidenced and under-discussed in the literature.
2. **Sound, field-aware bibliometric design.** Comparing each individual within their own SDS against national peers, with median (not mean) citation standardization, fractional co-author credit, and a per-year, per-subject-category benchmark, is a defensible way to avoid most cross-field and cross-country distortions. Because each referee is measured with the same instrument (WoS) as their national comparison pool, the *relative* ranking is internally valid even if absolute output is understated.
3. **The concentration index (register share ÷ staff share, benchmark ≈1) is simple, transparent, and effective** — it makes the imbalance legible at a glance.
4. **Honest about limitations**: the authors themselves flag the impossibility of an international benchmark, the small Sardinia sample, and the multi-SDS candidature caveat.

**Main concerns (in order of importance)**

1. **The core validity inference is never made explicit or validated.** The whole argument rests on the assumption that a referee's bibliometric output is a proxy for the *trustworthiness of their judgment* on a proposal. Citation strength is a measure of a researcher's own productivity/impact, not of the accuracy, fairness, or topical fit of their reviews. A highly-cited full professor can be a weak referee for an adjacent subfield, and a mid-career applied researcher can be ideal for an applied proposal. The paper repeatedly equates "scientific performance" with "referee competence," but no evidence links the former to the latter. This is the single biggest interpretive gap, and it is what carries the paper's strongest policy claims.
2. **WoS-only coverage, and the "inactive" claim.** Flagging 5% of academics as "nil performance — either didn't publish or got no citations" (fn. 18) conflates two very different situations. A bottom-up national register deliberately includes public-research-institution and applied/industry-adjacent scientists (Category B/C) much of whose relevant output (patents, applied/technical reports, non-English, national journals, mentorship) is under-represented in WoS. Labeling a fifth of a small UDA as "scientifically inactive" is a strong claim that the data source cannot fully support. The relative ranking survives this, but the "inactive" and "last quartile" narrative does not.
3. **The benchmark is too lenient for the stated task.** Referees for new funding are in principle expected to be at the *international* frontier, not above the *national* median. Because the comparison is national-only (acknowledged as "not feasible yet"), a referee scoring 37th nationally for a field in which Italy is not at the frontier may be a poor judge of state-of-the-art proposals. "Above the national median" should not be read as "fit to evaluate frontier research."
4. **The policy conclusion is under-supported and reads as promotional.** The conclusion pivots to recommending that registers be replaced by *top-down keyword queries of commercial databases (WoS/Scopus)*, "rank records by author." (a) This recommendation follows only from the (unvalidated) premise in point 1. (b) Keyword-matching a *specific proposal* to the "most published/cited authors in a field" is a different and harder problem than the SDS-level productivity ranking demonstrated; the paper does not show it would produce better-matched referees. (c) Advocating a commercial bibliometric product to a policy audience gives the final sections a mild vendor tone that weakens an otherwise empirical paper. A stronger ending would stop at "the register has material gaps and its quality distribution is heterogeneous," and frame bibliometric *screening* of registers as one corrective, without the replacement prescription.
5. **The demand model behind the concentration index is assumed, not measured.** Assuming every UDA generates evaluation demand proportional to its academic-staff size ("equal enthusiasm") is a strong prior. The Sardinia applicant distribution (Table 6) is, in fact, not simply proportional to staff (e.g., medicine 93 applicants / 12 referees), so the "imbalance" partly reflects a chosen demand model rather than observed competition.
6. **Small sub-samples and thin methodological detail.** The Sardinia referee-performance analysis (Table 7) is under-powered — several UDAs have 2–3 referees, where one person moves the percentage by ~33 points. The FSS applies a life-science-specific co-author weighting scheme (40/40/20, 30/15/15/10) but does not state clearly how FSS is computed for non-life-science fields (i.e., whether the positional fractionalization is dropped); that inconsistency across fields should be spelled out. The five-year window with citations frozen at June 2009 also means 2008 outputs had little citation time, biasing recent cohorts below the median.
7. **Novelty is modest and somewhat oversold.** Both the concentration index and the field-normalized FSS percentile are recombinations of standard bibliometric practice; the real contribution is the *application to a referee register*, not a new method. "We present a methodology" slightly overstates what is, more precisely, a well-executed applied analysis.
8. **Minor**: typos ("MATERIALS PHSYSICS," "the their national colleagues"); a word on the ethics of producing a named performance ranking of individual referees (even if aggregated here) would be appropriate.

**Net assessment.** A competent, clearly written, well-executed applied study in bibliometric research evaluation, addressing a real gap with clear and useful findings. It is not excellent because its central premise (research output ≈ referee quality) is unvalidated, its "inactive/low performance" narrative is overstretched by WoS-only data and a lenient national benchmark, and its most consequential recommendation (replace the register with keyword-ranked commercial-bibliometric selection) is not backed by the evidence presented. Solid, credible, and useful within its niche; its limitations are largely acknowledged but are more consequential than the framing lets on.

**Recommendation (if it were a real referee report):** minor-to-major revision — clarify the output→referee-quality inference, tone down the "inactive" narrative given WoS coverage, temper the top-down replacement recommendation, and add caveats on the small sub-samples and the assumed demand model.

## [1810.13359v1-critique] Critique


# Critique of Abramo, D'Angelo & Viel (2013), *"Selecting competent referees to assess research projects proposals: a study of referees' registers"*

## 1. What the paper does

The paper asks a concrete, well-motivated question: how well does Italy's MIUR register of referees (created 2001, 1,545 experts, 205 hard-science *scientific disciplinary sectors*, SDS) actually cover the discipline landscape and contain referees whose research output is above average? The authors have access — apparently via a commissioned study — to a register that is not publicly available, and use it to: (i) measure "coverage" of the 205 SDS by declared competences, (ii) measure the *Fractional Scientific Strength* (FSS) of the 956 academic referees using WoS 2004–2008 publications, with citations standardized by the Italian median for the same publication year and subject category, (iii) validate the findings on a 2008 Sardinia call where 75 referees drawn from the register evaluated 467 proposals.

## 2. Strengths

1. **Access to non-public data.** The principal contribution is not the bibliometrics — those follow Abramo & D'Angelo's prior work (2011, 2012) — but that the register was actually observed. The register does not self-report, and the authors demonstrate that the gap between how such a mechanism *is* and how it *should be* is large.
2. **Two-axis design.** Separating "coverage" (breadth) from "performance" (quality of those present) is the right decomposition. A register can be broad but weak, narrow but strong; the paper shows the MIUR register is weak on both axes.
3. **Quantified gaps.** The finding that 18 of 205 SDS have no registered expert, 25 more have exactly one, and 43 total SDS (21%) lack adequate coverage, is a clean, actionable number. The concentration indices (46% of academics in industrial engineering vs 3.30× over-representation; only 13 experts in medicine, the largest hard-science UDA, 31% of national staff) are striking.
4. **External validation.** The Sardinia case — where 58% of subjects were evaluated by referees from an SDS different from the submitter's, and 83% of industrial-engineering applicants were cross-evaluated — is the kind of field evidence a purely register-based study would never produce.
5. **Concrete counterproposal.** The closing recommendation — use ORP/WoS/Scopus to identify referees top-down per project topic — is implementable, and the paper sketches the query. That is more than most research-evaluation critiques manage.

## 3. Limitations

### 3.1 The "declared SDS" variable is circular

The coverage analysis (Section 3) treats the declared SDS of an expert as ground truth, but the paper's own Section 3 finds "cross-colonization" — 37 SDS are not covered by their *official* staff but by scientists from other SDS who applied as experts in a foreign field. The paper does not reconcile this: if self-declared SDS is unreliable for performance (and is, by the paper's own evidence), why is it reliable for coverage? A referee who self-declares "ING-IND/05" but whose papers are in "ING-IND/08" — is that coverage of /05 or /08? The paper implicitly answers "both," but that answer breaks the counting in Table 2 and is not acknowledged.

### 3.2 Citation-maturity bias

Citations are snapshotted at 30 June 2009, but publications span 2004–2008. A 2008 publication has had 12–18 months to accumulate citations; a 2004 publication has had about 5 years. The FSS formula does not age-correct. The "5% inactive" (zero cited publications) and "34% below median" figures therefore systematically under-count recent entrants and over-penalize fields with shorter citation half-lives. The authors acknowledge using medians (footnote 11) but do not test the sensitivity to window length or to alternative age corrections (e.g., the field-normalized impact indicator of Moed et al. 2004, which the paper cites but does not use).

### 3.3 The normalization unit is unstable

`M_e_i` is estimated from "all Italian cited-only publications of the same year and same subject category." The WoS subject-category granularity is coarser than the Italian SDS; some SDS (e.g., "Energy and environmental systems," 30 experts; "Aerospace propulsion," 9 experts) sit inside subject categories that are very small in an Italian-only sample, in which case the median of even tens of publications is highly variable. No SE or bootstrap on `M_e_i` is given, and the FSS values that flow from it inherit that noise. The paper would be more persuasive with at least a sensitivity analysis to (a) a different normalization year and (b) inclusion of foreign-only references.

### 3.4 No inferential statistics

Every quantitative claim is descriptive. Table 6 has 3 referees in Civil engineering, 2 in Physics, 3 in Earth science — samples under which no hypothesis test has power. The Sardinia "58% of applicants from SDS not covered by referees" is presented without a confidence interval; with 327 subjects it would be roughly a ±5.5 pp 95% CI, but the sub-UDA numbers are far too small for the paper to make the discipline-level claims it does.

### 3.5 The "national staff" reference is a policy assumption, not a fact

The concentration index in Table 3 takes the ratio of academic experts per SDS to the SDS share of total Italian academic staff. The paper explicitly says "we assume that researchers of all the UDAs will participate with equal enthusiasm in calls … and thus create demands for evaluation that are proportional to the size of the UDAs." That assumption is doing essentially all of the weight in the "under-representation" claim. A different — reasonable — prior would be that the *demand* for evaluation is proportional to the *volume of grants submitted per SDS*, which the Sardinia data hint at but do not fully reveal (the paper's own `Applic.` column in Table 6 is the best proxy for that demand, and it is never used outside the Sardinia section).

### 3.6 The bottom-up / top-down dichotomy is false

The conclusion frames the issue as "self-declared register (bad)" vs "open bibliometric query (good)." Top-down identification has its own well-known biases — publisher bias toward English-language journals, citation-driven identification that misses emerging fields, inability to capture institutional context or service record, and the fact that WoS itself has known gaps for many hard-science domains. The ORP/Scopus pipeline the paper recommends has not been validated as a referee-selector in this paper, and the paper does not compare the two on the same task.

### 3.7 No international benchmark

Acknowledged in Section 4 as "not feasible yet." This is a serious limitation for a register whose authors themselves say *is* "increasingly less international." An Italian-normed percentile of 60 says nothing about whether the referee is competitive with a European or North American colleague for a given grant. The paper's normative claim — that the register is inadequate — is therefore relative to an Italian reference group and may be more conservative than the real gap.

### 3.8 Policy framing undersells the institutional role

The paper's normative claim — that registers are technical artifacts that should be replaced by bibliometric queries — ignores the governance function of a public register: transparency (who is eligible to judge public money), accountability (an audit trail), and political balance (the register includes non-academic experts in category B and C, 19% of the list). A bibliometric query over ORP cannot be audited in the same way, and cannot, by construction, include anyone without a WoS footprint.

## 4. Specific quantitative issues to flag

- **Table 4, "5% inactive":** the denominator is 956 academics. Five percent is 48 referees. For a 2004–2008 window with WoS-only indexing, a portion of this group is almost certainly not *inactive* but simply not *English-indexed*. ## The paper's stated contribution, in my own words

The authors propose and apply a **methodology for auditing the adequacy of a national register of expert referees** used to select peer reviewers for competitively-funded research projects, and they do it against Italy's MIUR register (created in 2001). Two dimensions are examined:

## Novelty judgment given the cited prior work

**What is genuinely new (and not in the cited literature):**
- **The object of study itself is novel.** The authors explicitly report an *unsuccessful literature search* for prior studies assessing the adequacy of referees' registers. An empirical audit of a *national referee/expert register* — combining a "coverage" index against the full disciplinary spectrum with a head-to-head bibliometric performance comparison against the national population — was, to my knowledge, unprecedented. This is real novelty, reinforced by the fact they obtained access to otherwise private referee identities through a regionally commissioned study.
- **The "coverage" construction and the detection of cross-colonization** are specific, novel analytical moves not found in the peer-review-bias literature they cite.

**What is NOT new (reused building blocks):**
- **The bibliometric machinery is entirely inherited.** FSS, fractional co-authorship credit, and field/median citation standardization come directly from the same group's own earlier work (Abramo & D'Angelo 2011; Abramo, D'Angelo & Di Costa 2008) and from standard bibliometric practice (Moed et al. 2004; Glänzel 2008; Lundberg 2007). ## [1810.13359v1-methodology] Methodology reproducibility


# Evaluation

This is Abramo, D'Angelo and Viel (2013), published in *Research Evaluation* 22(1), 41–51 (DOI: 10.1083/reseval/rvs031) — a single-case, Italy-only study of the adequacy of the MIUR (Italian Ministry of Education, Universities and Research) register of experts used to referee competitively-funded research proposals under the PRIN/FIRB programmes, with a field test on a 2008 call by the Region of Sardinia.

## Methodology, in detail

### 3. Coverage analysis (Section 3) — *is the register broad enough?*

**Unit of analysis.** Each of the MIUR's 205 "scientific discipinary sectors" (SDS, within 9 hard-science UDA — Math/CS, Physics, Chemistry, Earth sciences, Biology, Medicine, Ag/Vet, Civil eng., Ind.& Information Eng.) is a coverage unit. Experts are assigned to SDS by their *official* academic classification (the one that won a national tenure vote), not the SDS they *self-declared* competence for when applying to the register. Total register = 1,545 (800 category A, 304 B, 307 C, 134 unspecified); the analysis restricts to 956 hard-science academics who belong to the 205 hard-science SDS (97% of the 1,492 hard-science register entries).

**Per-SDS diagnostic (Table 2).** For each UDA: (i) count of total registered experts whose official SDS lies in that UDA; (ii) of those, how many are academics; (iii) number of its SDS with zero registered experts ("not covered"); (iv) number of SDS with exactly one expert — the authors' rule of thumb is that a project needs at least two competent referees, so single-expert SDSes give only a "partial" evaluation. Across the 205 SDS: 18 are uncovered, 25 have only one expert, so 43 SDSes (21% of total) cannot receive a fully competent dual-referee review.

**Concentration index (Table 3).** For each UDA, compare the share of that UDA's academics in the **register** to the share of that UDA in **national academic staff** (from MIUR's average 2004–2008 faculty headcount of 39,512 in the hard sciences). The ratio = "concentration index": 1 is "balanced", >1 is oversupplied (e.g. Industrial & Information Engineering = 3.30), <1 is undersupplied (e.g. Medicine = 0.29, Earth science = 0.37). This is the only quantitative balance measure in the paper.

**Cross-colonization check.** Look for SDSes whose *registered* experts mostly come from a different official SDS (e.g. SDS ING-IND/05 has 6 accepted professors, all from other SDSes).

### 4. Research performance (Section 4) — *are the referees actually good scientists?*

**Metric: FSS (Fractional Scientific Strength)** for each of the 956 hard-science academics in the register, over the **2004–2008** quinquennium, defined as

$$\mathrm{FSS} = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{c_i}{me_i}\cdot\frac{1}{s_i}$$

* t = length of the observation window (5 years in this study).
* n = number of publications by the researcher in the window.
* c_i = number of citations received by publication i (citations counted to **30 June 2009** — six months after the last window year).
* me_i = **median** of the distribution of citations received by *all Italian cited-only* publications in the **same year** and in the **same WoS subject category** as publication i. (For multidisciplinary journals the category is replaced by a weighted average across the categories the journal spans.) The median is used instead of the mean because citation distributions are highly skewed.
* s_i = number of co-authors on paper i, with fractional attribution weights *only in the life sciences*: 40/40/20 to first/last/other when first and last author are affiliated to the same university, 30/30/15/15 when they are not, and the remaining 10% split among middle authors.

**Ranking.** FSS_i is compared to the FSS of every Italian university scientist whose official SDS matches the researcher's SDS (the Italian university system is, as the authors note, unique in classifying every professor into exactly one SDS). Output is a national percentile rank, and the headline result table (Table 4) is a 4-quartile histogram by UDA of where the register's 956 academics fall. The cited "national comparison" is therefore within-SDS national distribution, not a global benchmark.

**Data sources (reproducibility).** Raw publication and citation records are Thomson Reuters Web of Science. Per-paper author disambiguation, Italian-affiliation assignment, and publication→individual attribution use a heuristic algorithm of D'Angelo, Giuffrida and Abramo (2011), claimed to mis-attribute less than 5% of publications. The output of that pipeline is the ORP (Osservatorio Produzione della Ricerca) database, a list of all Italian university scientists with their productivity/quality scores — the same database the authors then use to compute each register member's percentile.

### 5. Sardinia field test (Section 5)

For a 2008 call by the Region of Sardinia, 467 proposals were submitted (414 from the two Sardinian universities, 53 from regional research/hospital agencies). 75 referees were drawn from the MIUR register — 69 academics, all resident **outside** Sardinia. Restricted to hard sciences: 382 proposals, 327 unique proponents, 55 referees (73%). "Coverage" is computed as the number of applicant-SDSes vs. referee-SDSes; the difference in this study is 86 of 130 applicant SDSes (58% of the 191 projects in those SDSes) were evaluated by referees from *other* SDSes. Bibliometric profile of the 55 referees: 22 (40%) below the national median, 8 (15%) inactive over the same 5-year window.

## Assumptions the methodology papers over

These are the load-bearing, unexamined or under-examined assumptions the reader should weigh:

1. **Publications as the sole proxy for research output.** Patents, software, data sets, technical reports, clinical work, consulting, PhD supervision, and policy deliverables are all ignored. Justified by a citation to Moeed (2004) claiming publications are followed by content that "avoids double counting" — but no validation is offered that this holds for each of the 9 UDAs, where the mix of patent- vs. paper-driven output varies widely (e.g. industrial engineering vs. fundamental physics).

2. **Citation counts as the impact measure, "notwithstanding possible distortions."** This is stated verbatim rather than mitigated. Citation behaviour varies by age of paper, by sub-field, by self-citation habits, by collaboration size — all of which are *not* controlled for by the year × WoS-subject-category median.

3. **A 6-month citation lag.** Citations are counted to 30 June 2009, six months after the last year of the 2004–2008 window. The 2008 cohort therefore has had far fewer opportunities to accumulate citations than the 2004 cohort, and the year-by-year median scaling does not fully correct this. Recency bias systematically disadvantages the most recent publications of any researcher.

4. **WoS subject category ≈ the field the researcher works in.** The scaling factor is keyed to the *journal's* WoS subject category, not the paper's content. A paper in a multidisciplinary journal is assigned a weighted average of categories; the weights used are not disclosed. A methodological physicist publishing in an applied-math journal is scored as an applied mathematician.

5. **One-and-only-one-SDS rule for every professor.** The whole ranking relies on the Italian peculiarity that all professors belong to exactly one SDS. Cross-disciplinary scientists (the authors' own "cross-colonization" finding is evidence such cross-discipline movement exists) are scored in a single field, and any paper they publish in another field is effectively invisible to the comparison because the comparison pool is restricted to "all national colleagues in the same SDS". The authors acknowledge this is "unique in the world" but treat it as an advantage rather than a distortion risk.

6. **Equal research input per professor.** The FSS is called a "labor productivity" measure, which only makes sense if: (a) all professors in the same field had the same tangible research resources (which the authors justify by saying core funding was input-oriented until 2009); (b) all professors devoted the same fraction of their working time to research. The 2004–2008 observation window *already overlaps* with the start of VTR (2004–2006), the first merit-based 7% component, so the input-orientation assumption is only partially valid during the study period. No sensitivity analysis.

7. **Equal willingness to enter a peer-review call.** The concentration index treats the *national academic-staff share* as the fair-demand denominator, on the assumption that demand for referees in each field is proportional to the size of the field. If demand for referees in basic research is systematically higher (or lower) than for applied fields, the index is mismeasured.

8. **The 5-year window is "robust enough" for an individual.** Stated, not justified. A researcher on parental leave, on an industrial secondment, or in the middle of a long single-project cycle can legitimately show near-zero output over 5 years and still be a good field peer; these are all counted as "inactive" referees.

9. **Author disambiguation error < 5%.** A 5% random misattribution, propagated through a percentile-comparison ranking, is not negligible when a finding is "34% of referees are below the national median" — a 5-point error band makes that number "34 ± 5" rather than a precise statistic. No error bars anywhere in the paper.

10. **The "official SDS" used for register composition (vs. the self-declared SDS used for register membership) is the right lens.** The authors explicitly note, and quietly prefer, the official-SDS classification. This is a defensible choice (it is the system of record, verifiable, and tied to the tenure vote), but it understates the cross-SDS competences that the register *was asked to record* and may therefore understate actual coverage.

11. **"Cross-colonization" is inferred, not validated.** A professor from SDS X who is registered to cover SDS Y is counted as "colonizing" Y. The author may have a legitimate, current research programme in Y (e.g. a physicist working on biophysics). The paper does not cross-check this against the author's published work, only against the official tenure SDS.

12. **Sardinia matching ("85% of projects evaluated by referees from a different SDS") is an estimate, not a measurement.** Confidentiality rules prevented the authors from seeing which specific referee reviewed which specific proposal. They therefore report an estimate, but do not disclose the estimator. The exact figure and the 17% error on it are unknown.

13. **The "top-20%" and "bottom quartile" categories (Tables 4, 5) use the register's own percentile distribution against the national percentile distribution, without testing whether the register is a random or non-random sample of the field.** The 2001 register was assembled by *self-nomination*, so the register population is self-selected; comparing self-selected individuals to national medians assumes the nomination process did not filter for (or against) productivity. The very finding that the register under-represents some UDAs is consistent with self-selection, which the paper does not model.

14. **Hard-sciences-only scope is asserted, not defined operationally.** The 9 UDA list (Math/CS, Physics, Chemistry, Earth sciences, Biology, Medicine, Ag/Vet, Civil Eng., Ind. & IT Eng.) includes the "life sciences" (Biology, Medicine, Ag/Vet) under "hard sciences" while also treating them specially in the FSS (fractional weighting). The boundary is not a hard one — biomedical physics, biochemistry, computational neuroscience are not clearly classified.

15. **Italian-only comparison, no international benchmark.** Explicitly noted as "not feasible yet" due to disambiguation. But the policy question of "is this register good enough to referee national peer-review calls" is inherently a comparison against the *international* frontier, because the projects being evaluated are competing with internationally-competitive proposals. A register where 37% of referees are in the national top quartile may be weak if their national quartile is far from the world frontier.

16. **Register size (1,545) and the 978 Italian subset are not justified.** 567 of the 1,545 register entries are "not Italian". The paper does not discuss how this was decided by MIUR, how non-Italian experts affect coverage of Italian SDSes, or whether the 956-academic subset is itself a self-selection that misses the 46 other hard-science experts (categories B and C) who *are* in the register but not in the 956.

## What a careful reproducer would still not be able to do

* Recompute the exact Sardinia "58%" cross-SDS figure (method undisclosed).
* Recompute the exact FSS percentiles (D'Angelo et al. algorithm, ORP database, and 30-June-2009 WoS snapshot are all required; the WoS snapshot is not archived in a reproducible form).
* Quantify how sensitive the "34% below national median" claim is to the 5% disambiguation error, the 6-month citation lag, or the choice of median vs. mean scaling.
* Reproduce the "cross-colonization" counts without MIUR-internal access to the register.

The methodology is a coherent application of known tools (coverage ratios, FSS, national percentile rankings) to a real, policy-relevant object, and it is more explicit than most single-country studies about which of its assumptions it is making (e.g. "we assume researchers of all the UDAs will participate with equal enthusiasm"). But the empirical claims it reports are only as strong as the assumptions above, and the paper's strongest policy recommendation — replace the bottom-up register with a top-down, query-driven selection from the same ORP/WoS databases — rests on the (unexamined) assumption that a "highly cited in this field at this time" ranking is a better indicator of *peer-review competence* than the register's official SDS classification, which is itself a question the paper does not test head-on.

# Evaluation of "Selecting competent referees to assess research projects proposals: a study of referees' registers" (Abramo, D'Angelo & Viel)

## My understanding of the paper's logical argument

The paper rests on a two‑premise normative claim followed by a two‑pronged empirical test and a policy prescription:

1. **Normative premise.** Trustworthy evaluation of competitively‑funded research projects requires referees who satisfy two conditions simultaneously: (a) their competencies closely match the topics of the proposals being evaluated, and (b) they are excellent (top‑performing) in their fields. Peer review is otherwise exposed to specialization problems, bias, conflicts of interest, and non‑blindness — particularly when referee pools are small, national, and non‑rotated.

2. **Institutional setting.** Italy's MIUR maintains a national expert register (created 2001, used for PRIN/FIRB project calls and by regions) built "bottom‑up": scientists apply, self‑declare competencies in SDS (sector), a commission checks them. The claim is that a static, self‑selected register is structurally at risk on both premises — in coverage (a) and in quality (b).

3. **Prong one — coverage (Section 3).** Two measures:
   - *Binary SDS coverage:* with 205 hard‑science sectors, 18 SDSs have no registered experts and 25 have exactly one (and reviews need at least two referees), so 43/205 sectors (≈21%) of potential projects cannot be adequately evaluated. Medicine is worst. 37 further SDSs are "cross‑colonized" (covered only by outsiders).
   - *Concentration index:* share of register experts per UDA divided by the share of national academic staff in that UDA (the demand benchmark — assuming evaluation demand is proportional to field size). Industrial & information engineering is 3.30× over‑represented; medicine (0.29) and earth science (0.37) badly under‑represented.

4. **Prong two — performance (Section 4).** The 956 academic referees are ranked bibliometrically against all Italian colleagues in the same SDS using *Fractional Scientific Strength* (FSS): fractionally counted publications, citations standardized by year and WoS subject‑category median, divided by years worked, 2004–2008. Findings: 5% inactive, ~20% in the bottom quartile, only ~37% in the top quartile; 66% of civil‑engineering referees are inactive or bottom‑quartile.

5. **Field validation (Section 5).** A 2008 Sardinia call drew 55 hard‑science referees from the register; only 44 of 130 applicant SDSs had a matching referee, so ~58% of applicants were (estimated) evaluated by referees outside their sector, and 40% of the drawn referees were below national‑median performers.

6. **Prescription (Section 6).** Replace bottom‑up self‑declared registers with a top‑down, bibliometric decision‑support process (their ORP, or WoS/Scopus queries) that ranks candidates by field productivity/quality per evaluation need.

The logical chain is clean in form: norm → audit of coverage → audit of quality → real‑world use case → remedy.

## Feedback

**Strengths.**
- The question (are referee registers adequate?) is genuinely underserved in the literature, and getting identity data on actual referees — via the commissioned Sardinia study — is a real data advantage few studies in the field have; the field study in Section 5 is the paper's strongest part because it connects register properties to an actual use.
- The concentration index is simple, transparent, and interpretable, with a sensible benchmark. FSS is standard, well‑established methodology (median standardization, fractional counting, intra‑field comparison), and the authorship reconciliation step (error <5%) is properly credited.
- The authors state their scope limits honestly (no international comparison, confidentiality blocks exact referee–applicant matching, single country).

**Weaknesses in the argument.**

1. **The demand model is assumed, not tested.** The concentration index treats the share of academic staff per UDA as the "desirable" demand for evaluation, explicitly assuming all fields apply to funding calls "with equal enthusiasm." That is plainly false (field propensity to apply, call eligibility rules, historical program emphases — e.g., the 2001 public call itself being over‑subscribed by engineers, plausibly because engineering had the most competitive programs to staff). The 3.30× over‑representation in industrial engineering could partly be *supply matching a real demand*, in which case the "imbalance" finding is attenuated. No sensitivity analysis or call‑history data is used.

2. **Two inconsistent competence criteria, never reconciled.** Table 2 coverage counts self‑declared (but ascertained) competencies across multiple SDSs per expert (average 4.2); Table 3's representativeness count uses the single *official* SDS of tenure. The same register is judged by two different rules that give different pictures, and the binary SDS competence model ignores that many projects are inherently cross‑sector — the very "cross‑colonization" the authors count as a defect may be a legitimate feature of some fields.

3. **Bibliometric performance ≈ referee competence is an unargued equivalence.** The paper never defends why a top‑cited producer makes a good *judge of others' proposals*. Being current, broadly informed, and willing is partly different from being the field's most cited person. Worse, the prescription is self‑contradictory: the paper's own introduction cites the ESF finding of declining willingness to referee, and the bibliometric superstars are precisely the reviewers most likely to decline (over‑requested, least marginally motivated). Selecting referees by the measure that makes them busiest is a tension the paper does not acknowledge.

4. **"Inactive" is not neutralized for confounders.** A WoS‑only, 2004–08 window, no citation time‑decay, and no career‑stage adjustment means that junior appointees in the window, WoS‑poor fields (notably parts of medicine and agriculture, where coverage of Italian‑language or regional literature is spotty), and — crucially — the fact that the register was *built 2001–02 and barely updated* (footnote 3 only) can all inflate the apparent inactivity. A stale register mechanically accumulates retirees; the paper does not separate "stale" from "was‑never‑good."

5. **No outcome analysis.** The whole policy stakes ("the more competent the referee, the more the best proposals are funded") are never connected to evidence: were projects evaluated by mismatched or low‑performance referees scored differently, or funded differently? Without even a correlational test, the 58% cross‑sector review rate in Sardinia is presented as a defect while it may simply be how such calls are managed in practice. The findings‑to‑prescription link is assertoric.

6. **Minor issues.** "This feature of the unique‑in‑the‑world" (single‑field classification) is a strong unsupported claim; the commissioned‑study origin and the critique of the register its commissioner uses is noted only obliquely (a mild, undeclared interest asymmetry); some coverage thresholds (need ≥2 referees per SDS) are asserted rather than derived; the top‑down remedy as described (keyword search, rank by author, time window) would be weakest exactly where matching is hardest — novel and interdisciplinary proposals.

**Overall.** A competent, internally coherent descriptive audit with valuable data access, but whose key quantitative claims rest on untested assumptions (demand–staff proportionality, binary SDS competence, citations as referee quality) and whose recommendation is not supported by outcome evidence and sits in tension with parts of its own motivation. It reads as a useful feasibility/audit demonstration rather than a definitive case.

I've extracted and read the full 18-page PDF. The paper's headline empirical claim is the **Sardinia field analysis: "58% of applicants were evaluated by referees with competencies foreign to their specific [disciplinary sector] SDS"** (Table 6: 191 of 327 hard-science projects fell in one of the 86 SDSs not covered by any of the 55 selected referees; e.g., 83% in Industrial and information engineering). The register analyzed is the 2001 version; a 2010 ministerial decree updating it is acknowledged only in a footnote (fn. 3), and the paper never establishes that the old and new registers differ. The bibliometric data (citations to June 30, 2009; WoS 2004–2008) is a ~3–9-year-old snapshot relative to publication in 2013. For the "is the register adequate *now*" policy question the paper poses, the evidence speaks to a version of the register that may already have been revised.

**Error bars / statistical significance?** **None anywhere.** No confidence intervals, no p-values, no goodness-of-fit or proportion tests. This is the most glaring gap, because two of the most natural tests are cheap:
- Under a fair register a random sample should fall in each national quartile ~25% of the time; the observed distribution (5/20/14/25/37% of 908) is a trivial chi-square test that was apparently never run.
- The Sardinia case study is *tiny* at the UDA level — 2 Physics referees ("all... show performance above the national median" on n=2), 3 Civil engineering, 3 Earth science — yet point estimates and strong qualitative claims ("the most striking case") are reported with no binomial CIs. With n=2, "both above median" is a 25% coin-flip event and is reported as if notable.

The 58% headline survives as a well-documented, arithmetically consistent descriptive finding of one 2008 call in one region, but it overstates what the data can support: no uncertainty, no precision check on referee–project matching, a single snapshot of a register being concurrently updated, and a citation-only performance metric with known misclassification.

**Bottom line:** a thoughtful, internally consistent descriptive audit with a novel object (a national referees' register), but it reads as a report that passed through no statistical scrutiny — no significance tests, no error bars, ad hoc weights, and a performance metric that conflates "no citations" with "inactive." The strongest claim is directionally credible but quantitatively unsupported beyond raw counts.

## [1810.13359v1-weak-point] Weakest link in the argument


## Evaluation: Abramo, D'Angelo & Vieil (2013), "Selecting competent referees to assess research projects proposals: a study of referees' registers"

**The argument chain, compressed:** (1) prepared registers of referees are risky because selection biases distort peer review → (2) therefore the register should be audited on two dimensions: disciplinary **coverage** and scientific **quality** of the listed referees → (3) the audit finds real coverage deficits (18/205 SDSs have no referee, 25 single-expert, Medicine under-represented) and a quality profile that is, per the authors' FSS (Fractional Scientific Strength) metric, no better than the national median and in several UDAs below it (25% inactive or bottom-quartile) → (4) a field case (Sardinia 2008) "confirms the risks," with 58% of applicants allegedly judged by referees "foreign" to their SDSs and 40% of referees below-median → (5) conclusion: abandon bottom-up self-declared registers and replace them with top-down bibliometric decision-support (WoS/ORP keyword search, "rank results by author").

**The single weakest link is step 5's engine: the inference from bibliometric (publication/citation) performance to *refereeing competency*, which carries almost the entire policy recommendation.**

The data never measure anything about how well these referees judge proposals. FSS is a productivity-and-citation proxy; the paper shows some referees published little or received few citations in 2004–2008. That would only support the conclusion "part of the register's members had weak publication records." The paper then repeatedly re-labels this as "a risk of depending on a referee with an **inadequate scientific profile**" and "judgment ... trustworthy," but it provides zero validation of the bridge: that FSS percentile predicts the quality of a referee's assessment of a project (topical fit, currency of knowledge, discriminatory ability). These are different skills; a below-median publisher can be an excellent judge of proposals in a narrow subfield, and a star-citation scholar can be a poor one. Because the recommendation (drop self-declared registers, rank reviewers by bibliometric queries) is justified solely through this unmeasured link, the conclusion stops being entailed wherever the data end — i.e., at the step where "citation performance" silently becomes "competence to referee" and then "the alternative system would be better."

The Sardinia field analysis, which the authors lean on most in the conclusion ("confirms the entity of these risks"), deepens rather than repairs the gap:

1. Its 58%"foreign competencies" figure is computed by matching the *coordinateur's official single-SDS membership* against the *referee's official single-SDS membership* — while the paper itself (Section 3, footnote to Table 1) establishes that experts are accredited in up to ~4.2 SDSs, and that the Ministry *routinely accepts* cross-SDS referees (all six referees of SDS ING-IND/05, 12 of MED/06, come from other SDSs). So the metric counts normal, accepted practice as "mismatch," and the paper admits upfront that confidentiality "makes it impossible to precisely check the alignments."
2. The applicant's SDS is the applicant's tenure field, not the project's actual topic — another proxy-of-a-proxy with unquantified error.
3. There is no counterfactual: the proposed top-down WoS/ORP pipeline is described but never actually run on the Sardinia call, so no data exist showing it would have matched better than the register did.
4. The field sample is tiny (55 referees; several 2–3 person rows in Tables 6–7 where "0%" or "33%" cells are single individuals), so even the direct bibliometric findings there are unstable.

The *coverage* part of the argument is comparatively sound — the concentration-index assumption (evaluation demand proportional to UDA staff size) is declared, it's a benign prior, and the headline gaps (no-referee SDSs) don't depend on it. The *quality* part, and with it the prescriptive payoff of the paper, rides entirely on the unsupported FSS→judgment-quality inference. That is where the conclusion outruns the data.

**Secondary defects** (context, not the chosen weak link): FSS standardization by year-and-subject-category medians and the intra/university author-weighting scheme are asserted, not defended with sensitivity analysis; reliance on a self-declared register to audit itself; and the final claim that commercial databases are "certainly more effective than any register developed through a public call" is stated with no comparative evidence at all.

**Verdict:** a careful, genuinely useful demonstration that the Italian referee register has real *coverage* problems, over-generalizing into a *quality* diagnosis and a policy prescription resting on a proxy (citations) never shown to measure the thing that matters (review competence).

1. **The MIUR referees' register data are not public.** The list was obtained by the authors only because a *Sardinian regional adminstration commissioned the study* (p. 3). 5. **Percentile/quartile binning convention is not stated.** Table 4 uses quartiles, Table 5 uses 80th/50th percentiles, and Table 7 uses "under the national median." No statement of: rank-based (`rank/N·100`) vs. empirical CDF, inclusive/exclusive boundaries, or tie-handling. Small differences in these rules shift who lands in the 43%-inactive (Civil Engineering) cell.

So the flagship finding that "58% of Sardinia applicants were evaluated by referees in a different SDS" is a margin-comparison estimate and cannot be reproduced from a full matching even if you had both registers.

7. **Software, environment, and versioning are absent.** No language (R/Python/SAS/Matlab), no package versions, no random-seed issues (none should arise, but no seed is declared), no reproducibility tag, no Open Science Framework / Dryad / Zenodo deposit.

8. **Register-creation details are thin.** The MIUR "public call", the 2001 commission evaluation, and the 2012 ministerial update (footnote 3) are referenced by URLs that are now dead; no snapshot of the register schema or the self-declared-competency records is provided. - Compute: the workload is trivial (a few thousand referees × tens of thousands of publications); any modern laptop can re-run the FSS arithmetic given the data.

- "Prompts / pretraining": N/A; no LLM is used.

The **methodology** is written down well enough that *if the data existed*, a competent bibliometrician could recompute FSS, the quartile distributions (Table 4), and the concentration index (Table 3). ## [1810.13359v1-writing-framing] Writing and framing critique


# Critique: writing and framing of Abramo, D'Angelo & Viel (2013), *Research Evaluation* 22(1), 41–51

The paper is competent and has one genuinely solid descriptive contribution: a concrete account of **coverage gaps** in Italy's bottom‑up MIUR referee register (18 of 205 hard‑science SDSs have *zero* registered experts; 43 have a *single* expert; 25 are "covered by a single expert"). That finding is concrete, defensible, and directly tied to the register's actual rules. The rest of the paper is where the framing outruns the evidence. The core problem is a **triple conflation** the authors never validate:

> bibliometric productivity (FSS) = scientific competence = trustworthy reviewing.

Every headline and every policy recommendation rests on that equation, and no sentence in the paper defends it or cites it.

## Sentences that overclaim

- **Abstract:** *"the greater the competence of the referee concerning the core topic, the more their judgment is trustworthy and the more the financing of the best proposals is probable."* This is the load‑bearing causal chain (topic‑competence → trustworthy judgment → best proposals funded) stated as self‑evident, with no citation. It conflates "published a lot in the topic" with "judges proposals well" — a senior, highly cited scientist can still be a poor evaluator of novelty, feasibility, or fit. The entire empirical apparatus below is built to measure the *first* term and then quietly read off the *last*.

- **Abstract / §4:** *"to evaluate the level of scientific performance of the register's referees… compared to the their national colleagues."* "Scientific performance" is defined purely as citation‑weighted WoS publication output. Using a single citation‑based indicator (FSS) at a single snapshot (citations frozen at 30 June 2009) and then labelling it "scientific performance" is an overclaim; it is a proxy for a particular slice of output, not performance, not competence.

- **Introduction:** *"the selection process must adhere to two fundamentals: … 2) excellence of the referees in their fields of research."* Presents "research excellence" as a *normative* criterion for referee quality, again without argument. This is a position the literature disputes (review quality is not strongly tied to own productivity), not a settled fundamental.

- **Introduction:** *"it is quite possible that the evaluation process would be totally 'blinded', not be able to express any competent judgment."* "Totally blinded" is rhetorically charged; the hedges ("quite possible") do not offset a claim that no competent judgment is attainable, which the paper never demonstrates.

- **§4.1:** *"In fact, in the Italian university system all professors are classified in one and only one field. **This feature of the Italian higher education system is unique in the world.**"* An unqualified, almost certainly false superlative, offered in support of a methodological convenience (clean within‑field ranking). This is the single most flagrant unsupported sentence in the paper.

- **§4.2:** *"we see that 5% of the referees on the list… show **nil performance**, either for lack of publications over the five years under observation or for lack of citations to works they may have published."* "Nil performance" conflates "no WoS‑indexed, cited output in 2004–2008" with "no performance." A senior (category A/B) full professor or research‑institution director with reports, patents, books, or clinical/engineering deliverables outside WoS can be exactly that and not be "nil." The label converts a database‑coverage gap into a competence verdict.

- **§4.2 / Conclusion:** the repeated framing that **34% below the national median** (and the 20% last quartile) indicates an *"inadequate scientific profile"* / *"a risk that evaluation of projects would be entrusted to experts with an inadequate scientific profile."* Falling below the field median means being *average‑minus* — not inadequate. The authors re‑label the lower half of an ordinary distribution as "inadequate" without ever tying a percentile to an actual standard of evaluating competence. The choice of *median* as the adequacy bar is asserted, not defended.

- **§3:** *"there is a real possibility that a small group of referees from other SDSs would **dominate** the evaluation of proposed projects and articles."* "Dominate" is strong for describing two SDSs (ING‑IND/05, 6 out‑of‑field referees; MED/06, 12); the data show cross‑colonization, not demonstrated dominance of particular evaluations.

- **§4.1:** *"it is reasonable to hold that the SDS of official membership is the one where they show greatest competence in evaluation."* This assumption is quietly under‑mined by the *same paper*, which in §3 documents systematic "cross‑colonization" (experts officially in SDL X applying to and being accepted in SDL Y). So the FSS within‑official‑SDS comparison may be exactly where the field mis‑specification lives — an internal inconsistency the authors flag in one section and ignore in the other.

- **Conclusion:** *"it is possible to have decision support systems and **unequivocal identification of who does what and how well, in every field of knowledge**."* "Unequivocal" and "in every field" are maximal claims that contradict the paper's own caveats (they only address hard sciences; they cite Glänzel on citation distortions; they say disambiguating foreign authorships is "not feasible yet"). 

- **Conclusion:** *"decision makers should seriously consider adopting these **more effective, methods** to support their choices in the **very awarding of research financing**."* This is the paper's biggest overclaim. The study never compares outcomes of referee‑based vs. WoS‑based selection, never shows that the register *as used* produced worse funding decisions, and never tests the competence→funding link. "More effective" and "very awarding of research financing" assert an effect the evidence does not establish. The paper shows *coverage* and *bibliometric performance* gaps; it does not show that those gaps changed which projects were funded or how well they were judged.

- **Conclusion:** *"When referees are to be chosen in the international context, **databases such as WoS and Scopus would serve the same purpose**."* Directly tensions with §4.1's own admission that an international comparison "is not feasible yet, because of the difficulty of disambiguating foreign authorships." The recommendation to extrapolate to international selection (and further to *journal editors*) is the farthest the paper reaches from the two Italian datasets it actually has.

## Smaller framing weaknesses (balance and honesty where they appear)

- The **case study is n=55 referees / 327 subjects**, and UDA‑level "notable cases" are drawn from cells of 2–9 referees (Table 7: Agriculture n=8, Physics n=2, Chemistry n=8). The authors are honest that *"confidentiality rules make it impossible to precisely check"* the 58% figure — good. But they then present sub‑UDA percentiles as "notable" findings while the cells are single digits.
- The **concentration index** (register share ÷ national‑staff share) presupposes evaluation *demand* is proportional to staff size ("We assume researchers of all the UDAs will participate with equal enthusiasm"). The "over/under‑sized" verdict (Medicine 0.29, Industrial engineering 3.30) is assumption‑driven, though the authors do flag the assumption.
- The register was built by **self‑nomination**, so field imbalance is partially a reflection of who chose to self‑declare — the paper frames self‑selection as a *defect* without acknowledging it may be a neutral property of a bottom‑up register.

On the positive side, the authors do use appropriate hedges in places ("a more informative… comparison… is not feasible yet," "confidentiality rules make it impossible…"), and the coverage gap (43/205 SDSs) is the real, citable contribution.

## What an adversarial reviewer would put in their first paragraph

> The paper's central claim — that a bibliometric reading of a referee's publication record and citation profile can certify that a register of experts is "adequate," and that substituting Web‑of‑Science–based selection will lead to the "financing of the best proposals" — rests on a causal chain (productivity → competence → trustworthy review → better funding decisions) that the authors never state as a hypothesis, never cite, and above all never test. The empirical sections measure only the first link (coverage and citation‑weighted FSS) and then silently treat the last as if it had been measured. Three distinct constructs — bibliometric output, scientific competence, and reviewing competence — are used interchangeably throughout. Worse, the "problems" identified are in large part artefacts of the chosen instruments: "nil" performance is defined as absence of WoS‑indexed, cited output, a below‑median FSS percentile is labelled an "inadequate scientific profile," and the authors' own "unique in the world" claim rests on an unverified and very likely false factual assertion. The only analysis not dependent on these unvalidated leaps is the coverage gap (18 empty, 43 single‑expert SDSs) — a modest but real descriptive result — whereas the headline policy recommendation ("decision makers should seriously consider adopting these more effective methods… in the very awarding of research financing") is advanced with no counterfactual, no outcome comparison, and no demonstration that the register as actually used produced poor evaluations. In short, the paper measures where a register is thin and how registered referees rank by citations; it has not shown — and its framing repeatedly implies — that citation‑based selection would make any funding decision better. Until that gap is closed, the descriptive findings (respectable) should be separated from the normative programme (unearned).

---

**Summary.** A competent, internally‑consistent descriptive study with one strong, defensible finding (register coverage gaps) undermined by an over‑ambitious normative framing that conflates bibliometrics, competence and review quality, relies on unvalidated causal assumptions, uses two or three unqualified factual superlatives, and makes policy recommendations the data never support.

## [1810.13359v1-expert-reviewer] Expert Reviewer


# Peer Review

**Manuscript:** *"Selecting competent referees to assess research projects proposals: a study of referees' registers"* (Abramo, D'Angelo, Viel)

**Recommendation: Minor-to-moderate revisions (borderline accept, as currently framed)**

## Summary

The paper studies the adequacy of the national register of experts (MIUR, Italy, created 2001) used to select referees for competitively funded research projects (PRIN/FIRB). It proposes a two-part audit methodology: (i) a "coverage" analysis against the spectrum of disciplinary sectors (SDSs), including a ratio-index that compares the register's disciplinary composition to the national academic staff distribution; and (ii) a bibliometric assessment (Fractional Scientific Strength, FSS: fractional, citation-median-standardized publication counts per researcher-year, compared within same-SDS national peers) of referee performance. A field case — the 2008 Sardinia regional call (467 projects, 75 referees drawn from the register) — tests the actual consequences of register inadequacy. Main results: 43/205 SDSs lack adequate coverage (18 empty, 25 single-referee); the register is heavily skewed (concentration index 3.30 for Industrial/information engineering vs. 0.29 for Medicine); ~25% of academic referees were inactive (5%) or in the bottom quartile (20%) nationally; and in Sardinia, 58% of applicants in uncovered SDSs were evaluated by referees from other SDSs and 40% of referees performed below the national median.

## Strengths

1. **Genuinely under-studied question.** Referee registers are the operational backbone of competitive funding in many countries, yet their composition has essentially never been empirically audited. The authors correctly note the literature gap, and the coverage/concentration framework (register distribution vs. staff distribution; empty and single-occupied sectors; "cross-colonization," e.g. ING-IND/05 and MED/06 staffed entirely by outsiders) is a useful, transferable audit protocol. For a prestigious forum, the Sardinia case study is valuable precisely because it connects register properties to actual assignment outcomes, not just to static composition.

2. **Solid, documented bibliometric method.** Within-field comparison, fractional counting, and median (not mean) citation standardization by year and subject category are all defensible, and the authors cite the skew literature (Lundberg 2007) for the median choice.

3. **Honest about scope and mechanism.** The Italian single-SDS classification is exploited as a natural control for field, the input-oriented (equalized) funding regime is used to justify the resource-homogeneity assumption, and the authors disclose that access to the register came via a regional commission.

## Major weaknesses

1. **The central causal chain is never validated.** The paper's premise — "the greater the competence of the referee concerning the core topic, the more their judgment is trustworthy and the more the financing of the best proposals is probable" — is asserted, not tested. "Bibliometrically productive" is taken as proxy for "competent to judge," and "SDS mismatch" as proxy for "inadequate evaluation," but no evidence is offered that low-performing referees actually gave worse judgments, or that mismatched referees gave worse judgments than matched ones (e.g., agreement with ex-post outcomes, calibration, or a blinded re-assessment of the Sardinia files, which the authors plainly had access to). Without at least one such validation, the study is a descriptive audit whose interpretive leap to "risk to funding quality" rests on unexamined literature.

2. **Cross-field referral is treated as uniformly negative.** Interdisciplinarity is a real and growing feature of funded research; a project at the boundary of two fields may *benefit* from a second referee in an adjacent sector. The analysis cannot distinguish a MED/04 referee reviewing a MED/06 proposal from a physicist reviewing a biochemistry proposal. The 58% "mismatch" figure, and the 83% for Industrial/information engineering, would be substantially re-interpreted by an adjacency analysis. As written, the metric conflates two very different events.

3. **Arbitrary, unvalidated author-weighting scheme.** The 40/20 (intra-mural first/last) vs. 30/15/10 (extra-mural) life-science co-authorship weights were "assigned following advice from Italian professors." No sensitivity analysis shows how much the quartile classifications in Table 4 change under other defensible schemes, and equal-split fractional counting (the norm) would move referee positions materially. Similarly, the claim that citation standardization by year × journal category is distortion-free at the individual level ignores self-citations, citation-halves and the well-known lag differences between fast- (e.g., CS) and slow-moving (e.g., civil engineering) fields — the very fields where the authors find the worst referee performance. Some of the "inactive" civil-engineering referees (17 of 40) may reflect a field that publishes differently, not inactivity.

4. **Small-cell statistics presented as percentages.** Sardinia cells include 3 referees in civil engineering, 2 in physics, 3 in earth science (Tables 6–7); Table 2 medicine coverage percentages are based on 85 experts. No confidence intervals or even binomial proportions are given. Statements like "the risk is significantly high" are not supported by any significance testing; the concentration indices likewise need uncertainty bounds.

5. **The evidence base is stale even at publication time, and the preprint is re-posted.** The register was built 2001–02, publications cover 2004–2008, the field case is 2008, and the arXiv version (1810.xxxx) is a 2018 re-post of the 2013 *Research Evaluation* paper — the paper itself footnotes that the register was updated by a 2010 ministerial decree, which it ignores. A reviewer must ask how much of the picture survives the update; a short "does this still hold?" paragraph, or a 2016/2017 re-check, would be expected of a current journal version.

6. **Conflict of interest in the recommended remedy.** The constructive recommendation — replace bottom-up self-declared registers with top-down bibliometric selection, "using the same database as in the bibliometric analysis of this work" — is an endorsement of the ORP, a database the authors built and operate. The paper should (a) declare this plainly, (b) benchmark ORP output against an independent WoS/Scopus-derived ranking on the same 956 academics (the disambiguation "error < 5%" claim, D'Angelo et al. 2011, is not independently re-verified here), and (c) acknowledge practical objections (WoS journal-category fielding misclassifying interdisciplinary referees, databases missing gray/low-impact venues, and the governance question of a ministry trusting a vendor database with award decisions).

7. **Minor issues:** the sentence "This feature of the Italian higher education system is unique in the world" is asserted without support; the reference list has "Moxam" while the text cites "Moxham" (and the source title is *Research Policy*, not *Science and Technology Policy*); the "MATERIALS PHSYSICS" typo in the quoted MIUR description is evidently from the source but should be marked `[sic]`; and the FSS formula's "t = number of years of work of the researcher in the period of observation" is never operationalized for mid-career entrants.

## Verdict

A well-executed first empirical audit of a referee register, with a reusable methodology and genuinely useful descriptive findings (empty and single-occupied sectors; severe disciplinary skew; a fifth of referees with weak or nil recent output). But as a contribution to a prestigious journal it stops short of validating the inference from "inadequate register composition" to "impairment of funding decisions," treats interdisciplinary referral as unqualified harm, leans on an arbitrary life-science weighting with no sensitivity check, and promotes the authors' own database as the remedy without a declared conflict of interest or independent benchmarking. Addressing items 1–6 — at minimum a validation exercise on the Sardinia files, an adjacency analysis, sensitivity of the life-science weights, and a COI paragraph — would move this from a competent descriptive study to a persuasive one.

## What the paper does
It audits the Italian MIUR "referees' register" — the pool of experts drawn on to select peer reviewers for competitively-funded R&D (PRIN/FIRB). Two questions: (1) *coverage* — does the register span the full discipline spectrum (956 academics across 205 SDSs in 9 hard-science UDAs); and (2) *quality* — do the referees' outputs compare to national colleagues in the same field, using a bibliometric productivity metric (Fractional Scientific Strength, FSS). The Sardinia 2008 call is a natural-experiment case study.

## Genuine strengths
- **Honest scoping.** The authors repeatedly disclaim that they do *not* indict the Italian system specifically — they are offering a reusable audit methodology. That self-awareness is the right posture for a policy/evaluation-studies paper and it reads credibly.
- **Good concrete indicators.** The "covered by a single expert" column, the concentration index (register share ÷ national-staff share), and the Sardinia "58% of applicant projects reviewed by a referee in a different SDS" are all concrete, falsifiable, and memorable. The Sardinia case is the paper's strongest empirical point: it shows how much risk *compounds* when a small, national, self-declared register is then downscaled for regional use.
- **Authoritative data and transparent assumptions.** ORP/WoS is the right source; affiliation matching error is stated (<5%); assumptions (equal resources, equal research hours, single-field classification) are spelled out and defended (input-oriented funding, regulated teaching load).
- **Methodologically standard and defensible.** Median (not mean) citation normalization with justification for skew (Lundberg), fractional co-author credit, within-field percentile comparison — all appropriate choices given field-normalization.

## The core weaknesses (what you should press on)
1. **The citation window is too short to support the inactivity claims.** You observe 2004–2008 output but count citations only *accumulated to 30 June 2009*. A paper from 2008 has ≤~1 year of citation accretion. In high-cite fields this biases the 2008 cohort down and inflates "below-median/inactive." "Five years seems sufficient" is true for *counting output*, not for *accumulating citations*. At minimum, (a) truncate on calendar year and note the left-truncation, or (b) use a longer accretion window (your data are historical — you could push to 2011–12 and it would strengthen the paper).
2. **Declared competencies vs. official single-SD — an internal inconsistency.** Coverage (Sec. 3) is built on the *self-declared, multi-SD* profile (avg 4.2 SDSs). Performance (Sec. 4) benchmarks each referee against the national median of their *single official tenure SD*. These are two different definitions of a referee's field, used to draw the same "inadequate" conclusion. A referee over-credited on coverage may be under/over-penalized on FSS depending on which SD is chosen. This must be reconciled or at least flagged as confounding the two sections.
3. **WoS-indexing bias will masquerade as "inactivity."** The paper shows Civil Engineering 66%, IIE 43%, Agriculture 14% "inactive/below-median" — exactly the engineering/applied areas with historically *lower* WoS coverage (technical reports, industrial standards, locally-indexed journals). Part of this may be a database-coverage artefact, not true inactivity. The authors explicitly use "or did not receive citations to works they may have published" (fn 18) — that's an admission that "inactive" is a lower bound on *observed* inactivity. Acknowledge this as a ceiling on the claim rather than a footnote.
4. **Small cells are over-interpreted.** "66% of Civil Engineering referees are inactive" is 2 of 3 people; Sardinia IIE has 9 referees. Several bold per-UDA percentages rest on n < 10. Report the counts next to every percentage (Table 4/7 do have them) and soften any per-UDA narrative claim resting on tiny denominators.
5. **"Below national median" ≠ "inadequate referee."** The conclusion repeatedly equates sub-median performance with "inadequate scientific profile." The median is, by definition, split down the middle — half of *every* national field is below its median. Finding 34% of referees below-median is unremarkable unless you show it is *disproportionate* relative to some expectation, or you show that referees drawn at random from the population would be better. Right now there is no null model: you should compare the referee distribution to the distribution you'd get by *randomly* sampling 956 academics in the same SDSs. That is the single highest-value addition — it would tell you whether the register is actually *worse* than its source population or merely *median-ish*.
6. **Soft sciences are dropped entirely.** The register covers all fields, yet the analysis restricts to 956 hard-science academics. That's defensible methodologically (WoS coverage, field-comparability), but the coverage problem is structurally *worse* in soft sciences (more SDSs, more self-declaration ambiguity); a one-paragraph extrapolation would strengthen the policy punch without new data.

- What does *random* sampling of the same 956 slots yield? (Builds the null model, point 5.)
- Can we extend the citation accretion window to ~2012 and re-run? (Point 1.)
- Can we compute FSS on the *declared multi-SD* profile instead of the single official SD, and does the conclusion change? (Point 2.)

## Net assessment
This is a **competent, honest, well-executed applied policy study**. The data are strong, the framing is responsible, and the Sardinia case is its best contribution. It is **not** a methodological advance — every bibliometric tool (median normalization, fractional credit, percentile ranking) is pre-existing (FSS is the authors' own 2011 work), demonstrated in a single country, with a short citation window and an unresolved declared-vs-official-field inconsistency. For the subcommunity of research-evaluation/bibliometric audit, it is genuinely useful and the top-down recommendation was taken up in practice; but its influence is localized and the toolkit was already known.

The one change I'd actually demand before publication is the **random-sampling null model** — without it, "below the median" is doing more rhetorical work than the analysis technically supports. The rest are caveats to add, not re-runs.
