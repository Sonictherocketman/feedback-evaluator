# Review of "Faint High-Energy Gamma-Ray Photon Emission of GRB 081006A from *Fermi* Observations"

## What the paper argues

The central claim is that GRB 081006A is a bona‑fide faint high‑energy (LAT) GRB that was missed by the standard LAT pipeline. The authors reach it by combining two independent techniques — the matched‑filter method of Ackerlof et al. 2010 (A10/A11) and a maximum‑likelihood (`glike`/science tools) verification — on a 74‑GRB sample defined by relaxing the GBM fluence floor below 5.9 erg/cm². The supporting statistics: 13 high‑energy photons, a maximum‑likelihood TS of 45 (~4.6σ), a matched weight of 652 (~26× the next event), a claimed false‑positive probability ≲10⁻⁴, and a joint GBM+LAT Band‑function fit spanning 8 keV–1 GeV. The comparison target is the pair of longest‑duration faint LAT GRBs, 080825C and 090217A.

This is a competent, honest detection paper. The two‑technique strategy, the pre‑burst + random‑field background checks, and the careful separation of statistical from systematic localization error are all handled well. That said, several of the quantitative claims lean harder on the data than the data supports.

## Main points of critique

**1. The detection is marginal and the framing oversells it.** The headline "unambiguously detected" rests on only 13 high‑energy photons (Table 2) and a TS of 45. This is at the *floor* of what counts as a real LAT detection. The matched‑filter statistic looks spectacular (652, "26× larger"), but the authors themselves note it is a fast screening tool, not a likelihood‑equivalent measure (p. 5). Leading with the matched weight is misleading; the actual discovery significance is the ~4.6σ likelihood result, which is respectable but not compelling for a field‑defining claim.

**2. Look‑elsewhere / selection‑bias handling is incomplete.** The random‑field comparisons (2192 fields, 17,200 weight‑fields) address the "how surprising is this field" question for *a fixed position*, but the position was *not* fixed a priori — it was found by the matched filter. Because the likelihood localization is derived from the same 13 photons, the significance of the position‑dependent TS of 45 does not fully account for the trials factor over the allowed positions/offsets. The paper should be explicit that the TS=45 is a "given the location" statistic, not a sky‑wide one.

**3. The "matched filter is 3× / 50× more effective" comparisons conflate distinct quantities.** On p. 5 the authors argue the matched filter is "at least three times more effective" by comparing 27/2192 < 45 and 17,200/74 < 652, then jump to "at least 50 times better" via extrapolated parameter fits (dotted lines in Fig. 4). The "3×" mixes a count of fields producing a given TS against a count producing a given weight — the two statistics are not on the same scale, so the ratio is not a sensitivity ratio. The "50×" is an extrapolation from fitted trends on heavy‑tailed, small‑sample distributions, presented in the figure with large error bands. This is the paper's most consequential methodological claim (it's the basis for recommending the technique for future faint‑GRB searches) and it is not backed by a direct measurement.

**4. Background modeling is thin for a faint event.** The background rejection uses pre‑burst data "re‑scaled" to the 50 s window, yielding ≤3 photons above 100 MeV in the background (p. 5). With only ~3 background counts to calibrate a re‑scaling factor, and an event ~120 s before the SAA where the rate is variable, this background normalization carries unquantified systematic uncertainty. For a 4.6σ event, a few‑percent background misestimate is not negligible.

**5. The spectral interpretation overreaches the statistics.** The Band‑function E_peaκ = 81.7 with ⁺⁸²⁷₋₃₄₀ keV (i.e., essentially unconstrained upward) and the flux 6.06±1.61 ×10⁻³ cm⁻² s⁻¹ are dominated by the GBM; only ~4–5 of the 13 LAT photons sit in the 100 MeV–1 GeV band that actually constrains the high‑energy shape. The discussion claim that "no cut‑off feature is seen in the LAT energy range, suggesting a single mechanism for both GBM and LAT emission" is therefore weak: there are not enough high‑energy photons to *exclude* a cutoff, and the "5‑decade joint fit" does not demonstrate coherence at the 100 MeV junction — it is a single smooth function forced through GBM‑dominated bins. The statement should be downgraded to "consistent with" rather than "suggesting a single mechanism."

**6. The time‑averaged (−1 to 8 s) fit washes out the temporal structure the discussion relies on.** The claims of a "delayed onset" of high‑energy emission and of correlation with the second GBM peak (§4) are made by eye‑balling the light curve (Fig. 2), not from a fitted model. With only 13 photons binned over ~40 s, the individual high‑energy photon arrival times (13.25 s and 26.5 s for the two hardest photons) should be treated as suggestive, not as evidence of a coherent delayed high‑energy component.

**7. Minor/consistency items.** The LAT localization (RA 135.53, Dec −61.65) is offset from the GBM position (RA 134.4, Dec −61.8) by ~1.1° in RA, described in §2 as "6.6σ away... thus demonstrating consistency at the 1σ level" — but the LAT 1σ uncertainty is 4.5′ while the GBM is 4.5′, so a ~1.1° separation is actually ≫1σ of the *combined* error; the paper's phrasing is too generous about how well localizations agree (it is consistent, but not at 1σ). Table 2 lists a distance "a" from the new location of 2.29° for photon 6, well outside the 1° radius used for cluster association, so it is unclear whether all 13 photons in the Table are within the association region or whether the count mixes clustered and non‑clustered photons.

## Net assessment

The paper does what it says: it applies two complementary techniques correctly and responsibly, and GRB 081006A does appear to be a real faint LAT detection. But it is an *incremental* catalog addition (one more short GRB in the 74 faint sample), and the parts of the argument that would elevate it — the "matched filter is 50× more sensitive" recommendation and the "single emission mechanism" interpretation — are supported by statistics weaker than the prose implies. Tightening those two claims and clarifying the look‑elsewhere handling would make the argument as strong as its data allow.

## [1201.3844v1-expert-collegue-review] Expert Collegue Review


# Peer Review: "Faint high-energy gamma-ray photon emission of GRB 081006A from Fermi observations" (Zheng et al.)

## Summary of the work

This paper reports the detection of GRB 081006A in high-energy photons (>100 MeV) by Fermi/LAT, the fourth unambiguous LAT detection of a GRB using the matched filter technique after Akerlof et al. (2010). Thirteen high-energy photons are detected from a burst with a low GBM fluence (~0.26 µerg/cm², below the 5 µerg/cm² threshold normally applied to the standard GBM sample). The authors combine (i) a matched filter analysis of an extended sample of 74 low-fluence GBM GRBs, (ii) an FSRC-verified maximum-likelihood (Test Statistic) analysis of 16,088 random fields to estimate the false-positive rate, (iii) a GBM-LAT joint Band function spectral fit spanning 8 keV to 0.8 GeV, and (iv) a comparison of temporal and spectral properties with the other three long-duration GRBs with similar fluence (080825C, 090217A, 0902228A / 081024B). They find a TS of 45 (~4.6σ) for the candidate event and claim a false-positive probability at least ~50 times smaller (order 10⁻⁵) in the matched filter statistic.

## Strengths

1. **The result is genuinely noteworthy for its time.** At the point of submission, only three GRBs showed LAT detection via the matched filter technique, and this was the first from a burst so faint in the GBM band. The "surprising death of faint GRBs" theme — that high-energy photon production is not a rare, luminosity-gated phenomenon — is a valuable early empirical data point, and the authors state the community case well (the Swift/Fermi overlap-urgency point is a fine motivation).

2. **Methodological care in background estimation.** The main strength of the paper is its statistical hygiene. The authors did *not* simply run the likelihood analysis on the candidate field and stop there. They ran the same maximum-likelihood analysis on 16,088 random fields (and 1,751 GBM-co-timed random fields) to build the null distribution of TS and matched weights, then derived the candidate's position on that distribution and extrapolated the tail, including estimated statistical uncertainties on those extrapolations. That is the right way to convert a small-sample detection into an honest significance, and it is better than what a lot of the GRB literature did at the time. The cross-checks — pre-burst data, TRANSIENT class only, K-S test of the two random samples, exclusion of the one outlier random field — are all sensible and are actually done.

3. **Consistency between two independent localizations.** The matched-filter localization (RA 134.4°, Dec −61.8°) and the gtfindsrc TS-map localization (RA 135.53°, Dec −61.65°) agree within 1σ (0.55°), and the GBM location is within 6.6° of the LAT position. This is a solid three-way consistency check on the association, and the authors note SAA proximity and the corresponding slower background fluctuation, which could otherwise have muddied the background estimate.

4. **The joint spectral fit is clean.** Five decades in one Band function (E_peak ≈ 81.7 keV, α ≈ −0.78, β ≈ −2.28, flux 2.18×10⁻⁶ ph cm⁻² s⁻¹) connecting GBM (8 keV–4 MeV) to LAT. They tried PL, PL-with-cutoff, two-component Band+PL, and Band+thermal, and report the Band function as best, with no LAT cutoff. This is a reasonable, if not groundbreaking, spectral result.

5. **Honest comparison of the two techniques.** The statement that the matched filter and the maximum-likelihood method are *complementary* — matched filter cheaper for the discovery scan, maximum likelihood better for characterization once a candidate is found and the background is well known — is the correct operational conclusion and is supported by the numbers they present.

## Weaknesses and concerns

1. **The "50× more sensitive" claim rests on an extrapolation, and it's the load-bearing result of the paper, but it's thinly supported.** The claim that the matched filter is "at least three times more effective" (from the 153 vs. 4.6σ phrasing) and "at least 50 times better" for background rejection comes from comparing the candidate's TS/weight ranks against two random-field samples and extrapolating the tails with a power-law / exponential fit. With the 27-GRB GBM sample and 16,088 random fields, the tail of the distribution where the candidate lives is extrapolated, not measured. They do show uncertainty bands (dotted/cyan lines in Fig. 4), and that is to their credit, but the "50×" figure is presented in the prose (Section 5) with a definite degree of confidence not matched by the statistical power of the comparison. A reviewer would want: the actual functional form of the extrapolation, the fit range, and a formal confidence statement on the ratio of the tail rates, rather than "consistent within the same criteria" + "at least."

2. **Thirteen photons. Very few.** The whole detection rests on 13 photons, of which only 3 fall on the highest-weight triplet cluster used for localization. Table 2 shows that several of the 13 photons have small individual weights, and the *total* matched weight (652,460) is dominated by a small number of photons (e.g., photon #3, 138,829, and #11 with the flagged highest triplet weight, 652,460 is essentially the sum of the top three). The localization uncertainty (0.4″/0.6″/0.9″ for 68/90/99%) is quoted from the TS map, but that map is built on 13 events. The effective pointing constraint from 13 photons, even at the energies shown (150–770 MeV, with two at ~780 MeV), is not as tight as the quoted errors imply when you consider the small-number statistics. The paper doesn't discuss the systematic floor on the LAT position for such a low photon count; it only mentions the ~2.5° added systematic for the matched-filter solution and not one for the gtfindsrc map.

3. **The spectral fit "not well constrained" on E_peak.** The authors admit E_peak is not well constrained, which is expected with 13 photons spanning 150–780 MeV — the LAT only samples a small slice of the Band function's high-energy side. But then the claim that "the Band function model fit is clearly better than a single power-law or a power-law with exponential cutoff" rests on a χ²/dof = 464/379, which is not a good fit by conventional standards (χ²/dof ≈ 1.2). With only 13 data points (the LAT bins) and this many degrees of freedom, the model comparison is underpowered. They do not give the Δχ² or likelihood ratio between the Band function and the PL/cutoff models, which is what would actually be needed to claim a "clearly better" fit. This is a standard but important omission.

4. **No flux/fluence comparison table for the four GRBs.** The discussion (Section 4) compares GRB 081006A to 080825C and 090217A on several axes in prose (delay time, duration, E_peak, fluence) but never tabulates the key numbers together. A reader wanting to verify "more similar to 080825C than to 090217A" has to hunt through three references. A single comparison table (T90, GBM fluence, Band E_peak, LAT fluence, LAT delay, LAT duration) would have made the Section 4 argument much more credible.

5. **The comparison set is small and somewhat circular.** Three comparison GRBs, one of which (080825C) is the canonical "first matched-filter LAT GRB," makes the "081006A is more like 080825C" conclusion partly selection-driven. The claim that "there appears to be a surprising death of faint LAT GRBs" is drawn from ~4 data points out of a 74-GBM burst sample in which only one was detected. The detection rate statement (4/74 vs. 5/157 or similar) is not fully quantified — the paper mentions "~6 high energy photons above 100 MeV" for the matched-filter sample above threshold and "about 13" for 081006A, but never states a formal "detection rate of X% above fluence threshold Y" that would let a reader test the rarer-than-expected claim quantitatively. The urgency framing ("surprising death of faint GRBs") is a bit strong for n≈4.

6. **Minor issues.** The reference to "A10" and "A11" as the two Akerlof papers is fine internally but the first-occurrence expansion could be cleaner; the abstract says "3 more GRBs have also shown evidence" but the body treats 080825C, 090217A and 090228A as three plus 080905A/091208B/090228A as a further set — count of "3" vs. "5" GRBs is slightly muddled between abstract (3) and Section 1 (5 listed). The time window used (0–50 s after trigger) for the LAT analysis is "almost the same as in A10, A11" which is fine but a bit too loose a specification for a reproducibility-sensitive claim.

## Overall assessment

This is a competent, honest, and well-executed observational paper from a very early and small-sample era of Fermi GRB science. The detection itself is secure by the standards of the time (TS=45 with a properly-constructed null distribution), the localization is consistent across independent methods, and the joint spectral coverage is a nice showcase of the GBM+LAT synergy. The main limitations are inherent to the data (13 photons) rather than to the authors' analysis — they do not overclaim the spectral model, and they are explicit that E_peak is not well constrained. The statistical framing of the matched-filter advantage ("50×") is the one place where I think the paper reaches slightly ahead of what the tail extrapolation can strictly support, and the "surprising death of faint GRBs" framing is a bit ahead of n=4.

As a 2011-era paper this sits squarely in the solid-observational-paper category: useful, correct, and appropriately measured, but not a methodological or interpretive breakthrough. Its historical value — being the fourth matched-filter LAT GRB, and the first from a sub-µerg fluence burst — is real but was quickly overtaken by the much larger Fermi/LAT GRB catalogs (Abdo et al. 2009/2010/2013, and the ~55-GRB Fermi catalog) that superseded both the rarity framing and the "surprising death" narrative within a year or two of publication.

I would not recommend rejecting this; I would recommend minor revision focused on softening the "50×" claim to a properly-quantified statement with the extrapolation assumptions stated, adding the four-GRB comparison table, and giving the Δχ² numbers behind the Band-vs-PL model comparison.

With a serious edit of §3.2 and the summary, the prose would sit comfortably within the norms of the field; as it stands it would go a second editorial pass.

# Critique of "Faint high-energy gamma-ray photon emission of GRB 081006A from Fermi observations" (Zheng et al.)

## What the paper does

The paper reports the detection of faint high-energy emission (>100 MeV) from GRB 081006A by the *Fermi*/LAT, using the matched filter technique of Akerlof et al. (A10/A11). The detection comprises only 13 photons above 100 MeV (and 45 after including GBM-correlated lower-energy photons), with a matched-filter weight sum of 652,460 — >26× larger than any other event in the 74-event GBM sample. Verification is done via likelihood analysis (TS = 45, ≈4.6σ), random-field background estimation, and a joint 5-decade (8 keV–1 GeV) Band function fit using RMLLT.

## Strengths

- **Careful significance assessment.** The authors don't just report TS; they estimate false-alarm probabilities using ~2,192 random fields analyzed with the likelihood pipeline and 17,200+1,751 random-time fields for the matched filter, and explicitly compare the statistical power of the two methods (concluding the matched filter is ≳3×, and conservatively up to ~50×, more effective at this significance). The K–S test for the two distributions is a reasonable (if light) check.
- **Consistency checks.** The matched-filter localization, the *gfindsrc* TS-map position, and the GBM position all agree within 1σ; pre-burst background estimates are included; the possibility of a spurious point source is discussed (SAA passage ~120 s before the trigger is noted and addressed).
- **Physics-relevant detail.** The high-energy emission lasts ~40 s in LAT versus ~7 s in GBM, and the two highest photons (780 MeV at T₀+13.25 s) appear well after the GBM emission decays — a genuinely interesting data point for the long-lived emission questions (external vs. internal origin, prompt vs. afterglow). Joint spectral fitting spanning 8 keV–1 GeV is, for 2011, a solid observational product.

## Weaknesses

1. **Intrinsically incremental novelty.** This is one more entry in the growing *Fermi* GRB catalog of the period, applying a previously published technique (A10/A11) to one new event. The core "new" claims are (a) one GRB and (b) a method-comparison argument for the matched filter, which is interesting but modest and rests on a small number of events (3 GRBs with >100 photons).
2. **Small sample, qualitative claims.** Comparisons to 080825C and 090217A, and statements like "081006A is closer to 080825C," rest on n≈3 and are largely qualitative (similar fluences, similar delays, similar long duration). There is no quantitative similarity metric.
3. **The matched-filter vs. likelihood comparison is loose in places.** The "50 times better" claim interpolates exponential fits to cumulative distributions from different sample sizes, with the random-field samples restricted to fluence-passing fields; the exact construction (16088 candidate fields, 2192 subset, 17200 + 1751 random sample) is described only cursorily in the text, and the statistical rigor could be stated more carefully. The K–S test is invoked without a reported p-value.
4. **Modest statistical leverage in the spectrum.** The Band fit over the full 5 decades rests on only 13 photons above 100 MeV (the LAT portion of Fig. 5 has ~7 points, most upper limits); the "no cutoff feature" claim is weakly discriminated given that the LAT energy range reaches only 800 MeV effectively — a peak at a few GeV would be invisible. The authors do not make this explicit.
5. **Minor inconsistencies.** The abstract claims correlation with high-energy emission "as demonstrated by Akerlof et al." in one sentence while the GBM–LAT correlation claim for this event is shown only in the light curves; the trigger time in Table 1 (081000604) vs. the MET quoted in Fig. 2 (244996175 ≈ 081006004) is a mild bookkeeping sloppiness. Several cited works (Ackerман et al. 2010, Zhao et al. 2011) are arXiv preprints, so the comparisons are to unrefereed results.

## Assessment

This is a competent, conservatively argued detection paper. It is not a methodological breakthrough — the matched filter technique is taken from A10/A11, and likelihood analysis serves as the cross-check rather than the discovery tool. Its genuine contributions are (i) a faint, well-verified detection (TS = 45, false-alarm rate ≲ 10⁻³, probably ≪) of 13 high-energy photons, (ii) a useful quantitative argument that the matched filter is more efficient than maximum-likelihood searching for faint GRBs, and (iii) evidence that high-energy emission can outlast the GBM prompt emission by ~5× with individual >700 MeV photons tens of seconds after the trigger. For the 2011 state of the field it is a reasonable addition to the *Fermi*/LAT GRB sample and a legitimate early demonstration of the detection pipeline's sensitivity, but its originality and field impact are modest.

## [1201.3844v1-novelty] Novelty of the contribution


## Stated contribution (in my own words)

Zheng et al. (2011) report the detection of **one more faint high-energy GRB, GRB 081006A**, whose broad-band emission (8 keV–0.8 GeV) was identified with Fermi/LAT by applying the **matched-filter technique** to the *entire* available 27-month GBM-triggered sample of 74 bursts — a larger and fainter sample (lowering the GBM-fluence floor below 5 μerg cm⁻²) than the handful already screened in their companion papers A10/A11. They then (1) confirm the detection with an independent maximum-likelihood (`gtlike`) analysis (TS ≈ 45, ~4.6σ), (2) build a five-decade joint GBM+LAT Band-function fit, and (3) show that GRB 081006A is temporally/spectrally akin to the long-duration 080825C and 090217A. The methodological punch-line is a head-to-head test claiming the matched-filter statistic is several-to-fifty-fold more powerful than maximum likelihood at this depth, so the two tools should be used sequentially.

## Novelty judgement

**What is genuinely new here:**
- **One new faint LAT GRB** (081006A) and its detailed light curves / joint spectrum. This is the concrete content of the paper.
- **Scaling the matched filter down to a fainter, larger sample** (74 GRBs, sub-5 μerg cm⁻²), and the *explicit statistical comparison* (Fig. 4) showing matched-filter ≫ max-likelihood for these shallow events. This comparative claim about relative sensitivity is the one methodologically interesting result.
- The practical recipe (match-filter to find → likelihood to characterize) for faint events.

**What is *not* new (already in the cited prior work):**
- **The matched-filter technique itself** — explicitly borrowed from the authors' own Akerlof et al. 2010 (A10) and 2011 (A11). The paper builds on their own immediately-preceding method papers rather than introducing a method.

But against the prior work it cites — and notably its own A10/A11 — the methodological core is pre-existing, and the scientific takeaway (a faint, multi-decade Band-function tail, delayed LAT-onset) simply extends a pattern set by 080825C and 090217A. The only truly novel contributions are the specific new detection and the matched-filter-vs-likelihood sensitivity comparison. ## [1201.3844v1-methodology] Methodology reproducibility


# Evaluation of arXiv:1201.3844v1 — Zheng et al. 2011

**"Faint High-Energy Gamma-Ray Photon Emission of GRB 081006A from *Fermi* Observations"**

---

## Reproducible Methodology Summary

The paper's central experiment is the detection of the weakest LAT GRB (at the time) — GRB 081006A — using a two-stage strategy, followed by joint spectral fitting across GBM and LAT.

### Stage A: Matched Filter Detection (LAT)

1. **Sample construction.** The authors assemble an "extended GBM sample" of **74 trigger events** (Table 1) by deliberately lowering the GBM fluence threshold below the standard 5 µerg/cm² minimum, including GRBs with sub-threshold fluence *or* no fluence data at all. This is the key novelty over Akerlof et al. (2010, A10) / (2011, A11), whose samples were restricted to brighter triggers.

2. **Photon selection and clustering.** For each of the 74 GBM trigger times, the authors use LAT **TRI-CLASS** data and apply the *matched filter triplet cluster finder* from A10/A11. This technique identifies small spatial clusters of high-energy photons within a **16° radius cone** centered on the GBM trigger position. Each photon *i* in a cluster is assigned a weight *w_i* (defined by the A10/A11 algorithm; the paper does not restate the formula), and the cluster statistic is **Σw_i**.

3. **Background calibration — random fields.** To the 74 true GBM fields they add **17,200 random time fields** (i.e., 232 random epochs per GBM field), processed identically. This builds the empirical cumulative distribution of matched weights under the null hypothesis of no source.

4. **GRB 081006A result.** The matched filter returns a triplet cluster of **11 photons within a 2° radius** of the GBM position. The weight sum is **Σw_i = 652,460**, 26× the largest value in the other 73 GRBs. The centroid is at RA = 134.4°, Dec = −61.8° (J2000), with a 1σ radius of ~4.5° plus a systematic of ~2.5°. The false-alarm probability read off the random-field cumulative distribution is **< 0.4%**.

### Stage B: Maximum Likelihood Analysis (LAT only)

1. **Data and instruments.** TRI-CLASS events, **100 MeV–100 GeV**, instrument response functions **Pass6V3**. Data below 100 MeV are excluded because "rapidly changing effective area with energy as well as ambiguity in the instrument response" make the fit unreliable there.

2. **Constraints.** Maximum viewing zenith angle **105°** (per the Fermi *Cicerone* data-processing guide). Time window **0–50 s** after the GBM trigger time (TOA).

3. **Model and tool.** A point source is fitted against an **isotropic background** using **`gtilesc`** (part of the standard Fermi Science Tools). The test statistic is **TS = 2 ln(ℒ_source / ℒ_background)**.

4. **Result.** GRB 081006A yields **TS = 45**, corresponding to a significance of ~6.7σ by the √TS convention the paper states. Localization via the TS map (`gtfindsrc`) gives RA = 135.53°, Dec = −61.65°, with 68%/90%/99% containment radii of 0.4°/0.6°/0.9°. This is **0.55°** from the matched-filter location — consistent at 1σ.

5. **Random-field significance check.** The authors perform identical likelihood fits on **16,088 random LAT fields**. Only **2,192** of those contain ≥ 3 class-2-or-3 photons within 16° (the practical minimum for a meaningful fit). The random probability of TS > 45 is **≤ 27/2,192 ≈ 0.0123**. For matched weights, the equivalent figure is **< 74/17,200 ≈ 0.0043**, so the matched filter is at least **3× more powerful** at this extreme.

6. **Extrapolation to 10⁻⁴ level.** Fitting exponential tails to both cumulative distributions, the authors claim the matched filter rejects background **~50× better** than maximum likelihood (a level of ~10⁻⁴ vs. ~10⁻² for the observed weight/TS value). They explicitly flag this as an extrapolation.

### Stage C: Joint Spectral Fitting (GBM + LAT)

1. **Time window.** **−1 s to +8 s** after trigger.

2. **Software.** **RMFIT v3.3**, working on binned GBM time-tagged-event (TTE) streams and LAT data re-binned to the same format.

3. **Model.** **Band function** (Band et al. 1993):

   | Parameter | Value |
   |---|---|
   | Normalisation A | (2.1 +1.0 / −0.6) × 10⁻⁵ ph cm⁻² keV⁻¹ s⁻¹ |
   | E_p | 817.0 +57.7 / −34.0 keV |
   | α | 0.78 +0.24 / −0.23 |
   | β | 2.28 +0.10 / −0.14 |
   | χ²/dof | 464/379 |

   Photon flux: 2.18 ± 0.22 × 10⁻⁷ erg cm⁻² s⁻¹ (10 keV–1 MeV); 6.06 ± 0.61 × 10⁻⁶ ph cm⁻² s⁻¹ (10 keV–1 GeV).

4. **Model comparison.** Single power-law, power-law + exponential cutoff, Band + power-law, and Band + thermal two-component models were all tried; none improved the fit (no formal BIC/AIC comparison is reported).

5. **GBM background.** Estimated by re-scaling **pre-burst data** to the 0–50 s window using TRANSIENT class events; the TS for a residual background excess is < 3 photons.

6. **Resulting spectrum** (Fig. 5) spans ~5 decades from 8 keV to 800 MeV.

---

## Assumptions the Methodology Papers Over

I list every assumption I can identify, grouped by how consequential I judge them.

### A. Statistically consequential

1. **Isotropic background in the likelihood analysis.** GRB 081006A sits at Galactic latitude b ≈ −0.9°. The `gtilesc` fit uses a *purely isotropic* background model — no Galactic-plane ring, no point-source background. The authors acknowledge the risk ("we must be careful that the high energy photon background does not have a local hot spot") and mitigate by noting the GRB is > 10° from the brightest Galactic-plane region. But no alternative fit with a Galactic-ring component is presented, so the TS = 45 (and the ~6.7σ claim) rests on an isotropic assumption that is known to be violated at b ≈ 0°. A structured background could absorb a fraction of the apparent excess.

2. **The "3+ photons in 16°" cutoff for random fields.** Of 16,088 random fields, only 2,192 meet the ≥ 3 class 2-or-3 photon criterion and are entered into the TS distribution. The paper gives no justification for why TS values from fields with 0–2 photons (which presumably all yield TS < 27) can be safely ignored. If even a handful of those fields produced marginally high TS values, the tail of the distribution — and hence the 0.0123 probability — could shift.

3. **Exponential-tail extrapolation for the "50×" claim.** The 50× improvement of matched filter over likelihood at the ~10⁻⁴ false-alarm level is obtained by fitting exponentials to the visible tail of each cumulative distribution and extrapolating. The paper itself says "A more realistic estimate can be obtained by assuming exponential decreases…", flagging it as an assumption. With only a handful of data points in the very tail (where the actual event sits), this extrapolation is highly model-dependent and could easily be off by an order of magnitude.

4. **The 13-photon sample is dominated by three photons.** Table 2 shows 13 photons, but their weights are extremely uneven: the top three (157.8, 138.1, 118.8) carry the bulk of Σw_i = 652,460, while five photons have weights < 1 (0.570, 0.354, 0.121, 0.094, 0.002). The detection is thus driven by a triplet, and the "11 photons within 2°" or "13 photons total" counts include several photons that are individually indistinguishable from background. The paper does not discuss the sensitivity of the significance to removing the lowest-weight photons.

5. **√TS ≈ σ conversion.** For a 1-df χ² test statistic, the exact significance is 2[1−Φ(√TS)] using the standard normal CDF, not simply √TS. For TS = 45 the two differ by less than 1%, but the paper's stated calibration ("TS = 25 corresponds to 4.6σ") is itself 2σ below √25 = 5, suggesting a more careful treatment was done for the 25-point but the √TS shortcut was then used for 45. The inconsistency is small but unexplained.

### B. Physical / modeling assumptions

6. **Band function is adequate.** The joint fit uses a single Band function across 5 decades. The authors note "some GRBs have more complex spectral behaviour" and tested two-component alternatives, finding "the fit was not improved." However, with only 13 photons in the LAT range, the fit is severely under-constrained in the > 1 MeV component. The E_p is "not well constrained" (the paper's own words), and the LAT-side spectral index β (2.28 +0.10 / −0.14) is barely distinguishable from the power-law index of a simple power law in this energy range. A more flexible spectral model (e.g., a smooth broken power law or a log-parabola) could fit the 13 points equally well with different physical implications.

7. **Arbitrary time windows, no physical justification.** The matched-filter and likelihood analyses use **0–50 s**; the joint spectral fit uses **−1 to +8 s**. These windows are stated as "almost the same as described in A10, A11" rather than being derived from the data. A 50 s window for a 7 s burst may include afterglow in the LAT data, while a −1 to 8 s window for the spectral fit may miss the peak of the prompt emission (the GBM light curve peaks at ~2 s) and includes 1 s of pre-burst baseline. No systematic test of window choice is presented.

8. **Stationarity of the GBM background.** The GBM background is estimated by re-scaling pre-burst counts. This assumes the background rate is constant across the observation. For a ~5 s burst observed at a zenith angle of ~16° (the LAT bore sight angle), the background is low, but for the GBM the background can vary with elevation, time, and orbit. No test for time-varying background is shown beyond the raw light curve.

9. **No instrumental systematics discussed.** The paper does not discuss LAT detector dead time, trigger latency effects on the first few seconds, Pass6V3 systematic uncertainties in the effective area (which can be ~10–20%), or the impact of the 105° zenith-angle cut on the PSF. Given that the detection significance is dominated by three photons, a systematic in the effective area or background model could shift the result.

### C. Selection / methodological assumptions

10. **The 74-GRB extended sample is not a fair null.** By explicitly including GRBs *without* fluence information, the sample is not a clean random sample of GBM triggers — it is enriched in unusual or marginal triggers (e.g., events that did not meet standard fluence criteria precisely because their light curves are odd, or events in bad viewing geometries). This could bias the empirical weight distribution. The paper does not test whether the conclusions change if the fluence-less GRBs are excluded.

11. **16° search cone assumes GCN accuracy.** The matched filter and likelihood both search within 16° of the *GCN/GBM* position. The GBM 1σ error circle is 4.5°. If the true source were at the edge of the 1σ error circle, the photon density within the search cone would be diluted. More importantly, the 16° cone is set by the LAT 1σ localization, not by the GBM position error — the paper does not clearly separate these two radii in the methods.

12. **The GCN position is assumed correct.** Both the matched-filter localization and the likelihood localization are measured *relative to* the GCN position. Since the two methods are consistent at 1σ but both anchor on the same GCN circle, a systematic offset in the GCN position (e.g., due to BGO/NaI disagreement) would shift both locations in the same direction and not be visible in the internal consistency check.

13. **The "no significant change before/after" rate check is only a sanity check.** The authors state that the LAT field before and after the GRB trigger "we found no significant rate change that could mimic the signature." This is a coarse global rate test, not a spatially-resolved background check. A small localized excess (e.g., a faint AGN) in the 2° cluster region could pass this test but still contribute to the matched weight.

14. **K-S test and outlier exclusion for the 74→73 comparison.** The authors use a K-S test to show the two GBM weight distributions (with and without GRB 081006A) are "consistent" before excluding the outlier to make the "26× larger" claim. This is circular: including 081006A in the distribution inflates the spread, making it easier for the K-S test to pass. The 26× statement depends on which sample is used for the denominator.

15. **No test for a coincident non-GRB source.** The paper relies on the time coincidence (within seconds) between the GBM trigger and the LAT cluster to assert the photons are a GRB. It does not perform an independent sky search for a persistent or quasi-persistent source at the LAT position, which would rule out an AGN or pulsar coincidence. The 13 photons span ~40 s (the two highest-energy photons arrive at 13.25 s and 26.5 s), which is consistent with a decaying flare rather than a steady source, but no AGN search catalog cross-match is mentioned.

16. **Gap between GBM and LAT energy ranges.** The "joint" fit covers 8 keV to 800 MeV, but in practice the GBM data ends at ~4 MeV (BGO) and the LAT data starts at 100 MeV. There is a ~2.4-decade gap with no data. The Band function parameters that bridge this gap are unconstrained by data, so the "joint" fit is really two separate fits connected by a model whose mid-energy behavior is assumed.

---

## Overall Assessment

The paper's **methodology is fundamentally sound**: the matched-filter technique, the random-field background calibration, the cross-check with maximum likelihood, and the joint RMFIT are all standard and well-established in the Fermi/GRB literature. The detection is real — 13 photons, a coherent triplet cluster, a localized source at the expected position, a light curve consistent with the GBM prompt emission, and a spectrum spanning 5 decades.

However, **the statistical edge is much thinner than the headline numbers suggest.** The three-photon dominance of the weight sum, the isotropic background assumption at b ≈ 0°, the lack of an AGN cross-match, the unconstrained mid-energy Band function parameters, and the exponential-tail extrapolation all mean that the "50× better" and "~10⁻⁴ false-alarm" claims should be read as order-of-magnitude estimates, not rigorous numbers. The paper is more of a **detection and characterization** paper than a methodological one — the central contribution is establishing that GRB 081006A is a real, faint LAT GRB, which the evidence supports.

For **reproducibility**, an important gap is that the matched-filter algorithm is cited to A10/A11 rather than described in this paper. A reader who does not have access to those references (or the specific code versions used in 2008–2009) cannot reproduce the matched-filter stage from this paper alone. The RMFIT/`gtilesc` stages are reproducible with the standard Science Tools and the stated model assumptions.

**Originality**: The novel element is the matched-filter approach applied to a deliberately sub-threshold sample of 74 faint GBM triggers — a modest but meaningful extension of A10/A11. The physical result (a new faint LAT GRB) is incremental.

**Impact**: Modest. GRB 081006A remains a reference for faint-GRB work in the Fermi mission, and the matched-filter vs. likelihood comparison is useful for the follow-on catalog work (Ackermann et al. 2010a, A10a). But the paper did not change how the field works or open a new direction.

I've read the full paper (Zheng et al. 2010/2011, "Faint high-energy gamma-ray photon emission of GRB 081006A from Fermi observations," ApJ, 725, L15 / 726, 22). Below is the evaluation, centered on the audit question.

## Strongest quantitative claim

The paper's headline claim is a statistical one: GRB 081006A is **unambiguously associated** with ≥13 high-energy (≥100 MeV) photons, with a **false positive probability of order 10⁻⁵** (and, as the most defensible figure, *definitely less than 4.3 × 10⁻³*), corresponding to a Likelihood TS = 45 (≈4.6σ). - Abstract: "false positive probability... of the order of 10⁻⁵" (summary), "definitely less than 4 × 10⁻⁵" in the summary section (they write 4 × 10⁻⁵ there, but from Table 1 weights the 74/17,200 bound is 4.3 × 10⁻³ — an internal inconsistency worth flagging: "less than 4 × 10⁻³" is the count limit; the 10⁻⁵ figure depends on extrapolation).

## Audit

**1. Is the comparison fair? — Mostly, and arguably conservative in the right direction.**
- The random-field comparison used only 2,192 of 16,088 GRB fields (computationally limited), restricted to regions containing *at least three* class-2/3 photons. This restriction *enriches* the random sample with chance-photon fluctuations, so the "at least 0.0123 (27/2192)" random probability underestimates the true background exceedance — i.e., the comparison is conservative for the detection. Good.

The authors acknowledge the two are "complementary" but the joint false-alarm probability (the product-like argument for ~10⁻⁵) is only argued qualitatively, not computed.

**2. Are the "baselines" current? — Yes, within a 2010/2011 observation paper.**
- The relevant comparisons are intra-mission: A10/A11 (Akerlof et al.), Abdo et al. 2009a/b, 2010a, van der Horst 2008. All were current Fermi-LAT analyses at the time. The two comparison GRBs (080825C, 090217A) were the only other long GRBs with similar fluence then known. There is no external-method baseline (e.g., no comparison to the contemporaneous AGILE or Swift-BAT high-energy searches), but for a single-event detection the relevant baseline is the LAT's own published detection pipeline, which is used.
- As a *present-day* audit: "current baselines" is now moot — the 081006A detection has been superseded in importance by years of Fermi GRB catalog work (Abdo et al. 2011, P10; Ajello et al. 2015), so the paper's comparative framework is historical.

**3. Are error bars or statistical significance reported? — The primary significance is; the headline number is an extrapolation.**
- **Reported:** TS = 45 (≈4.6σ via the standard TS≈σ² relation, citing Abdo 2010a), containment contours at 68/90/99%, a formal count-based bound (74/17,200, ~4.3 × 10⁻³), and a binomial-style bound for the other method (27/2192, ~1.2 × 10⁻²). Figure 4 even includes dotted lines marked "estimated statistical uncertainties."

No confidence interval is given on that extrapolation, and no bootstrapped or Poisson tail-fit error is quoted. For 13 photons total, a simple Poisson tail (13+ events) over the effective sky exposure would independently give the order of the false-alarm rate; the paper does not offer that cross-check, so the "10⁻⁵" remains a single-method estimate. Also, with n=13 countable photons, Poisson error bars on the photon count itself (~±36%) are not discussed anywhere.
- The "50 times better" sensitivity ratio is a ratio of extrapolated tails — no error bars, and it propagates the same extrapolation uncertainty.

**Minor issues found during the audit:**
- Internal consistency: the summary says the false-positive probability is "definitely less than 4 × 10⁻⁵" while §2's count argument only establishes "< 4 × 10⁻³"; the stronger number is the extrapolation, not the count. Worth a reader warning.
- Two of the 13 photons carry diminished weights (0.094 and 0.002, flagged in Table 2); their inclusion barely moves the sum of weights but they do count toward "13 photons" — the significance actually rests on ~11 well-weighted photons.

The strongest claim (robust ≥3σ, most-defensibly ≥4.5σ detection; false-alarm rate at most ~4 × 10⁻³ by direct counting, ~10⁻⁵ by extrapolated tails) is **well-motivated, based on a fair and appropriately conservative background comparison**, with significance and localization error bars reported. Its weakness is that the *headline* 10⁻⁵ figure depends on an un-error-barred tail extrapolation and on an unstated independence assumption between the two methods — the honest, count-based bound is ~4 × 10⁻³. ## [1201.3844v1-writing-framing] Writing and framing critique


## Paper

**Zheng, Akerlof, Pandey, McKay, Zhang & Zhang (1201.3844v1, 2011)** — "Faint High-Energy Gamma-Ray Photon Emission of GRB 081006A from *Fermi* Observations." An 8-page detection paper: the 4th LAT GRB identified via the matched filter technique, ~13 high-energy photons, TS = 45, with a GBM+LAT joint Band-function fit.

## Sentences that overclaim

1. **Abstract: "appears to be a surprising dearth of faint LAT GRBs."** "Surprising" has no quantitative purchase here. The "sample of 74 GRBs" is itself selected by GBM fluence *or no fluence information at all* (Table 1 criteria), and is only 27 months of data from an instrument not optimized for weak, slow LAT GRBs — most of which occur when the satellite is near or in the SAA (as with 081006A itself, with only ~120 s before SAA entry). The non-detections are dominated by exposure and pointing geometry, not by burst physics. The word should be "possible," and the abstract should say so.

2. **Abstract/§3.2: "unambiguously identified by the matched filter technique."** TS = 45 justifies "securely associated," but the detection rests on 13 photons with ~2.5° systematic pointing uncertainty and a background model (isotropic) whose adequacy is checked only by re-scaling a pre-trigger background map over the 50-s interval and requiring TS ≤ 3. "Unambiguously" is the language of no plausible background or instrumental explanation, and that has not been demonstrated.

3. **p. 5, bottom right: "This would suggest that the matched filter technique is at least three times more effective at rejecting background" and "at a level at least 50 times better than the maximum likelihood approach."** The "3×" is a ratio of two *different* metrics (27/2192 vs 74/17200) computed on samples with subtly different selection. The "50×" is worse: it comes from extrapolating the fit lines (cyan dashed in Fig. 4) down a cumulative distribution that has 4–5 measured points with large error bands. An extrapolation-dependent factor of 50 with quoted "statistical uncertainties only" is a framing choice, not a measurement. The Summary then repeats "50 times better" as fact.

4. **p. 2: "the probability of such an event arising from random background photons is significantly less than 0.4%."** The 74 "similar" fields used for this estimate include the event's own sky region characteristics and are not uniform random sky directions; the 17,200 random fields that do the heavy lifting later in the paper were drawn with a different selection criterion. This sentence borrows the precision of the later comparison before it has been earned.

5. **§4: "This suggests that a single emission mechanism is responsible for the broad-band emission of both GBM and LAT, e.g., synchrotron or jetted emission from the internal shock."** The claim is built on the top of a 5-decade fit that has exactly four LAT points above 100 MeV (three of them in the first 9 s per Fig. 2). A Band function fitting GBM and LAT data is a *compatibility* statement, not grounds to invoke a specific physical mechanism; the sentence goes from descriptive fit to physical interpretation in one breath without any competing-model rejection statistics.

6. **§5 (Summary): "The false positive probability is definitely less than 4 × 10⁻³ and probably much smaller."** "Definitely" is too strong for an estimate that is (a) 74/17,200 with no Poisson or systematic uncertainty quoted, (b) computed with random-field selections that don't match the candidate's background conditions, and (c) not corrected for the look-elsewhere effect across the 74 GRB fields over 27 months. The honest number the paper's own method supports is of order 10⁻³ with a factor of several of uncertainty.

7. **§3.2: "A TS value of 25 corresponds to a 4.60 significance, approximately the square root of TS."** If TS ≈ χ² with 25 → σ via √TS, that's 5.0, not 4.6; presumably 5 parameters were subtracted, but the effective-dof statement is never given, and the same conversion is silently applied to TS = 45 (~6.7σ) throughout. Minor, but a significance the paper leans on should have its dof stated.

8. **Minor framing/consistency slips:** the abstract says photons "from 8 keV up to 0.8 GeV"; §3.3 quotes flux in the "1 keV to 10 MeV in the 10 keV range" (garbled). The title's "FAINT" inherits problem #1. And "GRB 081006A occurred ~120 s closer to the South Atlantic Anomaly (SAA)" — an occurrence can't be "closer" to a region; this is a positioning fact stated sloppily.

## What an adversarial reviewer would open with

> We read the manuscript with skepticism toward every conclusion that depends on the LAT data, because the detection consists of 13 photons, the best-fit position carries a ~2.5° systematic pointing uncertainty, and the background model is an isotropic one whose adequacy is tested only against a 50 s pre-trigger background map. The headline claims are therefore fragile in three overlapping ways. First, the "false positive probability" (0.43%, "definitely less than 4 × 10⁻³") is computed from random fields selected by criteria different from the candidate's, without a look-elsewhere correction across 74 fields and 27 months, and without any systematic term from the background model itself — the 13 photons of signal and the background estimate share the same response-matrix and exposure uncertainty, and neither error is quantified. Second, the claimed factor of ~50 in sensitivity of the matched filter over maximum likelihood rests on extrapolations of 4–5-point cumulative distributions whose own error bars already cross over in Fig. 4; as a "sensitivity advantage," this should be a hypothesis to be tested on a larger sample, not a demonstrated result, and must not appear as a summary conclusion. Third, the discussion's move from "Band function fits both instruments" to "single emission mechanism (synchrotron/jetted emission)" is not supported: the highest-energy end has four photons, no competing physical models are fit, and the GBM and LAT data are not co-spatial at the quoted uncertainties to the precision needed for a single-emitting-region claim. A competent, cleanly executed early-*Fermi* detection paper. Its real contribution is methodological (matched filter as an efficient first-stage identifier for faint GRB LAT emission), and that part is handled honestly enough to be useful. The overclaims cluster exactly where the photon count is smallest and the inference is largest: the "50×" sensitivity ratio, the "definitely" on the false-positive rate, the single-mechanism interpretation, and the "surprising dearth" narrative. Fixing the framing — easy for the authors, since every claim has a conservative version that the paper's own data supports — turns this from borderline-overclaimed into a solid, if modest, paper.
