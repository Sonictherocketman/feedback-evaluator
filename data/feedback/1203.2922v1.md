## [1203.2922v1-general-review] General Review


I've read the paper. Below is a critique of its argument.

## Paper identification
**Vlemmings, Ramstedt, Rao & Maercker (2012), A&A ~ in press.** "Polarization of thermal molecular lines in the envelope of IK Tau." First claimed detection of thermal molecular-line linear polarization in an AGB envelope (CO(2−1), SiO(5−4); also non-detections in SO(6−5) and SiS(12−11)), interpreted as the Goldreich–Kylafis (GK) effect, with a predominantly east–west or north–south magnetic field.

## How the argument is structured
The logic chain is: (1) detect polarization in two species; (2) show the detection is statistically real (remove the Q–U positive bias); (3) show the EVPA is neither radial nor tangential; (4) therefore it is not a pure dipole / optical-depth-driven GK pattern; (5) by elimination, the field is predominantly E–W or N–S and the polarization traces it; (6) ALMA will let this be exploited over an ensemble.

This is a reasonable skeleton for a first-detection paper, and it is written with honest caveats throughout. That said, the argument has several real pressure points:

## Strengths
- **Significance is handled carefully.** Removing the positive bias from √(Q²+U²), quoting 4.9σ (CO) and 6.8σ (SiO) peaks, and reporting EVPA errors (6–10° vs 4–10°) is good practice. Confirming the CO detection without self-calibration (significance drops only ~15%) is a strong robustness check.
- **Multi-species cross-check.** The CO(2−1) (800 AU) and SiO(5−4) (3″–1″) directions "remarkably" agree with the blue-shifted 86 GHz SiO maser E–W/N–S orientation — the only external anchor for the field geometry, and it is well placed.
- **Honest scope.** They explicitly state modeling is needed to determine the origin of the high fractional polarization, and that one object gives "few independent polarization vectors."

## Weaknesses / things I would press
1. **The physical attribution is by elimination, not demonstration.** The whole case rests on "neither radial nor tangential, so must be magnetic." But several competing explanations produce a similar on-sky geometry that are not fully ruled out:
   - A **radial field in an anisotropic, east–west-elongated envelope** (the very elongation the CO itself shows, and the dust asymmetry) would naturally produce an *apparent* N–S polarization. They gesture at the envelope asymmetry but do not model it.
   - **Dipole–dipole / anisotropic-radiation** contributions are acknowledged only as the source of the *high fraction* (13% vs the ≤3% Cortes-2005 prediction), then dropped. Because they cannot even explain the amplitude with a clean dipole-only GK model, the *direction* conclusion is on shakier ground than is implied. There is no dedicated radiative-transfer modeling chapter, so "magnetic field" remains a plausible but unproven assignment.

2. **No model to discriminate scenarios.** The 14% maximum fraction — more than 4× the expected GK value at τ~1 — is unexplained. The authors attribute it to "other anisotropies" and say more modeling is "required." That is fair, but it means the central interpretive claim (field morphology) is only *consistent with* the data, not derived from it. A 2D LT model (dipole-only vs dipole+dipole vs field-dominated) fit to the Stokes maps would be the real test; its absence is the single biggest gap.

3. **Angle ambiguity (LPA vs EVPA) is never resolved.** In the GK effect the EVPA is parallel *or* perpendicular to the field depending on velocity direction; the paper uses EVPA but does not make the B-field parallel/perpendicular call explicit with respect to which side of the line (blue vs red). This leaves the E–W vs N–S assignment more approximate than the "10° precision" language suggests.

4. **The B ≤ 1 mG bound is thin.** "We assumed a magnetic field strength ~1 μG… and a reduced dipole matrix element" is presented without clearly showing how this yields a 1 mG upper limit. The link between the assumed dipole moment, the observed 13% fraction, and the density n(H)≤3×10⁻³ cm⁻³ is not made explicit enough to check.

5. **Single object, few vectors.** Two species, one star, ~7 velocity channels, with the SiO vectors heavily overlapping and error-prone. The "large-scale field throughout the envelope" conclusion is more a hopeful framing than something the data can support from one source; they should compare against dust polarization at the same scales to get a real cross-check (they don't).

## Minor
- Table 1 line-width column labeled "[km s⁻¹]" (should be km s⁻¹, and the values look like full width rather than 5 σ line widths — worth stating precisely).
- CO is binned at 5 km s⁻¹, SiO at 2.5 km s⁻¹ — direct channel-to-channel EVPA comparison across species is then slightly awkward; the "agreement" claim should acknowledge the different binning.
- Small scale mismatch: SiO is much closer in (3″–1″), so claiming the field is "the same" out to 800 AU from SiO alone is an extrapolation.

## Bottom line
A well-executed first detection with careful statistics and good taste in caveats, but an argument that is incomplete in proving its own central mechanism: it rules in the magnetic-field interpretation by elimination without a model to discriminate it from envelope-asymmetry/alternative-anisotropy explanations, and without explaining the anomalous fractional polarization that undermines the clean GK picture it relies on. The 2012 ALMA prospectus is the best future-looking part of the paper.

## Review: *Polarization of thermal molecular lines in the envelope of IK Tau* (Vlemmings, Ramstedt, Rao & Maercker)

### Summary of the contribution
This is a **first-detection observational paper**: the first detection of *thermal* CO(J=2→1) and SiO(J=5→4, ν=0) linear polarization in the circumstellar (CS) envelope of an AGB star (IK Tau), with the Submillimeter Array. The central argument is that the observed electric-vector polarization angle (EVPA) — predominantly north–south and consistent between the 800 AU CO region and the ~250 AU SiO region — is set by the magnetic field via the Goldreich–Kylafis (GK) effect, and therefore traces a large-scale CS magnetic field.

### Strengths
- **A genuine first.** Before this, the only significant AGB line-polarization detection was CS(J=2→1) towards IRC+10216 (Glenn et al. 1997). Extending the technique to CO and SiO in a well-studied M-type AGB star is a valid and useful step that directly set up the ALMA era of AGB polarimetry.
- **Technical care and honesty.** The data reduction is thorough: MIRIAD specific-pol calibration, >120° of parallactic angle, leakage measured per sideband (~2–5%), residual leakage <0.5%, and — importantly — the **Wardle–Kronberg positive bias properly removed** before quoting Pl significance. Defining significance via probability intervals rather than treating Pl errors as Gaussian is exactly the right thing to do for the weak-signal case. The self-cal test that reduced significance by only ~15% (and changed neither EVPA structure) is a nice robustness check.
- **Cross-band consistency as evidence.** The agreement between CO EVPAs (blue-shifted south / red-shifted north) and the averaged 86 GHz SiO maser EVPA (Herpin et al. 2006) is a credible independent confirmation of a coherent large-scale field, and the paper is careful to also note the *disagreement* with the more complex 43 GHz maser structure (Cotton et al. 2010). Flagging both is honest.
- **The discussion of the two physical mechanisms** (anisotropic radiation vs. field-aligned GK) is sensible. Arguing that the EVPA being neither radial nor tangential excludes a purely radiation-field origin is a reasonable, if qualitative, test.
- Forward-looking ALMA section is concrete (10σ, 1″×1″, ~20 min) and motivates follow-up well.

### Weaknesses / concerns
1. **The central claim is heavily inferential, and the evidence base is thin.** The headline — "if the polarization is due to the GK effect, the linear polarization direction is defined by the magnetic field" — rests on an *assumption* about field strength. Maser field measurements (>1 mG) probe only the inner few hundred AU. The paper then asserts the field *still* dominates the alignment axis out at ~800 AU (CO), but **does not measure the field there**; it substitutes a density-threshold argument (n_H ≲ 3×10⁶ cm⁻³ suffices for a ~1 µG field). That is necessary but not sufficient: it never demonstrates that the actual field at 800 AU is above the threshold, and the extrapolation of a 1 mG inner field to the outer envelope is untested.

2. **The polarization fraction discrepancy is acknowledged but left unresolved, and it is central to the interpretation.** The paper reports P_l up to **13.1% (CO) and 8.8% (SiO)** — a factor of ~3–4 above the ~3% that Cortes et al. (2005) predict for the GK effect at τ~1. The authors say "the high polarization fraction is potentially caused by other anisotropies… more detailed modeling will be required," and stop there. But if the *fraction* is not explained, that casts a shadow on the *GK* attribution of the *direction*, which is precisely what the field-morphology claim depends on. A first-detection paper can get away with not closing this, but the gap between the observed fraction and the GK prediction is the single most important unresolved tension in the paper, and it deserves more than a deferral.

3. **The 180° ambiguity is fundamental and underemphasized in the conclusions.** The EVPA gives only a *direction*, not an orientation: the inferred field could be either **east–west or north–south** (or the EVPA perpendicular to B). The paper carries both possibilities to the end, so it cannot actually determine the field morphology — only constrain it to two orthogonal options. The phrase "maintains a large-scale structure" in the abstract overstates what parallel/perpendicular-ambiguous, few-vector data support.

4. **The link to the E–W elongation is circular/speculative.** The paper says the field is "either E–W or N–S" and then that this field "might be related to the E–W extent" of the CO/dust. This is offered as a hypothesis with a single qualitative citation to MHD work (Matt et al. 2000); no model or simulation is run, and a dipole giving equatorial density enhancement would not trivially map onto the *observed* elongation. It should be framed more clearly as a proposal for future work, which the Discussion partly does, but the conclusion's tone edges too far.

5. **No forward modeling.** For a "first detection," the field interpretation would be substantially stronger if even a simple dipole/ring model of the GK EVPA pattern were compared to the data (predicted pattern vs. the observed S/N and EVPA gradient). The paper says modeling is needed, which is fair, but this is where a reviewer would most want to see at least an order-of-magnitude pattern check.

6. **Statistics are on the low side and appropriately hedged, but it limits novelty.** 4.9σ (CO) / 6.8σ (SiO) peak detections from a 9-hour single-night, single-target observation; the "potentially real" north–south EVPA gradient sits within the 6–10° EVPA uncertainties. This is exactly the kind of modest evidence the honest language reflects, but it also means the paper establishes a *detection and a method*, not a robust field map. That framing (honest) and the abstract's stronger language ("maintains a large-scale structure") pull in slightly different directions.

### Minor points
- The manuscript header carries a 2018 A&A copyright/ESO line but is arXiv:1203.2922v1 (2012) — just a typesetting/version artifact, not a scientific issue.
- SO(6-5) and SiS(12-11) are given in Table 1 with polarization limits only; fine, but worth a sentence on why (low S/N / resolved-out).
- Figure 2/3 velocity-channel labelling ("labeled according to the velocity at the lower end") is good practice and consistent.

### Bottom line
This is a **well-executed, honest, genuinely pioneering detection paper**. The observational technique, calibration, debiasing, and multi-tracer consistency are all credible, and the first-detection of thermal CO/SiO line polarization around an AGB star is a real contribution that pointed the field toward exactly the ALMA surveys that followed. My main scientific reservation is that the *interpretive* claim — that the EVPA *traces the CS magnetic field morphology* — outruns the data more than the text sometimes admits: the polarization fraction is unexplained (and ~3× the GK expectation), the field strength at the CO radius is assumed rather than measured, and the 180° ambiguity leaves the field direction two-fold degenerate. Treated as "we detect thermal molecular line polarization and show it is consistent with, but not yet definitive of, a large-scale field," the paper is solid. As a field-mapping claim, it is a promising first step rather than a conclusion.

## [1203.2922v1-critique] Critique


I've read the full paper (Vlemmings et al. 2018, "Polarization of thermal molecular lines in the envelope of IK Tau"). Here is my critique.

## Strengths

1. **First-of-its-kind result.** This is the first detection of linear polarization in *thermal* molecular line emission (CO J=2→1, SiO J=5→4) from the circumstellar envelope of an AGB star, and among the very few such detections anywhere (the only prior significant one being the IRC+10216 CS(2→1) result by Glenn et al. 1997). Given how little direct information we have about magnetic fields in AGB envelopes, that is genuinely valuable.

2. **Careful calibration and significance treatment.** The authors correctly identify the dominant hazard — systematic bias in the position angle when adding Q and U — and remove it via self-calibration, re-deriving the EVPAs to verify they are unchanged (only the significance drops by ~15%). They report confidence intervals (99.73% / 99.14%), convert to ~4.9σ and 6.8σ, list noise levels, and give honest line-by-line upper limits for the non-detected transitions (Table 1). That statistical discipline is good.

3. **Physically motivated interpretation.** The argument that B ≳ 1 μG at ~200 AU lets the Zeeman splitting dominate over the Goldreich–Kylafis effect — so E–W/N–S EVPAs trace the field plane — is reasonable and explicitly tied to the maser-measured field (Herrin et al. 2006) and density estimates. The two-alignment-state picture (parallel vs. perpendicular EVPA depending on optical depth) is a legitimate and interesting physical prediction.

4. **Honest scoping.** The authors note the CO and SiO EVPAs disagree, that the E–W elongation is "consistent with" but not proven by the field, and that more detailed modeling and ALMA are required. They do not oversell a single-object detection.

## Weaknesses / Concerns

1. **Circularity between B, τ, and polarization.** The conclusion that "Zeeman splitting dominates, so the EVPA traces the field" rests on assuming B ≥ 1 μG at 200 AU (from masers). But the same paper then *uses* the EVPA pattern to infer the field geometry (E–W/N–S) and to explain the E–W elongation. The chain "field → polarization → field geometry" is only as strong as the magnetic-strength assumption, which is itself an extrapolation from maser sight lines to the bulk envelope. This is partly acknowledged ("more detailed modeling will be required") but deserves stronger emphasis, since it underpins both main claims (E/W vs N/S field; field-caused elongation).

2. **No actual radiative-transfer / NLTE modeling.** The largest scientific uncertainty — *why* the fractional polarization (13.1%) is far above the Goldreich–Kylafis prediction (≤3%, Cortes et al. 2005) — is explicitly deferred to future modeling. The paper offers hypotheses (anisotropic radiation field, optical depth, anisotropies in the circumstellar environment) but rules out neither the anisotropic-radiation-field nor the optical-depth explanation, because the polarization is "neither tangential nor purely radial." The core puzzle is therefore posed but not resolved; the "magnetic field" conclusion is one of several non-exclusive possibilities.

3. **Small signal, marginal per-channel detections.** Several velocity channels carry only a few polarization vectors near 3σ (Fig. 2/3), and the position-angle structure at low velocity (22.5, 25.0 km s⁻¹) is weak/empty. With only tens of vectors, drawing a global "E–W" vs "N–S" morphology from one velocity set is a low-sensitivity inference. The claimed large-scale field geometry would benefit from a formal significance test of the E–W vs N–S orientation (e.g. a circular variance / Rayleigh test), which is not provided.

4. **Single star, no control.** With one target and no null comparison (e.g. a known non-magnetized analogue or a check that the E–W preference survives different calibration choices beyond the self-cal), it is hard to separate real field structure from residual instrumental or envelope-asymmetry effects. The E–W elongation of the CO line may simply reflect the pre-existing flattened envelope seen by Kim et al. 2010, an alternative the authors mention but do not quantitatively weigh against the magnetic explanation.

5. **Limited impact of the "prospects" framing.** The ALMA discussion (Section 4.3) is reasonable but speculative — the paper's real contribution is a detection, not a survey design. The claim that the field "is responsible for the east–west elongation" in the abstract is stronger than the "consistent with" language in the body, an overreach of one step.

6. **Minor issues.** Fig. 2's beam/contour and self-cal "10 arcsec" phrasing is a bit opaque; the flux error bar (≤10%) and the ~30% higher LSB noise are noted but their impact on the per-channel EVPA confidence is not quantified; the paper would be stronger with a stated number of independent baselines/weights contributing to each EVPA.

## Overall judgment

A solid, honest, first-detection observational paper on an underexplored topic, with good statistical care and a physically grounded (if somewhat circular) interpretation. Its main limits are that the key physical question (origin of the high fractional polarization, and hence whether the field truly dominates) is left to future modeling, the sample is a single star without formal statistical tests of the orientation, and the field-geometry claims rely on an extrapolated B estimate. It is a good but not definitive contribution — a strong observational foundation plus a plausible, clearly-flagged interpretation rather than a resolved mechanism.

## [1203.2922v1-novelty] Novelty of the contribution


## Stated contribution (in my own words)

This paper reports the **first detection of linear polarization in thermal molecular line emission from the circumstellar envelope of an AGB star** — specifically the CO (J = 2→1) and SiO(J = 5→4, ν = 0) lines in the well-studied M-type AGB star IK Tau, observed with the Submillimeter Array (SMA) at 230 and 217 GHz in 2010. The measured polarization is predominantly north–south, reaches ~13% for the CO line, is neither radial nor tangential (the directions predicted by a pure Goldreich–Kylafis radiation-alignment picture at the inferred optical depth), and the authors interpret it as tracing a large-scale magnetic field (consistent with 86/43 GHz SiO maser polarizations and with the elongated CO/dust morphology). The paper also positions the technique as a path to systematic ALMA field mapping of evolved stars.

## Novelty judgment against the cited prior work

**What is genuinely new (first-of-its-kind):**
- The paper's own claim — first thermal molecular-line polarization detection around an AGB star — is **accurate**. The only prior detection cited is Glenn et al. (1997) around the carbon star IRC+10216 at **0.68 GHz (435 µm far-IR)**, which they explicitly distinguish: different star, different chemistry (C-rich vs O-rich), different band.
- It is new to do this at **submm (217/230 GHz) on an O-rich star** and in **two molecular species simultaneously** (CO *and* SiO, including the first polarized SiO), letting the two lines be compared as independent probes (CO at ~800 AU vs SiO at ~250 AU).
- The quantitative **fractional polarization (~13%)** and its **mismatch with simple Goldreich–Kylafis / anisotropic-radiation predictions** (Cortes et al. 2005; Dedieu & Watson 1984) is a substantive new result, not just a confirmation of a known effect.
- The explicit **feasibility analysis for follow-up with ALMA** is a new practical contribution.

**What is *not* new (well-trodden ground the paper itself leans on):**
- The underlying **physics is standard**: magnetic-field-induced alignment of molecules (Zeeman splitting vs. spontaneous/collisional emission) and the **Goldreich–Kylafis effect** (1981) are ~30-year-old frameworks. The authors themselves state their interpretation "in the framework outlined by Kylafis (1983)."
- "Magnetic fields are present in AGB envelopes" is a well-established conclusion already supported by SiO maser polarization (Cortes 2005; Beuther 2010; Vlemmings 2005/2011) and dust-polarization/elongation arguments (Kim 2010; Baums 2003; Decin 2010) — all cited. This paper adds a **second type of probe**, not the field-detection conclusion itself.
- The **interpretive steps** (parallel vs perpendicular to B via the optical-depth criterion; east–west / north–south orientation; equatorial enhanced-density MHD argument citing Matt 2006) are extensions of reasoning the cited literature already supplies — the paper itself is modest: it offers "a possible" scenario and calls for "more detailed modeling" and "further observations."
- The statistical apparatus (quadratic sum Q²+U², probability-interval significance per Wardle & Kronberg 1974) and the data-reduction pipeline (MIRIAD/CASA, "briggs" weighting) are off-the-shelf; no new technique is invented.

**In short:** the novelty is a **first-detection / new-observational-probe contribution**, not a methodological or theoretical one. Its worth is that it (a) confirms the AGB magnetic-field picture with an independent, non-continuum probe, (b) shows the polarization is too large and mis-aligned to be explained by simple Goldreich–Kylafis radiation alignment — pointing to a magnetic-field or unmodeled-geometry origin — and (c) opens a scalable ALMA survey of evolved-star envelopes. That is a meaningful but incremental advance: a solid first observation in a well-motivated program, with the physical interpretation remaining suggestive rather than definitive.

# Evaluation of arXiv:1203.2922 (Vlemmings et al. 2012, A&A)

*Polarization of thermal molecular lines in the envelope of IK Tau*

## Reproducible methodology summary

**Object and setup.** Single target: the M-type AGB star IK Tau (α_J2000 03ʰ53ᵐ28ˢ.84, δ_J2000 +11°24′22″.56), V_LSR ≈ 34 km/s. Observed 2010 Oct 8 with the SMA in the compact (lower-) configuration, 9 hr total on-source. Dual sidebands: 216.8–220.7 GHz (LSB) and 228.9–232.8 GHz (USB), spectrally covering CO 2→1 (230.538 GHz), SiO 5→4 ν=0 (217.105 GHz), SiS 12→11 (217.818 GHz), and SO 6→5 (219.949 GHz). Correlator resolution ~0.8 MHz (~1 km/s); 230 GHz beam 3.0″×2.6″, PA 81.4°.

**Calibration.** Standard SMA polarization schedule: phase calibrator J0423-013 (≈2.2 Jy beam⁻¹ → <10% flux uncertainty), polarimetric calibrator 3C454.3 repeatedly observed to cover >120° in parallactic angle, bandpass/flux calibrated with Callisto and Neptune. Initial calibration in MIRIAD using the Marrone & Rao (2008) technique. Leakage solutions up to 2% (USB, CO) and 5% (LSB, SiO); post-3C454.3 residuals imply <0.5% residual leakage. Data exported to CASA; self-calibration on the **strongest SiO line channel in the LSB** using the initial Stokes I image as the starting model. Those solutions were verified consistent with analogous CO self-cal solutions, then **applied to both bands**.

**Imaging and bias removal.** Stokes I, Q, U maps with Briggs weighting; spectral-bin averaging of 5 km/s (CO, SiS, SO) and 2.5 km/s (SiO) → rms 35 mJy (CO) to 65 mJy (SiO) per beam, LSB ~30% noisier. P_l = √(Q²+U²); the Rician (Rayleigh) bias of P_l is explicitly removed (Wardle & Kronberg 1974) because errors are non-Gaussian; the reported significance is from probability intervals, with contours at 3σ = 99.73% (4σ added). Peak debiased detections: 4.9σ (CO), 6.8σ (SiO); maximum P_l/I ≈ 13.1% and 8.8%. Control test: a CO map made without self-calibration shows identical EVPA pattern and only ~15% lower significance.

**Physical-interpretation step.** Magnetic sufficiency for the Goldreich–Kylafis regime is checked with Kylafis (1983) Eqs. 1–3, retuned for CO 2→1 (µ = −0.27 µ_N, d = 0.11 debye), assuming B ≈ 1 µG at the CO emission radius and using the AGB envelope density, giving n_H ≲ 3×10⁶ cm⁻³ for Zeeman splitting to dominate collisional + spontaneous de-excitation. The EVPA pattern (north-south) is then mapped as a proxy for the B-field direction in the plane of the sky, up to the 180° ambiguity.

A reader could in principle re-implement every step *except* a few that the paper leaves implicit (below).

## Every assumption the methodology papers over

1. **Circular polarization is negligible** in the SiO self-cal model (stated, "verified to be the case") — an assumption about a quantity the data don't measure strongly, propagated into phase solutions for both bands.
2. **The SiO self-cal solutions are interchangeable with a CO solution** (found "identical") — no tolerance, error bar, or statistic is given for that claim, yet it governs the final EVPA pattern.
3. **The phase calibrator's polarization is linear with zero circular** — needed for the argument that J0423-013 gain corrections don't corrupt R/L amplitudes; not measured.
4. **<0.5% leakage residual is sufficient** to not mimic the 8–13% measured P_l/I. The 3C454.3-derived leakage bound is a single, time-variable source, used as a blanket systematics floor for every channel and pixel.
5. **Briggs weighting index** (typical uv-weights = 2 is the standard, but not stated); no statement about robustness, channel-coupled calibration, or Briggs/rectangular comparison.
6. **Velocity binning (5 vs 2.5 km/s)** is motivated by SNR "optimization," but no analysis of how bin width affects the recovered EVPA pattern.
7. **The detection is taken as "real"** without a false-alarm or Rayleigh-distribution p-value on the map as a whole — the single-pixel 4.9σ/6.8σ significance is reported, but the map has tens of pixels above 3σ, which is roughly the expected false-alarm rate at these SNRs.
8. **Pure Goldreich–Kylafis regime.** The paper *assumes* Zeeman splitting dominates collisional/spontaneous rates even at B ≈ 1 µG (extrapolated from a 1.9–6.0 G maser measurement at <1″ to the CO emitting region at ~3″). A 1 µG field in the outer envelope is not independently measured, and the assumption is exactly what's under test.
9. **B ≈ 1 µG at the CO radius** is a fiducial pick; Kylafis' inequality is evaluated only at that value, with no sensitivity analysis.
10. **Optical depth τ ≈ 1 at the peak polarization** (used to invoke the maximum fraction), and the *assumed* explanation of the polarized-emission map as an "optical-depth pattern" is stated, not modeled.
11. **Polarization fraction (13%) is 4× the canonical Goldreich–Kylafis ~3% ceiling** in the limit τ≲1. The paper flags this but then attributes the excess to "other anisotropies" without a model — i.e., the physical interpretation leans on the part of the data the model *fails* to predict.
12. **The 180° EVPA ambiguity** is resolved by *choosing* an interpretation consistent with the SiO maser results (Herpin et al. 2006: 170–180°); the alternative field orientation is not independently tested. The paper concedes only "east-west or north-south" and does not address which.
13. **Cross-object consistency (800 AU CO / 250 AU SiO) is used as evidence of a large-scale field** but no statistical test of the EVPA difference is performed (error bars 6–10° per pixel; reported −10°→+10° and −7°→+4° overlap within errors).
14. **Comparison with the 86 GHz SiO maser EVPA** relies on a multi-maser average and ignores the more structured 43 GHz SiO result (Cotton et al. 2010), which the authors mention as a caveat but do not incorporate.
15. **The dust-envelope / CO elongation** (evidence cited for the MHD-dipole story) is drawn from earlier published work (Castro-Carrizo et al. 2010, Weiner et al. 2006); consistency across independent reductions is assumed.
16. **A single 9 h track** is sufficient for the polarization claim. No repeat track, and no statement of how much of the 120° parallactic-angle coverage was actually used in the self-cal.
17. **L-SB is ~30% noisier**, but no statement that the noise-weighting was actually different per band in the self-cal, and no cross-band consistency test on the SiO/CO EVPA beyond a qualitative "identical" claim.
18. **No independent test** that the EVPA is not an instrumental artifact from the antenna leakage patterns at these particular parallactic-angle ranges (e.g., the "leakage pattern test" is not explicitly performed as it would be in a 24 GHz study; the <0.5% bound is the sole check).
19. **Source structure in the self-cal model**: using the initial SiO total-intensity image as the model assumes a single component or a resolved enough structure that phase-only self-cal suffices; the marginal resolution is noted but not quantified.

## Verdict

First detection of thermal CO and SiO line polarization around an AGB star, cleanly debiased and calibrated, with an explicit control test (no-self-cal re-imaging). The observation is reproducible from what is written. The *interpretation* is, however, a chain of stacked assumptions — most importantly, the assumption that a weak, unmeasured field (≈1 µG at 800 AU) dominates the de-excitation rates, that the 13% fraction is compatible with the Goldreich–Kylafis picture, and that the 180° EVPA ambiguity resolves in the sense favorable to the SiO-maser result. As a discovery paper it is competent; as a magnetic-field characterization it is underdetermined, and the authors are candid about this.

Scoring:
- **Originality**: first thermal-line polarization detection around an AGB star; a new observational mode applied to a new class of source, but the underlying physics (Goldreich–Kylafis) and the technique (SMA polarization) were already in place.
- **Overall**: sound observation, honest caveats, but the central "we mapped the magnetic field" claim rests on a chain of untested physical assumptions and the 180° ambiguity; the 13% polarization fraction exceeds the standard model ceiling without resolution.
- **Impact**: a genuine discovery of a new observable in an evolved-star context; it seeded much ALMA-era work, but a single-object pilot detection with an unresolved interpretation.

## [1203.2922v1-logical-fallacies] Logical fallacies


## Understanding of the paper's logical argument

**Premise/setup.** Vlemmings et al. (2018) report the *first* detection of linear polarization in thermal molecular line emission from an evolved (AGB) star — the CO(J=2→1) and SiO(J=5→4, ν=0) lines in the circumstellar envelope of IK Tau, observed with the SMA at 230 and 217 GHz.

**Logical chain:**

1. **Detection + significance.** They measure P_I in both lines and rigorously correct for the systematic bias introduced by combining Stokes Q and U, reporting the polarization as 4.9σ (CO) and 6.8σ (SiO) detections after de-biasing. Maximum polarization fractions are 13.1% (CO) and 8.8% (SiO).

2. **Physical origin — Goldreich–Kylafis (GK).** They argue that for any field stronger than a few μG, the magnetic-field alignment axis dominates the polarization direction in the GK picture (Kylafis 1983), so even where the Zeeman splitting is small, the field sets the direction of the linear polarization. This is the key physical move that lets a weak field produce a detectable GK polarization.

3. **Field geometry from position angles.** The EVPA of CO runs ~−10° (south) to ~10° (north), i.e. predominantly N–S on the sky, roughly perpendicular-or-parallel to the field (the GK ambiguity between 0° and 90°). Because the observed linear polarization is neither radial nor tangential, they rule out a purely radiation-field-driven (non-radial envelope) origin and attribute it to a magnetic field.

4. **Cross-calibration with masers.** They tie the CO EVPA to prior SiO maser polarization (Herpin et al. 2006, ~170–180°) and to the 43 GHz SiO maser EVPA (Cotton et al. 2010), arguing consistency across scales.

5. **Inference to a large-scale field.** Since SiO(5–4) traces emission within ~250 AU and CO traces ~800 AU and the polarization direction is coherent across both, they infer a *large-scale* magnetic field threading the envelope (E–W or N–S, given the position-angle ambiguity), and propose this field explains the observed east–west elongation/asymmetry of the CO dust distribution.

**Conclusion.** The polarization traces the magnetic field; ALMA can map this technique to many AGB/post-AGB stars.

## Feedback

**Strengths.**
- The detection is well-supported methodologically: the authors are unusually careful about the Q/U noise bias, report de-biased significance, and even verify by re-imaging without self-calibration (only the *significance* drops by ~15%, the *structure/EVPA* is robust — a genuinely good robustness check). Flux error bars and leakage estimates are stated.
- The physical argument that a weak (>few μG) field dominates polarization direction in the GK regime (Kylafis 1983) is the correct lever, and it is stated clearly.
- Cross-instrument, cross-scale consistency (masers vs. thermal lines) is the paper's real selling point and is stated honestly, with caveats about the small number of high-resolution maser vectors.

**Logical weaknesses / gaps.**
1. **The 0°/90° ambiguity is never resolved, yet is central.** The G–K effect gives the polarization *perpendicular* to the field (screening) or *parallel* (screen-out) depending on optical depth regime. The authors explicitly use "parallel or perpendicular to the magnetic field" throughout, so every geometric conclusion ("E–W or N–S") is *twofold* ambiguous on the sky (E–W/N–S) *and* carries the parallel/perpendicular degeneracy. The conclusion "the field is E–W or N–S" is therefore tautological — it's essentially always true unless the field is diagonal. This is the paper's weakest inferential step: the allowed set of field geometries is only weakly constrained by the data.

2. **The "neither radial nor tangential" argument is under-quantified.** They rule out a purely anisotropic-radiation-field origin by appealing to Deducchi & Watson (1984) multi-level predictions, but then *concede* the observed 13% polarization fraction is **larger** than the GK prediction (≤3% per Cotes et al. 2005) and punt to "more detailed modeling will be required to determine the origin of the large fractional polarization." In other words, the dominant observed effect is *not the one they attribute it to*. The headline claim ("polarization traces the magnetic field") rests on a mechanism whose quantitative prediction is a factor of ~4–5 off, and the discrepancy is left unresolved. This is a serious internal tension that the Discussion should own more directly than "more modeling needed."

3. **Sample of polarization vectors is sparse.** Only a handful of independent EVPA vectors per channel (a dozen or so, concentrated on the bright peaks), and the N–S vs S–N EVPA difference (6–10° vs 4–10° uncertainty) is noted as a possible real N–S gradient but not pursued. With such few vectors the "coherence" supporting a large-scale field is thin; a quantitative test (e.g. whether the position-angle field is statistically consistent with a single large-scale dipole/quadrupole vs. noise) would greatly strengthen the inference.

4. **Distance/physical-scale dependence.** The AU conversions (800 AU, 250 AU) ride on the distance; the paper gives no explicit uncertainty propagation there, and since the "large-scale field" claim is a scale statement, its robustness to distance error is unexamined.

5. **Maser EVPA agreement.** Herpin et al. (2006) report 170–180° and Cotton et al. 2010 "high angular resolution interferometric observations (of a much more complex and variable polarization structure)" — the authors acknowledge the maser picture is complex/variable yet still call the two "remarkably consistent." Given maser vectors rotate across the line (140→160° for red-shifted) while the thermal line gives a single N–S angle, the consistency argument is somewhat loose.

**Overall assessment.** The detection is real and the paper is careful about *measurement* error, but the *inferential* step from "we see ~10% N–S linear polarization" to "a coherent large-scale magnetic field is present and sets the envelope morphology" is weaker than the measurement quality suggests: the polarization-geometry sign/parallel-perpendicular ambiguities are unfixed, the observed fraction exceeds the claimed mechanism's prediction by several-fold, and the field-coherence claim rests on few vectors. The ALMA outlook (Sec. 4.3) is the strongest practical contribution — the technique is demonstrably viable and clearly under-observed, and that is the most defensible takeaway.

> scores: originality_score=4, overall_paper_score=3.5, impact_score=3

## [1203.2922v1-quantitative-claims] Audit of quantitative claims


# Evaluation: Vlemmings et al., "Polarization of thermal molecular lines in the envelope of IK Tau" (A&A, 2018)

## What the paper does

First detection of linear polarization in **thermal** molecular lines from an evolved (AGB) star: CO(J=2→1) at 230.5 GHz and SiO(J=5→4, ν=0) at 217.1 GHz around IK Tau, observed with the SMA. The velocity-resolved polarization-angle (EVPA) maps are interpreted as probing the large-scale (~800 AU and ≤250 AU) envelope magnetic field, and the method is proposed as a new tool for AGB magnetism, to be pursued later with ALMA.

## Strongest quantitative claim and evidence audit

**The claim.** The central quantitative result is the detection of line polarization at **4.9σ (CO 2-1) and 6.8σ (SiO 5-4)**, with maximum linear polarization fractions **Pmax = 13.1% (CO) and 8.8% (SiO)** (Table 1), plus the derived field direction (predominantly E–W or N–S) and the tension with the standard Goldreich–Kylafis expectation (≤3% for CO at τ∼1, per Cortes et al. 2005 — measured 13.1%, ~4× higher).

**1. Is the comparison fair?**
- *vs. theory (Goldreich–Kylafis / Cortes 2005):* Only partially. The ≤3% benchmark depends on the line optical depth, which is not independently measured here; the paper itself notes that velocity-dependent τ across the envelope makes a single-number comparison fragile, and concedes the high P could arise from anisotropic radiation rather than the magnetic field. The "4× above prediction" tension is therefore suggestive but not a rigorous refutation — the authors are careful to frame it that way ("more detailed modelling will be required"), which is appropriate.
- *vs. maser observations (Herpin et al. 2006, Cotton et al. 2010):* Different tracers, radii (masers probe a few–few hundred AU; these lines sample 3"–1" ≈ 800 AU and ≤250 AU), and instruments (30-m IRAM vs. SMA). The "consistent" EVPA agreement with the 86 GHz SiO masers is a useful cross-check but not an apples-to-apples comparison, and the discrepancy with the more structurally complex 43 GHz SiO masers is honestly pointed out.
- *Single object:* Only one target is observed. Acceptable for a first-detection/feasibility paper, but it means every quantitative claim (Pmax, EVPA pattern, field orientation) is a single-determination statement with no statistical leverage and no way to assess how special IK Tau is.

**2. Are baselines current?** Yes, for a 2012-data observation paper. There is no algorithmic/computational baseline concept here; the closest priors are the only previous evolved-star line-polarization detection (Glenn et al. 1997, IRC+10216 — ~15 years stale, weaker) and the maser-polarization literature (2006–2011), which is current. The SMA compact configuration was the most sensitive mm interferometer available then; the paper correctly identifies ALMA as the follow-up tool. No "outdated baseline" problem.

**3. Error bars / statistical significance?** Mixed — better in places than typical for the era, with clear gaps:
- *Reported:* Significance by the Wardle–Kronberg (1974) P = √(Q²+U²) method with an explicit caveat that P errors are non-Gaussian, using proper probability intervals; the 99.73% confidence level for contours; total flux uncertainty ≤10%; EVPA uncertainty ranges (6–10° CO, 4–10° SiO); antenna leakage characterized (≤2% USB, ≤5% LSB — an order of magnitude below the signal, so the 13% Pmax is not a leakage artifact); and a genuine robustness check (re-imaging without self-calibration: same EVPAs/structure, only a 15% significance drop).
- *Missing:* No error bars on the Pmax values in Table 1 (the paper's flagship numbers); no per-channel or per-pixel EVPA uncertainties; no statistical test that the spatial pattern (S→N EVPA gradient, E–W elongation) is significant rather than a mild calibration/beam artifact — a noise-injection/Monte-Carlo test is exactly what is lacking; no confidence interval on the "3"–1" (800 AU) radial claim. The 4.9σ/6.8σ figures are peak-channel significances, not map-wide.

## Verdict

The detection itself (4.9σ/6.8σ) is well supported by standard, correctly applied significance methods with honest systematics checks (leakage, self-cal independence). The interpretive step (high Pmax vs. ≤3% G–K prediction; E–W/N–S large-scale field) is appropriately hedged and model-dependent, but rests on a single target and on Pmax and spatial-pattern values for which no error bars or significance tests are given. As a first-light demonstration it is sound; as a quantitative characterization of the field it is thin.

The paper is nonetheless a genuine methodological advance: it established envelope-scale magnetic-field mapping via thermal CO line polarization as a viable technique for AGB stars — a technique that subsequently became standard in ALMA-era AGB magnetism work, validating the paper's forward-looking claims.

## [1203.2922v1-weak-point] Weakest link in the argument


## Evaluation

**Paper:** Vlemmings et al., "Polarization of thermal molecular lines in the envelope of IK Tau" (2012/2018). This is a careful first-detection paper: linear polarization in CO(J=2→1) and SiO(J=5→4, v=0) in the AGB envelope of IK Tau, with clean SMA calibration, a positive-bias correction, and proper probability-interval (4.9σ/6.8σ) detection tests. The data quality is sound. The weakness is not in the observation but in the inference it draws.

### The single weakest link

**The premise that the observed linear polarization is produced by the Goldreich–Kylafis (magnetic) regime — i.e., that the magnetic Zeeman splitting exceeds the combined radiative and collisional rate terms over the emitting region — is asserted, not established. This is the step where the conclusion stops being entailed.**

The argument chain is:

1. **Data, solid:** CO and SiO are linearly polarized; E/P angles run N–S (ΔEVP A ≈ 6–10 between the two lines/regions), not radial or tangential.
2. **Unverified premise:** "Assuming that even the small Zeeman splitting of CO and SiO dominates the molecular collisional and spontaneous emission rates" (B ~ 1–2 μG assumed), so the linear polarization direction is locked to **B** on the sky, at 90° (parallel) or 0° (perpendicular).
3. **Geometry:** therefore B is large-scale, either E–W or N–S.
4. **Causation:** therefore B is "responsible for the east-west elongation of the CO distribution and asymmetries in the dust envelope."

Steps 1 is entailed. Step 2 is the break. The same data admit at least one other consistent cause: an **anisotropic radiation field**. Multi-level excitation by anisotropic IR (itself produced by a non-radial envelope, a velocity gradient, or dust absorption) can set the statistical-rate tensor and produce a non-radial, non-tangential E/P without any magnetic alignment. The authors concede this explicitly — "the high polarization fraction is potentially caused by other anisotropies in the circumbstellar environment. More detailed modeling will be required to determine the origin" — and only *propose* the magnetic interpretation.

Why this specifically defeats entailment: the two leading candidates (magnetic alignment vs. anisotropic-radiation alignment) are **degenerate in the observable** available here. A single object, single transition, a 180° (parallel/perpendicular) degeneracy, and no density/optical-depth model that fixes which physical limit the CO 7 μG-scale splitting actually sits in mean the data cannot separate "polarization traces B" from "polarization traces the radiation/velocity anisotropy." Once step 2 fails, step 3 no longer follows (the N–S direction then traces the radiation geometry, not a field) and step 4 is a further leap that rests on a qualitative MHD-simulation argument (Matt et al. 2008) without the field geometry being known (E–W vs N–S is itself unresolved).

So the conclusion "the magnetic field maintains a large-scale structure … and is responsible for the east-west elongation" is not entailed by the data; it is a plausible hypothesis that the present observations leave open. The honest, entailed statement is only: *thermal molecular lines in an AGB envelope can be significantly linearly polarized.*

### Summary judgment
- **Originality:** first detection of the effect in an evolved-star envelope — a genuine, though anticipated, first; the technique is a straightforward application of line-polarization theory previously done in star-forming regions and IRC+10216.
- **Overall:** methodologically careful and honest about limits, but its headline claim is speculative and rests on one unsupported regime assumption.
- **Impact:** a stepping-stone that motivates ALMA line-polarization field mapping rather than a field-shaping result.

> scores: originality_score=3, overall_paper_score=3, impact_score=2

## What is missing — items that would actually block an independent re-implementation

1. **No data release.** There is no reference to a raw/correlated-visibility archive, calibrated visibilities, Stokes I/Q/U cubes, or EVPA/Pl FITS maps. In 2012, SMA did not deposit data publicly; the raw data live in the SMA archive at IfA/Hawaii and access is on request. Without them the pipeline is not runnable. Fig. 1 (spectra) and Fig. 2/3 (polarization maps) are PNG/JPG-style reproductions; there are no online data tables of the spectra behind Fig. 1, so the integrated fluxes in Table 1 cannot be re-derived from the paper alone.

2. **No code / pipeline script.** The paper describes the *software* (MIRIAD, CASA) but no script. A re-implementer has to fill in:
   - the **briggs `robust` value** (or the equivalent CASA `imager` weighting) — "briggs weighting" is named but the parameter is not;
   - whether a **taper** or uv window was applied, and the primary-beam (pb) correction scheme (relevant because the envelope extends beyond ~4 beams and the maps show structure out to ±8″);
   - the **self-cal loop**: number of iterations, convergence tolerance, which channel/region was used as the model image, whether the model was regenerated each iteration, and (for the CO check) whether CO was self-calibrated with the same cycle;
   - the exact **polarization-calibration task** from the Marrone & Rao (2008) recipe (their SPIE paper is a description, not the CASA task list) — e.g. `bandpass` type (`L/R` vs `X/Y`), `calapply` order, per-pol gain calibrator, whether the leakage solutions were applied per scan, whether the bandpass solution was full complex or amplitude-only, and how J0423-013's known polarization was used in the "specific" calibration;
   - how the **EVPA errors** (6–10° for CO, 4–10° for SiO) were computed — standard first-order formula in σ_P, σ_I, or a Monte-Carlo on the Q/U Gaussians? Not stated.
   - how the **significance** (4.9σ and 6.8σ) was computed. The authors say "probability intervals" but do not give the integral over the 2-D (Q,U) Gaussian or the number of sample points used. This number drives the "significant detection" conclusion, so the method matters.
   - the **Wardle–Kronberg debiasing implementation**: "carefully removed the positive bias, which was introduced when the Stokes Q and U measurements were combined to produce the linearly polarized emission" — but the exact formula (analytical ⟨|P|⟩, an explicit −σ_P² offset, or MC on the χ²) is not written out. A re-doer has to guess, and the choice changes the 4.9/6.8σ numbers.

3. **No explicit "physical-model inputs" beyond the G-K summary.** The Goldreich–Kylafis / Kylafis-1983 estimate cites a specific n_H threshold (~3 × 10⁶ cm⁻³) for the CO(2-1) Zeeman-dominant regime but only "adjusts the parameters" to CO. The full set of atomic/molecular data used in the estimate (Einstein A for CO J=2–1, the Zeeman splittings of the sublevels in a 1 µG field, collisional rates of ortho/para CO at the relevant T, the reduced dipole-dipole and dipole-quadrupole couplings, and the assumed gas kinetic temperature in the CO emitting region) is left to the reader. A re-implementer can reproduce the *order of magnitude* from Kylafis (1983) Eq. 1–3 and standard tabulations, but the specific 3 × 10⁶ cm⁻³ number is not independently verifiable from the paper alone.

4. **No morphological / radiative-transfer model.** The paper is essentially a detection study. It does not run a Monte Carlo line-transfer (RATRAN, MCFO) or LTE+MHD model of the CO envelope to predict the spatial pattern of Pl and EVPA given a specific magnetic-field geometry. As a result:
   - The paper's central claim — that the observed north–south EVPAs "trace the magnetic field" — rests only on (i) the argument that B > a few µG is enough for Zeeman to dominate, and (ii) the qualitative agreement with the maser field of Herpin et al. (2006). It is not a quantitative fit.
   - The stated tension — observed P_l^max ≈ 8.8–13.1% **vs. ~3% predicted by Cortes et al. (2005) for the standard GK case** — is flagged and then set aside ("more detailed modeling will be required"). Without the model, a re-implementer cannot even test whether a *single* anisotropic-radiation geometry can reproduce the 13% while giving the observed EVPA, or whether the field interpretation is in fact required to explain the fraction.
   - The statement that the field "maintains a large-scale structure" across the envelope (CO at ~800 AU, SiO at < 250 AU) is a visual/one-parameter comparison of two EVPA histograms; no formal statistical test is given.

5. **Randomness / seeds.** N/A for a deterministic pipeline, but the only "seeds" in the analysis (choice of which spectral channel is the self-cal model, the uv range kept for imaging, the pixel size, the PA grid for the EVPA map) are not all pinned down.

6. **Compute / observing cost.** Re-taking the observation is the main practical cost: 9 h of SMA compact at 220 GHz is a large allocation. The authors argue (in §4.3) that ALMA achieves the 10σ version in < 20 min on-source, which greatly lowers the barrier for a future re-do — but that ALMA observation is not in the paper either.

7. **Secondary items** that a re-implementer would have to guess or re-derive:
   - flux scale calibration details for Callisto/Neptune and any corrections applied to the J0423-013 "2.2 Jy" flux;
   - the exact form of the "probability interval" used for the σ-levels on Pl (Rayleigh/χ² with which degrees of freedom, and whether the 15% degradation quoted for the no-self-cal CO was measured with the same interval or a different one);
   - the **pixel scale and grid PA** for the Pl/EVPA maps (needed to overlay with Castro-Carrizo et al. 2010 or Kim et al. 2010);

## [1203.2922v1-writing-framing] Writing and framing critique


# Critique: Vlemmings et al. 2012 — *Polarization of thermal molecular lines in the envelope of IK Tau*

**The paper in one breath.** First thermal CO(2–1) and SiO(5–4) polarisation detection toward an AGB star (IK Tau), with peak linear polarisation at 4.9 σ (CO) and 6.8 σ (SiO), EVPAs "predominantly north-south." A single-object, nine-hour SMA experiment is then turned into three interpretive claims: (a) the polarisation traces the magnetic field, (b) the same field runs from ≲250 AU to ~800 AU, and (c) a dipole field explains the elliptical CO envelope and the dust asymmetry. The ALMA section extends all three to a whole population.

## Where the prose outruns the data

**Abstract & §4.2 — "defined by the magnetic field."**
> "the linear polarisation direction is defined by the magnetic field as even the small Zeeman splitting of CO and SiO dominates the molecular collisional and spontaneous emission rates."

The sentence is conditional on an unverified assumption ("*purely* due to the Goldreich-Kylafis effect") and then states its conclusion as though it were established. The GK model itself is in tension with the data (next item) — yet the "defined by" wording is what the abstract's headline relies on.

**Abstract / Conclusions — "we can thus conclude… it maintains a large-scale structure."**
This is the single most over-assertive sentence in the paper. "Maintains a large-scale structure throughout the circumstellar envelope" is an extrapolation across three orders of magnitude in radius from *two* measured radii, in one direction, with EVPA errors of 6–10° per pixel and only two velocity channels showing any signal per line. The paper's own caveat — "as only relatively few independent polarisation vectors are measured" — contradicts the "we conclude" framing. It should be "consistency with a large-scale field" at the strongest.

**Abstract — "We propose that the magnetic field… is responsible for the east-west elongation."**
"Responsible for" claims mechanism and direction simultaneously. But the authors *cannot* say which of the two degenerate field geometries (E–W vs N–S) is real — the GK framework gives a two-fold EVPA degeneracy that this experiment does not break. Asserting a specific field *geometry* while admitting the 90° ambiguity makes the causal claim circular. And attributing dust asymmetry from Weiner et al. 2006 — an independent data set, independent wavelength, independent physics (radiation-pressure dust vs Zeeman-split CO) — to a field that is only "likely tracing" polarisation here is a step beyond the evidence.

**§4.1 — "the linear polarisation thus *likely* traces the magnetic field morphology."**
"Likely" is doing all the work. The next paragraph in the same section admits the observed fractional polarisation (13.1 % at peak for CO; 8.8 % for SiO) is **several times** the canonical GK prediction (Cortés et al. 2005: ≲ 3 % for τ ~ 1). The authors then hand-wave: "The high polarisation fraction is potentially caused by other anisotropies… More detailed modelling will be required." That is not an observation of a magnetic field; that is an observation of a polarisation signal with an unexplained amplitude. The word "likely" should be "cannot be excluded."

**§4.2 — "remarkably consistent with the averaged 86 GHz SiO masers."**
"Remarkably consistent" is doing argumentative work. The 86 GHz maser EVPA (Herpin et al. 2006) was *averaged over several maser features*, and Cotton et al. 2010 (cited in the very same paragraph) show the 43 GHz maser field is "much more complex and variable." Averaging is what makes "consistency" possible; that is not evidence of a smooth large-scale field, it is a property of the maser diagnostic the paper is proposing to replace. The authors do note this, but the "remarkably consistent" sentence is left standing.

**§4.2 — "could be an indication that… the magnetic field has a large-scale component preserved throughout the envelope."**
"Indication" plus an explicit analogy to VX Sgr (a *different* star, *different* physics) is not the same as evidence for *this* star's field. There is no model in the paper comparing a large-scale field to a small-scale/twisted field.

**§4.3 — the ALMA paragraph.**
- "ALMA will reach the same sensitivity … in less than two seconds." The SMA data set took nine hours; the "~2 s" figure is a sensitivity-equivalence calculation that is not derived or referenced — the reader cannot check it.
- "a 10 σ linear polarisation detection … in less than 20 min" is a straightforward extrapolation from a 4.9 σ result; fine as a projection, but paired with "for a large number of (post-)AGB stars and planetary nebulae" it implies a population programme from a single-object proof-of-concept with **unresolved degeneracies and an unexplained polarisation amplitude**.
- "ALMA polarisation observations will provide unique information on the magnetic field morphology around evolved stars." — the strongest sentence in the paper and the least supported: it presumes the GK mechanism, the E–W/N–S degeneracy, *and* the polarisation–field link simultaneously.

## Cross-cutting framing weaknesses

1. **Sample size one, with no controls.** There is no comparison to other AGBs where no field is expected, no null test of the GK model on a second star, no Bayesian comparison against the Morris et al. (1985) pure-radiation model the paper itself sets up as an alternative. The "neither radial nor tangential" argument in the Conclusions is necessary but not sufficient: non-radial / non-tangential does not by itself pin down GK-vs-radiation-geometry mixing.
2. **The "first detection" is real but narrow.** It is first for *thermal CO/SiO* toward an AGB; the earlier CS(2–1) result of Glenn et al. 1997 (IRC +10216 — a *post*-AGB / proto-PN object) is not the same source class. The framing is fair, but the paper leans on the "first" to do more interpretive work than a single detection warrants.
3. **The two-fold field/EVPA degeneracy is named but never confronted.** The conclusion "oriented either east-west or north-south" is honest, but the subsequent "proposal" treats one specific geometry as if it were selected.
4. **Numerical inconsistencies in the abstract:** ~800 AU vs ". 250 AU" (should be "≲ 250 AU"), "nearly identical" is never actually shown quantitatively (the CO EVPA spans −10° to +10°, the SiO −7° to +4°; the overlap is real but the CO range is wider).

## Draft first paragraph of an adversarial reviewer

> The paper reports the first thermal CO and SiO molecular-line polarisation detection toward an AGB star, and is to be commended for the observation. However the interpretive claims that follow are substantially ahead of the data.
>
> The detection itself is modest: 4.9 σ at peak for CO (with the largest polarisation confined to roughly one pixel per velocity channel) and 6.8 σ for SiO, with per-pixel EVPA uncertainties of 6–10° on a source that is only marginally resolved. The authors then take this detection and conclude — in each of the Abstract, Discussion, and Conclusions — that (i) the polarisation "is defined by the magnetic field," (ii) the field "maintains a large-scale structure throughout the circumstellar envelope" from ≲250 AU to ~800 AU, and (iii) a dipolar field "is responsible for" the CO elongation and dust asymmetry. >
> Most seriously, the authors themselves show that the observed peak fractional polarisation (13 % CO, 9 % SiO) is a factor of ~3–4 higher than the canonical Goldreich–Kylafis prediction for CO(2–1) at τ ~ 1, and they offer no model that brings the prediction into agreement with the observation. The paper's own language concedes this: "the high polarisation fraction is *potentially* caused by other anisotropies… More detailed modelling will be required." Yet the Abstract turns this open item into "if the polarisation indeed traces the magnetic field, we can thus conclude…". That is not a conclusion; it is the hypothesis the paper sets out to test.
>
> The single-object sample, the absence of a null-model or competing-model quantitative comparison, the "large-scale field" inference from two radii, and the ALMA prospects paragraph that projects these unresolved interpretive issues onto a whole population programme compound the concern. In its present form the paper supports a defensible statement — that a 4–7 σ thermal-line polarisation is detectable toward IK Tau, with N–S EVPAs that are *not* purely radial or tangential — but not the magnetic-field morphology claims the title and conclusion are built around. Substantive modelling work (including quantitative comparison to Morris et al. 1985 and to at least one non-GK mechanism capable of producing the observed ~10 % fractional polarisation) and, ideally, a second star — with or without significant polarisation — are needed before the field-morphology interpretation can stand.

Bottom line: the observation is solid and genuinely the first of its kind; the interpretation is not. The writing repeatedly uses assertive verbs — "defined by," "we conclude," "responsible for," "remarkably consistent," "unique information" — to present what the data support only conditionally.

This paper is the first detection of thermal molecular line polarization (CO J=2→1 and SiO J=5→4) in the envelope of the AGB star IK Tau, observed with SMA at 230 and 217 GHz. It reports linear polarization fractions up to 13.1% (CO) and 8.8% (SiO), well above the ~3% expected from the general Goldreich–Kylafis effect, and argues that this high polarization suggests the field traces large-scale morphology (east-west or north-south elongation) and that ALMA will enable field mapping across many evolved stars.

**Strengths**

- Genuinely first-of-its-kind result: thermal molecular line polarization around an AGB star was previously only detected in the infrared (Glenn et al. 1997) and at SiO maser wavelengths. Adding a thermal millimeter line to the AGB toolset is a real step.
- Good measurement care: significance estimation via a Q/U positive-bias correction, self-calibration on the strong SiO line, cross-checks with and without self-cal, and EVPAs measured from both CO and SiO. The two-line consistency (CO EPVA ~ south 100°, north ~10°; SiO ~ −70° to +40°) is a reasonable internal coherence check.
- Honest about interpretation: the authors correctly note that only two orthogonal (E–W / N–S) geometries are permitted by the KY framework and that distinguishing them (hence inferring morphology) needs many more field vectors, which only ALMA can provide.

**Weaknesses / concerns**

- The central quantitative claim is thin on rigor. The key argument (fractional polarization ≫ 3% ⇒ anisotropic radiation / optical-depth effect) rests on a comparison to the *general* Goldreich–Kylafis limit of 3% for optically thin CO (Cortes et al. 2005). The authors acknowledge τ is low (≲1) but never actually compute the expected P_L with the measured n ≲ 3×10⁻³ cm⁻³, B≲1 μG, optical depth, and the full line/branch structure of CO 2–1. A proper multi-level radiative-transfer (e.g., Dedeckers & Watson) fit — mentioned, but conspicuously absent — is doing the lifting of their conclusion.
- The morphology claim (east-west elongation traced at ~800 AU–10,000 AU) is a big leap: from a handful of coarse vectors at 3″–1″ resolution they infer global field structure, and only two geometries are consistent, and both are presented as equally viable ("east-west or north-south"). This weakens the "first morphology" claim in the abstract.
- Systematics vs. structure: a north–south EPVA gradient (≈100° south to ≈10° north) is the strongest single signature, but the paper only briefly dismisses it as "potentially real." A cleaner treatment (a position-EPVA profile, a significance map showing where the gradient is measured rather than inferred from averaged channels, or at least one more independent check) is worth having before invoking large-scale morphology.
- Figures 2/3 are hard to read: the polarization vector overlays are short and dense at 3–4σ in the blue-shifted side, and the beam circle plus color scale are not always clear; several panels' segments would benefit from being length-normalized or plotted separately from the significance contours.

**Recommendation: Borderline accept (score 6/10)**

This is a solid, well-executed first detection in an important (if narrow) niche, with implications for a whole coming program of ALMA AGB polarization studies. That merits acceptance on merit; the reasons it is only borderline are (a) the interpretation runs ahead of the modeling that is needed to back it, and (b) the two "headline" claims (anomalous fractional polarization; morphology) each rest on a weaker evidentiary base than the paper suggests.

**Three changes that would most improve the paper**

1. **Run the multi-level radiative-transfer model (Dedeckers & Watson / Cortes et al. setup) for CO 2–1** with the measured density and field range, and quantitatively show whether the observed ~13% P_L requires an anisotropic radiation field and what its anisotropy must be. This one thing would upgrade the paper from "consistent with" to "demonstrates the anisotropy."
2. **Sharpen the morphology claim or scale it back.** Either obtain/fit additional field vectors (even the SiO channel maps alone give a few more, and a position-vs-EPVA figure would help) to distinguish the E–W and N–S geometries, or revise the abstract/conclusion to say the data "constrain the field to be predominantly E–W or N–S" rather than "traces the morphology / is responsible for the elongation."
3. **Tighten the statistics and the figures.** Propagate the EVPA and P_L errors properly through the Q/U averaging, produce a 3σ/4σ significance map of P_L (showing where the N–S gradient is actually measured), length-normalize the vector overlays, and explicitly quantify beam dilution in the comparison to the 60-GHz SiO maser E/VPA.

# Peer Review: "Polarization of thermal molecular lines in the envelope of IK Tau"

## Summary
The authors present the first detection of linear polarization in the thermal molecular line emission (CO 2-1, SiO 5-4, SO 6-5, SiS 12-11) in the circumstellar envelope of the M-type AGB star IK Tau, made with the SMA in 2010. Polarization is detected in CO (up to ~13%), SiO, SO and SiS; the linear polarization vector (EVP) is predominantly north–south in both CO and SiO. The authors argue the polarization likely traces the large-scale magnetic field through the envelope and is at least partly consistent with the goldreich–Kylafis mechanism. The detection also sets up a prospective survey of evolved stars with ALMA. This is a legitimate first-detection paper with careful data reduction.

## Major points

**1. Interpretation is under-determined and partly inconsistent with the field model they propose.** The core claim — "the polarization traces the magnetic field via the goldreich–Kylafis effect" — is weakened by a point they raise but do not resolve: the observed fractional polarization (~13% for CO, up to 5.5% for SiO) substantially exceeds the ≤3% that goldreich–Kylafis produces for low optical depth (Cortes et al. 2005). They acknowledge this and defer the explanation to "other anisotropies," but no quantitative modeling is performed. Without a radiative-transfer model constraining the radiation field anisotropy, the optical depth, and the field orientation, the magnetic-field interpretation remains a hypothesis. The claim that "even a relatively weak magnetic field is sufficient" (from Vlemmings 2005, >1 mG) is asserted, not demonstrated for this system. A prestigious journal would want at least one constrained RT model, or the magnetic interpretation stated much more tentatively.

**2. Ambiguity of the field geometry is acknowledged but unresolved.** They conclude the field is "either east–west or north–south" and the EVE is "parallel or perpendicular" to the field along the line of sight — i.e. the two classical degeneracies. The east–west elongation of the CO distribution is offered as evidence for a magnetic field, but this is correlational; quadrupolar enhancement of density profiles (Matt 2006) is mentioned and then set aside ("however, we note that…"). The causal link between the elongated envelope and a magnetic axis is speculative. The paper would be stronger if the authors more clearly flagged which claims are model-independent (detection of linear polarization, its NE–SW offset structure) versus which require assumptions (field alignment).

**3. The CO–SiO agreement is thin.** A central supporting argument is that the EVE of CO(2–1) and SiO(5–4) are "remarkably consistent" and thus trace the same field at different radii (~250 AU vs ~800 AU), implying a large-scale organized field. But the sample is small (a handful of independent vectors per line at two radii), and the CO EVE is explicitly noted as potentially real only in the difference between the northern and southern halves (EVP ~10° N vs ~–10° S). The statistical basis for "large-scale structure throughout the envelope" is not established. Error bars, number of independent points, and the degeneracy between field geometry and velocity-gradients should be quantified rather than judged by eye.

**4. The "13.1%" figure for CO in Table 1 deserves scrutiny.** The peak fractional polarization of 13% for a 4.9σ linear polarization detection is high. The paper does not discuss systematic effects in detail that could inflate or bias the polarization — the Q/U position biases they correct (Wardle & Kronberg 1974), channel-noise differences (CO rms ~35 mJy vs ~65 mJy in the LSB), and the fact that only 30% of the LSB is self-calibrated. A bias analysis (e.g., Monte Carlo or systematic error propagation on P_l) would substantially harden the fractional polarization claim.

## Minor points
- The discrepancy "EVP is north–south" yet the abstract says "predominantly north–south" while Fig. 2 vectors look somewhat scattered and tilted is not discussed; a small angular histogram/mean-vector analysis would help.
- No comparison to the existing IRC+10216 CO polarization (Glenn et al. 1997) is made quantitatively — the paper is positioned as the first in an *envelope of an evolved star* but does not engage in detail with the one prior detection, limiting the comparative context.
- The ALMA prospects section is generic; a concrete sensitivity/timing estimate for a multi-source survey would give the outlook more substance.
- Line 1 of the abstract states "the linear polarization direction is defined by the magnetic field" — too strong given the unresolved degeneracies; should be softened.
- Figures: vector errors on the EVE are not shown in Figs. 2/3, making significance of individual vectors hard to judge.

## Overall assessment
This is a clean, honestly-written first detection with defensible data reduction and a well-motivated physical discussion. Its main limitation — typical of the literature — is that the magnetic-field interpretation is asserted on the strength of general goldreich–Kylafis reasoning and qualitative agreement between two lines, without the radiative-transfer modeling and statistical quantification that a top journal would require before treating "the CO polarization traces the magnetic field structure of the envelope" as a conclusion. The detection itself is solid and genuinely valuable (and the ALMA prospect is compelling), but the inference from detection to magnetic topology is not yet firmly earned.

This is, on balance, a good, publishable observational paper with an interpretation that I would recommend the authors tone down until a model is in hand.

I've read the full paper (Vlemmings et al., "Polarization of thermal molecular lines in the envelope of IK Tau," A&A, arXiv:1203.2922v1, 6 pages). Here is my review.

## Summary

First detection of thermal molecular-line linear polarization — CO(2−1) at 230.5 GHz and SiO(5−4) at 217.1 GHz — in the circumstellar envelope of the M-type AGB star IK Tau, observed with the SMA. The observed evpa (EVPA) is predominantly N–S, matches in both tracers (CO at ~800 AU, SiO at <250 AU), and is neither tangential nor radial, so the authors argue it is not caused by the anisotropic radiation field (Morris et al. 1985; Deguchi & Watson 1984) and therefore — for pure Goldreich–Kylafis (GK) polarization — likely traces the large-scale circumstellar magnetic field.

## Strengths

1. **Genuinely first-of-its-kind result.** The only prior significant AGB detection was CS(2−1) toward IRC+10216 (Glenn et al. 1997). Filling this gap for a well-studied, nearby object is valuable in its own right.
2. **Careful polarization data reduction.** Specific polarization calibration, leakage check against 3C454.3 with residual leakage < 0.5%, Wardle & Kronberg (1974) debiasing with proper treatment of the non-Gaussian Pl errors, a test of the self-calibration's effect on the polarized images (structure unchanged, significance down only ~15%), and cross-checking SiO- and CO-derived self-cal solutions. This is exactly the battery of checks a skeptical reader wants.
3. **Physical consistency checks done, not just stated.** Density threshold for Zeeman dominance vs. collisions is estimated from Kylafis (1983) parameters; the CO/SiO EVPA agreement at very different radii is a meaningful internal consistency test; the blue-shifted CO/SiO EVPAs match the averaged 86 GHz SiO maser polarizations of Herpin et al. (2006).
4. **Honest about its own limitations** in places: "relatively few independent polarization vectors," the parallel/perpendicular ambiguity, the unexplained high polarization fraction.

## Weaknesses — the main problems

1. **The interpretation outruns the evidence.** The abstract asserts "assuming that the polarization is purely due to the Goldreich–Kylafis effect, the linear polarization direction is defined by the magnetic field..." and the conclusions soften this to "potentially traces." But the observed fractions (13.1% for CO, 8.8% for SiO) are *several times* the ~3% typically predicted by GK theory at τ ≈ 1 (Cortés et al. 2005). You cannot ignore a factor of 3–4 discrepancy in the very quantity whose direction you are interpreting, and then hand-wave it as "more detailed modeling will be required." That modeling is the hard part of this paper and it is missing entirely. No radiative-transfer, no level-population, no Zeeman-splitting calculation — just the threshold density argument.

2. **A single-object, single-epoch study with ~2–3 independent vectors can't resolve the geometry claims.** The east-west vs. north-south dichotomy is the 180° ambiguity, and you cannot break it with the data presented. The connection to the E–W CO elongation and dust asymmetry then rests entirely on an unmodelled MHD dipole argument (Matt et al. 2000) that you don't actually work out — you never even state which field orientation (N–S or E–W) predicts the E–W elongation, or whether an axial dipole's equatorially-enhanced density is consistent with an E–W rather than the observed western elongation in the inner envelope (Castro-Carrizo et al. 2010).

3. **Tension with the 43 GHz SiO masers is underplayed.** Cotton et al. (2010) found a "much more complex and variable" maser polarization structure; a field that smoothly connects 1 mG-scale structure at <250 AU to 800 AU needs a physical argument (field-line topology, reconnection, time dependence), not just "could be an indication."

4. **The ~20° EVPA trend across the CO channels (−10° to +10°) is not modelled.** With 6–10° uncertainties it is at most a 2σ trend, and it is precisely the kind of information that could distinguish radial dipole geometry. Saying it is "potentially real" and moving on wastes it.

5. **Presentation issues:**
   - The abstract states a conditional result as a fact — fix it to match the body.
   - Typos: "anistropies," "enviroment," "As a results the channel rms," "The spectra of these transitions is shown."
   - "Pmax [%, km s⁻¹]" in Table 1 — the km s⁻¹ unit for the polarization fraction is wrong/typo for the integrated-flux column; check the header.
   - Figure 1 labels "VLSR = 25 km s−1" and "34" as stellar velocity in different places — make sure the stellar-velocity reference is consistent.
   - The 4.2 claim of "remarkably consistent" maser comparison should quantify the angular difference and its error bars.

## What I would require before calling this a finished story

- A minimum two-level / multi-level model (Deguchi & Watson style, or the existing ALMA-era codes like POLAR) that (a) explains the >8% polarization fraction — optical-depth structure, radiative pumping — and (b) predicts whether the EVPA is parallel or perpendicular to B for the CO and SiO radii and optical depths you measure. This single piece of work would turn "potentially traces the field" into "traces the field with orientation X."
- At least a second, geometrically distinct object to show the morphology is not an IK-Tau-specific accident.
- A statement of exactly what angular-resolution and sensitivity requirements (i.e., ALMA band 6, ~0.5" resolution) would break the two-degeneracy in EVPA (the 180° ambiguity and the parallel/perpendicular ambiguity) before promising the field-mapping claim.

## Bottom line

A solid, well-executed first detection with exemplary polarization data reduction and honest internal consistency checks. Its limitations are interpretive, not technical: the quantitative modeling is absent, the field-geometry inferences are doubly ambiguous and single-object, and the high polarization fraction is never explained. As a first step toward a real field-mapping program it is publishable and was; as a stand-alone claim about the magnetic field of IK Tau, it proves that the field is *possibly* there and large-scale, and not much more. Do the modeling and a second object, and this becomes a strong paper.
