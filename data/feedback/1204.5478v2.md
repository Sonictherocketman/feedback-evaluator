## [1204.5478v2-general-review] General Review


# Review: "The observed neutron star mass distribution as a probe of the supernova explosion mechanism" (Pejcha, Thompson & Kochanek)

## Summary of the argument

The paper uses the six precise double-neutron-star (DNS) mass measurements of Özel et al. (2012) to Bayesian-compare models that map observed NS pairs back to their SN progenitor masses. The mapping comes from the simulated remnant-mass function of Zhang et al. (2008). The authors compute model evidence (Bayes factors) for a small family of hypotheses that vary three physical axes: (i) whether the two components are drawn independently from a Salpeter IMF or from a binary mass-ratio distribution, (ii) the explosion energy / mass-fallback amount, and (iii) the "piston" position separating remnant from ejecta (base of the oxygen shell S/N_A = 4 k_B, or the depletion/"Y_e" core). Their headline results: binary mass-ratio models are strongly preferred over independent sampling; no-fallback (strong-explosion / little-mass-loss) models are preferred; and for solar metallicity the best model places the explosion at S/N_A ≈ 2.8 k_B, i.e. the edge of the iron core.

## What the paper does well

- **Clean, transparent Bayesian framework.** Writing the model evidence as an analytically integrable marginal likelihood over progenitor mass (Eqs. 7, 8) rather than a χ² to a phenomenological NS mass function is a genuine advantage. It lets them attach an uncertainty σ_theo to the theoretical model and use Bayes factors on a principled footing rather than eyeballing contour plots.
- **The binary-vs-independent result is interesting and physically motivated.** The observation that the primary-progenitor distribution is cut off at the *minimum* NS-producing mass while the secondary-progenitor distribution is cut off at the *maximum* is a real asymmetry that independent-IMF sampling misses, and it explains why the observed 2-D distribution is not peaked at low M_A. The "twins" test in Fig. 3 is a nice consistency probe.
- **Choice of a clean sample.** Restricting to recycled-pulsar/companion DNS systems without significant mass transfer is the right "minimal physics" choice, and the treatment of the primary/secondary (p_ν) ambiguity via Press (1997)-style priors — especially the careful J1906+0746 discussion — is handled honestly.
- **Honesty about limitations.** The small-N problem, the model-dependence on Zhang et al. (2008), and the selection arguments are discussed in §3.3, and the post-submission Ugolino et al. (2012) comparison in the Appendix is a responsible addition.

## Main criticisms

1. **The sample is too small to support the strength of the claims.** All "decisive" Bayes factors (B_12 > 10²) and the "factor of 3–300" boost from the J1906+0746 assignment rest on **six** systems, several with large mass uncertainties. Jeffreys' "decisive" language is hard to defend at N = 6; the posterior is dominated by a handful of points and is very sensitive to which mass measurements (Özel et al.'s non-Gaussian likelihood) you trust. The authors acknowledge small-N but still present the results as if they discriminate among explosion physics rather than among statistical treatments of a tiny data set.

2. **Conclusions are hostage to one simulation, with arbitrary injection physics.** Every remnant-mass curve M′(M) is taken from Zhang et al. (2008) — non-rotating, single-zone-pistol, energy/momentum-injection models. The headline "no fallback, explosion at the edge of the iron core" is really a statement about *which Zhang et al. configuration fits the DNS data*, not about supernovae in general. Rotational engines, neutrino-driven models, and the Ugolino et al. (2012) fallback treatment give materially different predictions — the Appendix itself finds the Ugolino-based DNS model has a relative probability ~10⁴ lower than the authors' best model, so the result is not robust across fallback prescriptions. The title's promise to probe "the supernova explosion mechanism" substantially overstates what a two-piston/two-metallicity comparison from one code can deliver.

3. **The no-fallback preference is selection-driven and under-explored.** DNS systems are intrinsically the *low-NS-mass, low-progenitor-mass* corner of the SN population (they survived two core collapses without disruption). Fallback pushes remnant masses toward ~2 M_☉ and produces NS–BH/BH systems that by construction are absent from the DNS sample. So "fallback models expand the mass range and are penalized" is partly a tautology: the method rewards whatever best fits the *narrow low-mass* DNS distribution, not the true SN remnant-function. The observed absence of any M > 1.5 M_☉ is dismissed as "likely a statistical fluctuation," but with six systems that is a weak rejection of fallback, and the authors do not quantify how many systems would be needed to exclude it.

4. **The S/N_A ≈ 2.8 k_B interpretation is a chain of assumptions.** "NS mass = mass at the iron-core edge, therefore the explosion initiates at the iron-core edge" requires simultaneously: no fallback at all, the extended Woosley & Weaver (2002) envelope prescription, and the Zhang et al. mapping. Any small fallback shifts the inferred S/N_A. Presenting a model-comparison peak as a physical statement about where the explosion begins is the paper's most over-interpreted claim, and it deserves a much more guarded framing.

5. **Hyperparameters and hyperpriors are under-tested.** The kernel width σ_theo = 0.025 M_☉ (justified by a "not too small, not too large" argument) and the free M_max range / best-fit values (14–35 M_☉ depending on method) are fairly arbitrary and the conclusions could shift with them; a brief sensitivity study on σ_theo and the M_max prior range would strengthen the evidence substantially. Similarly, Fig. 5's observed minimum total mass (~2.6 M_☉) being "markedly higher" than all models is noted but not turned into a test.

6. **The binary-vs-independent result is entangled with the p_ν choice.** Because the factor of 3–300 boost comes from assigning J1906+0746's pulsar to the (small) secondary, the headline binary preference is materially sensitive to one modeling decision about one system. This deserves a clearer sensitivity statement (e.g., report B_12 with and without that assignment).

## Minor points

- The "decisive/did-not-change with σ_theo" robustness checks are good but only explore two extremes; a log-grid would be more convincing.
- Fig. 2's claim of "essentially no model with fallback" matching the data is a bit strong given the selection argument above.
- The paper would benefit from stating explicitly that the *absence* of high-mass NS in DNS is expected from binary-evolution selection (dual-epoch supernovae) and not a clean probe of fallback, to preempt the most likely reader objection.

## Bottom line

A technically competent and cleanly executed Bayesian comparison whose statistical machinery is sound and whose *statistical* result (binary mass-ratio distributions beat independent-IMF sampling for the DNS system) is a legitimate, interesting, and reasonably novel contribution. But the *physical* claims — no fallback and the explosion localized to S/N_A ≈ 2.8 k_B at the iron-core edge — rest on a single simulation code with arbitrary injection physics and on a six-system, intrinsically low-mass, fallback-depleted sample, and they are presented more strongly than the evidence warrants. With a clearer separation of "what the DNS statistically prefer" from "what this says about the explosion mechanism," and with the hyperparameter/sensitivity issues addressed, the paper would be noticeably stronger. As it stands it is a solid, niche contribution with an over-claimed headline.

## [1204.5478v2-expert-collegue-review] Expert Collegue Review


# Review: "The observed neutron star mass distribution as a probe of the supernova explosion mechanism"
**Pejcha, Thompson & Kochanek (MNRAS, 2019)**

## Summary

The authors use the observed masses of the six known double neutron star (DNS) systems as a probe of core-collapse supernova physics. Rather than fitting a phenomenological mass function to the remnants (as in Kizilay et al. 2010 and Öztel et al. 2012), they build a **Bayesian framework that directly compares the six measured (M₁, M₂) pairs to remnant-mass predictions** from the artificial-explosion models of Zhang et al. (2008). The model space spans two metallicity compositions (Z=0 and Z=Z⊙), three piston positions (S/N_A = 4 k_B, Y_e core), a range of explosion energies, and two ways of drawing binary components (independent vs. uniform/twin mass ratio). They conclude that (i) binary-drawing models are strongly favored over independent sampling, (ii) little or no fallback is preferred at both compositions, and (iii) the most probable models place the explosion at the **edge of the deoxygenized (Y_e) core**, S/N_A ≈ 2.8 k_B — a specific claim about where the shock launch occurs.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated and well-written.** The physical premise — that a tight remnant-mass distribution (mean ≈ 1.33 M☉, dispersion ≈ 0.06 M☉) carries genuine information about the explosion mechanism and progenitor structure, not merely about progenitor mass — is sound and cleanly argued in §1.
- **Genuine methodological advance.** Comparing the *data directly to physical model output* via a likelihood rather than interpolating a mass function is a real step beyond Kizilay/Öztel, and the Bayesian comparison of models with unequal numbers of parameters (via Bayes factors) is done correctly.
- **Careful treatment of the primary/secondary assignment.** The Press (1997) mixture over p_i, and the decision to adopt p_i = δ(p_i−1) except for 1J1906+0746 (where they correctly show the alternative is ~300× more favored), is handled properly.
- **Honest self-assessment.** The authors are candid about the small sample, the single set of 1D single-star models, the "somewhat arbitrary" piston positions, and the limits of the inference. The "frequencyist diversion" (all 6 systems inside a 75% contour, vs. 4.5±1.1 expected) is a transparent and defensible consistency check.

## Weaknesses and concerns

- **The sample size is the fundamental limitation.** With only 6 systems — and the authors note that the *width* of the distribution is what drives the inference — the statistical power is very low. A single additional 1.5 M☉ system, or even a 7th system, would reshuffle the model ranking. The strong-sounding "decisive" preferences (B₁₂ ~ 10⁶ for binary vs. independent) are fragile at this N; the error bars in Fig. 3 are often an order of magnitude.
- **Entirely dependent on one model grid (Zhang et al. 2008).** Every conclusion maps back to 1D, non-rotating single-star progenitors with simple momentum-piston "explosions." The "explosion energy" is an injected momentum, not a physical explosion energy, and the Ugliano et al. (2012) self-consistent-fallback calculation in the Appendix is a different population (low-mass, ~10²× less probable) and so largely sidelined. The headline "explosion at the Y_e core" claim inherits all the arbitrariness of the entropy table.
- **Model space is narrow.** One metallicity per panel, two piston positions, uniform log M_max with no physical constraint, no rotation/magnetic-field channels, and no stochasticity. The strong preference for *no* fallback is in tension with the fact that a single 2 M☉ system (1614−2230, noted in footnote 5) would demand it — the inference hangs on the absence of such a star, which is a low-information statement at N=6.
- **The "edge of the deoxygenized core" interpretation is suggestive, not established.** It is a nice and interesting picture, but it is a model-by-model extrapolation over a coarse entropy grid, and the difference between S/N_A = 4 k_B and 2.8 k_B is modest; the authors are right to call it a "fingerprint" rather than a measurement.

## Recommendation

This is a solid, carefully executed methodology paper that establishes a reusable framework and an appealing physical probe. Its main value is the demonstration that the DNS mass *distribution* — not just the mean — tightly constrains fallback and shock-launch physics, combined with the honest quantification of what 6 systems can and cannot tell us. Its main weakness is that the inference is statistically thin and entirely hostage to a single 1D model set. I would recommend acceptance with the caveat that the authors should (a) make explicit throughout how quickly the model ranking changes under the addition of a single system, (b) treat the "Y_e core" conclusion as a hypothesis-generating result rather than a measured location, and (c) strengthen the Appendix comparison so the Ugliano et al. self-consistent models are engaged on a more comparable footing.

**Minor points:** the 75%-contour / 4.5±1.1 "diversion" would read better as a one-sided consistency statistic; Fig. 3's error bars on the assignment priors are hard to interpret without a note; the choice of σ_theo = 0.025 M☉ (vs. the 0.01–0.1 M☉ tested) is a free knob that deserves a sentence more of justification.

Overall: a competent, honest contribution of moderate importance — useful as a framework and a proof of concept, but its specific physical conclusions are too tentative and its statistics too small to shift the field's view on the explosion mechanism.

## [1204.5478v2-critique] Critique


# Critique: Pejcha, Thompson & Kochanek (2009) — *The observed neutron star mass distribution as a probe of the supernova explosion mechanism*

## What the paper does

The authors build a Bayesian framework to compare the observed mass distribution of the six double-neutron-star (DNS) systems measured at the time (Özel et al. 2012) against theoretical predictions of NS birth masses derived from 1D artificial-explosion models (Zhang et al. 2008). The comparison runs over several physical choices: independent vs. binary progenitor distributions (uniform vs. "twin" mass-ratio priors), explosion energy, piston position (S/N_A = 4 k_B vs. the Y_e core ≈ 2.9 k_B), metallicity (primordial vs. solar), and whether remnant masses include fallback. They conclude that the data strongly favor: a genuine binary mass-ratio distribution (B₁₂ ~ 10²–10⁵), essentially no fallback, a solar-composition progenitor, and an explosion initiated near the edge of the delaminated (Y_e) core, S/N_A ≈ 2.8 k_B.

## Strengths

- **Methodologically clean.** The hierarchical marginal likelihood (Eq. 1–2) is handled correctly, and the use of Bayes factors rather than ad-hoc χ² comparisons is appropriate for models with different numbers of free parameters. The Gaussian P_i(**D**|**M**) with analytic Gaussian kernels (Eqs. 7, 8) is a sensible computational choice, and the midpoint-rule integration is justified.
- **The primary/secondary ambiguity is handled properly.** Accounting for both assignments via p_i (Press 1997, Eq. 6) and showing that the 1J1906+0746 pulsar almost certainly came from the secondary (factor ~300, in agreement with Lorimer et al. 2006) is a nice physical cross-check.
- **Honest sensitivity analyses.** Varying σ_theo (0.01–0.05 M_☉), M_max, the free lower cutoff M_min, and the Salpeter-slope minimum all leave the qualitative ranking intact. The Ugliano et al. (2012) 1D fallback distribution is folded into an appendix, demonstrating the framework generalizes and that it shifts the preferred distribution by ~0.1 M_☉ in a physically sensible direction.
- **The "no fallback" inference is well framed.** They are explicit that the fallback is baked into the Zhang et al. high-M relation via the binding-energy term (Eq. 9), so the comparison is genuinely a fallback comparison and not a tautology.

## Weaknesses

1. **Severe small-sample fragility masked by dramatic Bayes factors.** N = 6 systems with per-point errors of ~0.007–0.018 M_☉ produces the reported B₁₂ values of 10²–10⁵. With such a tiny sample, Gaussian-likelihood Bayes factors over such small error bars overrepresent the discriminating power. The "decisive" language (Jeffreys) overstates significance; a single future DNS measurement with a mass >1.5 M_☉ — which the authors themselves flag as likely — would move everything. The paper gestures at this but the headline numbers invite over-reading.

2. **Over-reliance on a single 1D model set as the whole "theory space."** Everything physical flows from Zhang et al. (2008): a single artificial-explosion M′(M) relation, non-rotating progenitors, fixed metallicity. The "explosion at the Y_e core" conclusion is really "within the family of Zhang-2008 piston injections, the low-M/low-fallback one fits best." It cannot distinguish a genuine physical location of shock breakout from the mere shape (near-monotonicity and width) of M′(M); the S/N_A = 2.8 k_B number is an entropy-coordinate re-encoding of a model assumption, not a direct measurement.

3. **The binary model is physically naive for a "probe of the explosion mechanism."** Primaries are drawn independently from a Salpeter IMF and secondaries from a fixed q-distribution; there is no metallicity evolution of the progenitor, no common-envelope or mass-transfer reshaping (the authors acknowledge these), and no realistic population synthesis. The strong prefer

ence for "binary over independent" is robust to their checks, but it essentially tests "correlate versus don't correlate the two masses" — a property that any realistic binary population would have by construction, so the contrast is less informative than the B₁₂ magnitude suggests.

4. **Physical confounds go unaddressed.** Luminous Type-IIIa, stripping, and progenitor mass-loss history all shift M′(M); the DNS sample at solar metallicity is treated as a single fiducial point. The authors correctly note that LMC/low-metallicity SNe are the least-constrained theoretically, yet solar-metallicity progenitors are those least examined in modern multidimensional simulations — meaning the inference lands on models of lowest independent theoretical status.

5. **Unused observables.** Spin periods, the fact that these are all short-period recycled systems, and the non-detection (or rarity) of isolated-pulsar/DNS ratios are all consistent with binary survival but are not folded into the likelihood. A survival-modification to Eq. 8 (they only add a binary-fraction factor) is minimal.

## Verdict

This is a well-executed proof-of-concept that introduced a legitimate way to constrain the SN mechanism from the DNS mass distribution, at a time when such an exercise did not exist. The Bayesian machinery is sound, the treatment of component-assignment ambiguity is unusually careful, and the sensitivity checks are candid. However, the small N (6), the collapse of "theory space" onto one 1D model, and the dramatic-but-fragile Bayes factors mean the physical conclusions — particularly the precise "explosion at S/N_A ≈ 2.8 k_B" and "no fallback" statements — are best read as model-conditional statements rather than robust inferences about the explosion mechanism. Impact is real but modest: it seeded later work on using NS masses to probe explosions, and its framework was reused, but it did not itself change the field.

# Evaluation of arXiv:1204.5478 (Pejcha, Thompson & Kochanek)

## The stated contribution, in my own words

The authors propose using the remarkably tight observed mass distribution of neutron stars in double-neutron-star (DNS) systems — six systems with precise millisecond-pulsar timing masses (σ ≈ 0.06 M⊙, all ~1.34 M⊙) — as a direct quantitative test of supernova explosion physics. Their methodological device is a Bayesian framework in which the observed NS masses are connected *directly to physical explosion models*: instead of fitting a phenomenological (Gaussian-type) mass function as in Kiziltan et al. (2010) and Özel et al. (2012), they tabulate the remnant mass M′ as a function of progenitor mass M, explosion energy E, metallicity Z, and piston position (at S/N_X ≈ 4 k_B, the base of the oxygen-burning shell, or at the Y_e/"degeneracy" core), with fallback modeled as a uniform expansion of the NS mass range, using the artificially-piston-driven 1D simulations of Zhang et al. (2008). They then compute likelihoods and Bayes factors for competing hypotheses: (a) binary formation with independent (IMF-sampled) primaries vs. correlated mass-ratio distributions (uniform P(q), "twins"); (b) no fallback vs. significant fallback; (c) piston position and metallicity. Their headline findings: the binary models are preferred over the independent model by Bayes factors of ~5–200; no-fallback models are strongly preferred because fallback widens the mass dispersion, inconsistent with a tight observed distribution; and for solar metallicity the best match places the explosion at the degenerate-core edge, at S/N_X ≈ 2.8 k_B, rather than at the oxygen-burning base.

## What is and isn't new, given the authors' own citations

**Not new (explicitly credited as prior work, or already established by it):**

1. **The observational fact itself** — the tight DNS mass dispersion (~0.06 M⊙) and the argument that it constrains fallback growth and NS-formation physics is Kiziltan et al. (2010) and Özel et al. (2012). The data sample (Table 2) is literally Özel et al.'s.
2. **The phenomenological comparison the paper supersedes is prior work**: the binary-vs-independent comparison, the Salpeter IMF for primaries, and the two mass-ratio distributions (uniform P(q) and the 50/50 twins+uniform mix, per Pineda & Staneck-style work / Kobulnicky & Fryer 2007, Kochanek 2009) were all already proposed and fit to this same data by Kiziltan et al. (2010) and Özel et al. (2012). The conclusion that the binary origin is favored appears already qualitatively in Kiziltan et al.
3. **All supernova physics is imported, not derived**: the piston-driven remnant-mass relations M′(M, E, Z), the fallback "mass expansion" prescription (Woosley & Weaver 1995; Zhang et al. 2008), and the entropy-coordinates of the explosion (S/N_X = 4 k_B oxygen base; the ~2.8 k_B degenerate-core edge) come entirely from Woosley & Weaver 1995 and Zhang et al. 2008. The authors explicitly note the two Zhang piston positions are "somewhat arbitrary."
4. **The primary/secondary ambiguity treatment** follows Press (1997) and Lorimer et al. (2006)'s argument that J1906+0746's recycled pulsar is the secondary; what's new is only the *quantification* of how much (factors ~3–300) this choice matters.
5. The qualitative physics conclusions — no fallback preferred, dispersion reduction as the key diagnostic, explosion tied to core edge in solar metallicity — follow straightforwardly once you accept the Zhang table: no *surprise* physical mechanism is discovered.

**What is new:**

1. **The methodological bridge is genuinely new relative to the cited comparison**: coupling the *physical* remnant-mass function M′(M, E, Z, piston) directly into a joint 2D Bayesian likelihood over (M̄_A, M̄_B, σ_theo) for both components, including their individual timing uncertainties, and doing systematic model selection with Bayes factors (binary vs. independent; fallback on/off; piston position; Z=0 vs. Z⊙; M_max fixed 100 M⊙ vs. free). Kiziltan et al./Özel et al. used parameterized mass functions and simpler statistics; this is, as far as the citations show, the first direct physical-model comparison.
2. **Quantified, model-graded conclusions that prior work only gestured at**: Bayes factors 5–200 for the binary model; strong penalty on any fallback (Fig. 4); and the *inference* that the shock breakout occurs at S/N_X ≈ 2.8 k_B for solar metallicity (Fig. 6) — a specific, testable statement about the supernova coordinate of explosion that no cited work had extracted from the DNS masses.
3. **Use of the 2D joint distribution (mass difference *and* sum) as diagnostics** (Fig. 5), including the K-S-style check and the "no DNS with both masses > 1.5 M⊙" argument; the asymmetric shape of the binary model in (M_A, M_B) space (primaries cut off at M_min for NS production, secondaries cut off at M_max, Fig. 1 vs. 2) as the reason the binary model is favored is argued more explicitly than in the cited work.
4. The post-submission comparison with Ugliano et al. (2012)'s 1D fallback simulations (Appendix) — a useful but derivative add-on, showing their Zhang Y_e-core no-fallback model is favored by ~10×.

**Weak spots (affecting how much credit the novelty gets):** the whole inference rides on six data points, on the Zhang et al. 2008 tabulation whose piston choices are admittedly arbitrary, on the neglection of common-envelope/mass-transfer effects (the authors cite Kalogera 1996's disagreement between binary models and observations), and on an idealized no-fallback reading. The authors are honest about all of this ("whether current assumptions... are correct" limits the strength of the inference). The S/N_X ≈ 2.8 k_B result is elegant but is essentially confirming a prior expectation (explosion at the degenerate-core edge) that the authors themselves flag as unsurprising for many models — so the "probe of the explosion mechanism" framing is somewhat generous given that the data's real discriminative power is: (i) fallback must be small, (ii) the binary origin is favored, (iii) the mass spread is small.

## Verdict

## My understanding of the logical argument

**Motivation / causal chain.** A core-collapse supernova leaves behind either a neutron star (NS) or a black hole. The final NS mass is set by (i) the mass at core bounce, (ii) the amount of mass that falls back during the explosion, and (iii) stochastic effects. Because the remnant mass encodes the explosion physics and the core structure, the *distribution* of NS masses should be a diagnostic of the explosion mechanism. The most precise NS mass measurements exist in double-neutron-star (DNS) systems with recycled millisecond pulsars — ~6 systems with ~12 precise masses (Ozel et al. 2012/2013), with a remarkably tight dispersion of ~0.06 M☉ and a mean ~1.35 M☉.

**The conceptual advance.** Prior work (Kiziltan et al. 2010; Ozel et al. 2012) described the DNS mass distribution with *phenomenological analytic mass functions* and fit them to the data. The authors instead **replace the analytic mass function with physical SN models** and ask directly which physics (explosion energy, metallicity, fallback) reproduces the observed NS masses. This is the real point of the paper: moving the constraint from *"the data match some formula"* to *"the data match the predicted remnant masses from a specific explosion mechanism, traced back to the progenitor."*

**The Bayesian machinery.** For a hypothesis H with parameters θ: P(θ|H,D) ∝ P(θ|H)P(D|θ,H), comparing models by marginal likelihood (Bayes factors B₁₂), with priors fixed to 1 so only ratios matter. The data are N mass pairs; P(D|M) are Gaussians from the measurement errors. To get P(M|θ,H) they use two progenitor models:
- an **independent** model — each star drawn independently from a Salpeter IMF (conceptual control, ignores correlations);
- a **binary** model — primaries from the Salpeter IMF, secondaries from a mass-ratio distribution P(q), truncated at the minimum-mass-for-NS-production cutoff.

They integrate analytically over the (continuous) mapping M′(M) from Zhang et al. 2008 by swapping integration order (midpoint rule). Crucially, they marginalize over explosion energy E and the maximum-progenitor-mass M_max with a uniform prior.

**A subtle, important detail.** The observed "pulsar" in a DNS does not unambiguously correspond to the *initially more massive* primary. Binary theory favors that identification, but for J1906+0746 the recycled pulsar is *more massive* than its companion — hard if it came from the primary. So they test P(p_i)=0 (pulsar from the secondary) for that system, which boosts the binary models by a factor ~3 to ~300 relative to the default P(p_i)=δ(p_i−1).

**Best model and headline results.** Of the Zhang et al. 2008 set (piston at S/N_A = 4 k_B or at the Y_e core; zero or solar metallicity; with or without fallback), the best model is **no fallback, remnant = Y_e core mass, solar metallicity, uniform P(q), binary** — i.e. an explosion initiated at the edge of the deprocessed core. - strong preference for binary over independent models (B~5–200) and little difference between uniform and twin P(q);
- no-fallback models are far preferred over fallback models (B₁₂ ~ 10²·⁸ for the most extreme comparison);

- the observed distribution is *narrower* than all model predictions, even with an M_max cutoff applied.

The observed minimum *total* mass (~2.6 M☉) is also noticeably higher than the 2.2–2.4 M☉ predicted by all models.

(2008) was the only tabulated remnant-mass relation M′(ℳ, Z, E) from artificially initiated explosions available at submission (2012) and still at acceptance (2018); 2D/3D models (Marek & Janka, Müller et al., Scheck et al., O'Connor & Ott) produced no systematic (Z, M, E) → remnant tables, and the authors state this limitation explicitly. Population-synthesis mass ratios (Belczynski et al. 2010) are cited and judged incompatible with DNS rates. The six systems are exactly the six precise-mass DNS systems in the Özel et al. (2012) catalog — current at the time. The 1.97 M_⊙ object M1614−2230 is mentioned but excluded (recycled? disputed birth mass), a conservative, defensible choice. The main currency issue: a paper written in 2012/2013 (v2 = Oct 2018) sits behind the remnant-mass literature by several years, but no replacement baseline existed in the required functional form.

### 3. Error bars and statistical significance

**This is the weakest part of the paper.**

- Individual mass measurements carry 1σ Gaussian errors (Table 2), propagated through the likelihood (eq. 5); Fig. 3 shows 1σ/2σ on the Bayes-factor ratios. Good for the input data.
- **No error bar on S/N_A ≃ 2.8 k_B.** Fig. 6 is a broad curve, peaking only ~10⁵× over a wide 2.4–3.2 plateau, with no confidence interval, no model-averaging uncertainty, no mention of the arbitrary 2.0–4.0 entropy window, and no statement of how sensitive the peak is to the σ_theo and pᵥ choices that move things by orders of magnitude elsewhere.
- **No p-values or significance statements for the Bayes factors themselves.** Six systems, many models compared (metabolic explosion: 8 Zhang et al. 2008 entries × binary/independent × q-law × pᵥ — dozens of hypotheses), and no multiple-comparison correction or posterior-predictive validation beyond the single KS-flavored check. The 0.178 "no systems outside the 95% contour" number is suggestive, not a test, and is presented as a "frequentist diversion."
- Small-sample risk is acknowledged qualitatively ("only six systems") but not quantified (no bootstrap/jackknife on B₁₂).

## Verdict

A careful, unusually self-critical statistical paper. Its robustness checks are better than norm, its caveats are explicit, and the conclusion (little fallback; explosion at ~2.8 k_B entropy for solar-metallicity DNS progenitors) is genuinely interesting and consistent with later 2D/3D thinking. But the strongest numbers rest on: (i) a deliberately weak control model, (ii) a single 1D explosion-model source with arbitrary piston positions, (iii) six systems, and (iv) assumptions (J1906+0746 assignment, S/N_A = 4 "no fallback" identification) that move answers by factors of 10–100 — none of which receive formal error bars. The S/N_A = 2.8 k_B claim in particular is closer to "a broad preference" than a measurement.

## [1204.5478v2-weak-point] Weakest link in the argument


## The single weakest link

**The inference that the small observed neutron‑star mass dispersion forces "no fallback," and therefore that solar‑metallicity supernovae initiate at the edge of the iron core ($S/N_A \approx 2.8\,k_B$).** This is the step where the conclusion stops being entailed by the data.

Everything in the paper is a carefully built Bayesian machinery, but it rests on one non‑trivial physical leap: *measured DNS mass width* → *little/no fallback* → *explosion coordinate / entropy value.* Three things make that leap under‑determined:

**1. The data do not pin the explosion energy, so fallback is constrained only through the width — and the width is a 6‑point variance estimate.** Figure 4 shows the Bayes factors are essentially flat in $E$ over $10^{50}$–$10^{53}$ ergs. Since the *amount of fallback is set by $E$* (low $E$ → strong fallback → wide remnant range; see the flat dashed lines and Fig 1 vs Fig 2), the data never measure $E$ and hence never measure fallback directly. The entire "no fallback" verdict rests on one number: the dispersion of the six systems ($\sigma\approx0.06\,M_\odot$) being much narrower than the fallback models' intrinsic spread. That is a low‑statistics variance argument — essentially a 2–3$\sigma$ rejection of a broader distribution from six points, one of which (J1906+0746) the authors flag as atypical. Ranking "narrow model ≫ wide model" is plausible, not entailed.

**2. The likelihood conditions on observed masses but not on the DNS‑survival selection function — and survival itself depends on $E$ and metallicity (i.e. on fallback).** The six systems are a *surviving* binary subset: both SNs must succeed, produce kicks that don't disrupt the pair, and avoid common‑envelope ejection. A wide‑in‑principle fallback model could yield a *narrow* observed DNS dispersion if the very fallback that widens the remnant range also preferentially leaves the low‑mass‑remnant / lower‑$E$ systems as the ones that survive as tight binaries. The model comparison never integrates over this selection; only the *chosen* best model gets a rough survival check (Sec 3.3, "adding this had no significant consequence"). So "the DNS we happened to observe have tight masses" does not entail "all SNs have tight remnant masses," and therefore does not entail "SNs explode without fallback."

**3. Even granting no fallback, the specific entropy and iron‑core localization come from a single, two‑piston, 1‑D model set the authors themselves call arbitrary.** The flat $M'(M)$ "no fallback" relation (remnant = core mass at some piston) is what actually fits, and *any* piston sitting in a core region where the core mass barely varies with progenitor mass reproduces it. The numeric value $S/N_A\approx2.8\,k_B$ and the claim "edge of the deionized / iron core" are not measured — they are read off Zhang et al. (2008) at the one piston position that happens to be the Y$_e$ core, where the authors state the two sampled positions are "somewhat arbitrary" and note that a true explosion coordinate (whether the shock stalls inside the Fe core or near the Si/Si interface) is a 2D/3D hydrodynamics question the 1D nucleosynthesis tables simply cannot answer. The Appendix Ugliano comparison reinforces this: a *newer, fallback‑including* 1D model also fits the DNS (roughly an order of magnitude worse), so "no fallback" is a preference of the model family, not a data‑driven fact.

**Why this is *the* weakest step:** the methodology (Eqs 1–9) is sound and the comparison is honest, so every quantitative detail up to this point tracks the data. The break occurs at the sentence "a flat/no‑fallback remnant relation fits well ⇒ the explosion occurs at the iron‑core edge, $S/N_A\approx2.8\,k_B$." That physical statement requires (i) a 6‑point variance to rule out fallback, (ii) ignoring a selection function that is itself correlated with the property being tested, and (iii) trusting an external, self‑declared‑arbitrary two‑piston 1D lens to convert "flat remnant mass" into a specific entropy. None of these is entailed by the six pairs of masses; together they carry 100% of the physics conclusion while being the least constrained part of the analysis. (Secondary, but not the weakest, weaknesses: the hard assumption $P(p_1)=\delta(p_1-1)$ that the pulsar always came from the more massive star — tested only for J1906+0746 — and the Salpeter‑slope choice.)

## [1204.5478v2-reproducibility] Reproducibility audit


## Paper

Pejcak, Thompson & Kochanek, MNRAS 2018 — *"The observed neutron star mass distribution as a probe of the supernova explosion mechanism."*

## Framing

This is a Bayesian **analysis** paper in stellar astrophysics, not a machine-learning or LLM paper. So the rubric items "prompts" and "pretraining details" are not applicable, "compute" is trivial (deterministic 1‑D integrals, no Monte Carlo), and there is no random **seed** to report — the only stochastic ingredient in the whole workflow is none; the authors explicitly swap the order of integration so the Bayes factors are evaluated analytically with a midpoint rule (Sec. 2.3). The real reproducibility question is: given the paper alone, can someone else recompute Figures 1, 3, 5 and 6 and the Bayes factors?

## What the paper gives (and would make re-implementation feasible)

- **The full statistical model**: Eqs. (1)–(8) define the posterior, the Bayes factor, the independent (Eq. 7) and binary (Eq. 8) remnant-mass likelihoods, the prior over progenitor masses, and the mass-ratio prior.
- **All hyper-priors, stated numerically**: Salpeter IMF α = 2.35; mass-ratio prior either uniform on [0.2, 1.0] or the "twins" mixture (0.02–0.9 / 0.9–1, half each, with 0.2≤q); free M_max (best-fit 14–35 M⊙, prior uniform 10–100 M⊙); σ_théo = 0.01 M⊙ (default) and a check at 0.05 M⊙; Gaussian P(D|M) per system.
- **The observational data** in Table 2: all 7 DNS/pulsar systems with pulsar mass ± error, companion mass ± error, spin periods and references (the source being Öz el et al. 2012).
- **The model grid** in Table 1: for each scenario (Z = 0 and Z⊙; piston at S/N_A = 4 k_B or Y_e core; with/without fallback) the progenitor mass range, M_min, and the exploration energies.
- **The physics closures**: binding-energy correction Eq. (9) (Lattimer & Yahil 1989, 0.084 M⊙ leading factor), the 2.0 M⊙ maximum NS mass cut, primary/secondary assignment via Eq. (6), and how M_min/M_max enter the integration limits.

In isolation this is a well-specified framework.

## What is missing that would block (or strain) an independent re-implementation

1. **No released code, and no data files.** There is no repository, no archive link, no "code available on request." The analysis is simple enough to type up from the equations, so this is a low-to-moderate barrier, but a reader cannot simply download and run it.

2. **The core physical input — the remnant-mass function M′(M) — is not tabulated here.** The entire likelihood depends on the mapping from initial progenitor mass to final remnant mass *for each of the (Z, piston, energy) models*. The paper only cites it ("as tabulated in Zhang et al. 2008, in Table 1") and describes *how* Zhang et al. produced it (positioning a piston, injecting momentum, no fallback = setting M = M_core). It never dumps the actual (M → M′) values, so a re-implementer must obtain the Zhang et al. 2008 and Ugliano et al. 2012 remnant-mass tables (or re-run Woosley & Weaver 1995 progenitors with the *exact* same artificial-explosion protocol — piston positions, energy-injection prescription, and the "5 to 10 points … where no directly calculated progenitors are available"). Without that external table the contours in Fig 1/2 and all Bayes factors are not reproducible.

3. **The interpolation scheme for M′(M) is unspecified.** The authors state they smooth a discrete, "not intrinsically smooth" remnant-mass distribution that has "significant jumps in M′ between progenitors of similar masses," and integrate over the *binary* with a midpoint rule but over M with 5–10 points where no direct progenitor exists. The exact interpolation / discretization choice is not described, and a non-smooth M′(M) makes the Bayes factors sensitive to it.

4. **Secondary specifics.** The precise form of how secondaries are "cut off at M_max" vs primaries "cut off at M_min"; the exact constant values for the maximum NS mass; and the survival-probability extension (Sec. 3.3) uses Kalogera (1996) masses only qualitatively ("We tried a number of relative kick velocities … no significant consequences"). Minor, but they would need to be guessed in a bit-for-bit re-implementation.

## Bottom line on reproducibility

- **Framework + data + priors:** reproducible — everything except the remnant-mass table is stated or tabulated.
- **Physical input M′(M):** *not* reproducible from the paper alone — it must be pulled from Zhang et al. 2008 / Ugliano et al. 2012, plus re-creating the artificial-explosion protocol if those tables are unavailable/insufficient.
- **Code/seeds/compute:** no code released; but no seeds and negligible compute, so the only effort is re-typing the 1‑D integrals.

The single true blocker is the missing remnant-mass distribution table (item 2), compounded by the unspecified interpolation (item 3) and the absence of any code release (item 1).

## Scores

Originality **3** — a careful Bayesian re-framing that compares DNS mass distributions to physical SN models directly instead of parametrically, but it rides on the Öz el et al. DNS data, the Zhang et al. 2008 remnant models, and the existing "little fallback / Y_e-core-edge" themes (the core result overlaps prior parametric comparisons). Sound but not a paradigm shift.

Overall **3** — internally consistent, well-specified, honest about limitations; held back by very weak statistics (6 systems, 12 masses), reliance on simplified 1‑D non‑rotating artificial explosions, and results that hinge on the arbitrary piston positions. Conclusions are suggestive rather than decisive.

Impact **3** — a solid, useful contribution to the "DNS masses as SN probes" line of work (and the S/N_A ≈ 2.8 k_B edge-of-Y_e-core claim is a memorable, citable result), but not field-shaping on its own given the small dataset and the dependence on external remnant-mass tables.

## [1204.5478v2-writing-framing] Writing and framing critique


## Framing: where the abstract outruns the analysis

The paper's central move — build a Bayesian likelihood on the DNS mass pair (M_A, M_B) from Zhang et al. (2008) remnant functions, compare binary vs. independent progenitor draws and different piston positions, and read the argmax as where the explosion was initiated — is coherent and well executed in the body. Two things, however, prevent the abstract and title from being as strong as the paper *is*:

1. **The sample is six binaries** (twelve masses). The paper's own "frequentist" cross-check in §3.2 (p 7, right column) reports that the best model has a 0.75⁶ = 0.178 probability of containing all six systems in its 75 % contour. That is *not rejected*, but it is also not a *test*; it is a null. The abstract's wording ("excellent probe," "directly connect") treats a null as a confirmation.
2. **The "probe" is really a probe of the remnant-mass function of one specific SN model family (Zhang et al. 2008)**. The paper does not compare independent SN simulations on their own terms; it compares *remnant-mass maps* tabulated by one group, with two piston positions the authors themselves call "somewhat arbitrary" (§3.2, p. 6). The title's "probe of the supernova explosion mechanism" over-reaches what is demonstrated.

## Sentence-level overclaims

- **Abstract, "models with standard binary mass ratio distributions are strongly preferred over independently picking the masses from the initial mass function."** In §3.2 the B₁₂ range is *5 to 200* depending on model. On the paper's own Jefferies-scale table (Eq. 3 discussion, p. 3), B₁₂ ~ 5 sits below the "substantial" threshold of ~3 only marginally, and the high end reaches "decisive" at 200. "Strongly" is not right for the low end; "prefer … substantially" would be honest.
- **Abstract, "NS formation models with no mass fallback are favored because they reduce the dispersion in NS masses."** This is only true *inside* the Zhang et al. (2008) family. The Appendix (p. 13) explicitly says the Ugliano et al. The main conclusion is therefore model-family-specific, and the paper never restates this caveat in the abstract.
- **Abstract, "The double NS system masses thus directly connect to the mass coordinate where the supernova explosion was initiated, making them an excellent probe of the supernova explosion mechanism."** "Directly" is doing a lot of work. The connection is conditional on (a) no fallback, (b) a specific M′(M) relation drawn from Woosley & Weaver (2002), (c) the assumption that M′(M) is identical for primaries and secondaries (§2.3, p. 3, stated without independent justification), (d) negligible second-mass-transfer (0.03 M☉, which §1 claims via a single citation to Özel et al. 2012 rather than a measured value). Each of those is a link in a chain, not a "direct" relation.
- **Abstract, "the explosion most likely develops at the edge of the iron core at a specific entropy of S/N_A ≃ 2.8 k_B."** The number is quoted to three significant digits, but the underlying map is tabulated at only 2–3 piston positions per metallicity (Table I), and the authors concede (§3.2, p. 6) that these positions "are somewhat arbitrary." The value is really "2.8 ± 1 k_B, for Zhang et al. 2008, no fallback, Z=Z_☉, uniform P(q)." The "≃ 2.8 k_B" presentation in the abstract conceals the conditional character.
- **§1, p. 2, "the mean masses of the pulsars and their companions differ by only 0.03 M☉ (Özel et al. 2012). Thus, both observations basically represent the birth masses of the NSs."** The "Thus" makes a broad conclusion from one paper's 0.03 M☉ mean, cited without independent verification. Post-SN accretion onto the companion (from fallback and from the second SN) is not negligible on the 0.06 M☉ dispersion the paper measures — if anything, 0.03 M☉ on top of a 0.06 M☉ dispersion is a significant fraction, and the paper's own §3.3 admits "it is believed the NS masses are little affected by mass transfer, this may not hold for the progenitors" only in the discussion of BH-NS systems, not here. A hedged "suggest" would be more appropriate than "Thus … basically."
- **§3.1, p. 5, "The case of J1906+0746 is peculiar because the pulsar is significantly less massive than the companion … our results show that it is unlikely to have originated from the more massive progenitor."** This per-system inference rests on a single measurement, and later in the same paragraph the authors show that the *opposite* assignment boosts the binary vs. independent Bayes factor by "3 to ~300." A 300× factor on the system that the headline claim relies on is not a detail to bury in a paragraph. The "unlikely" should be "unlikely for this specific parametrization of M′(M), but with a factor-of-300 sensitivity to the prior on which star was more massive at SN time."
- **§4, p. 10, "This model also appears to match the data in an absolute sense (Figs. 1 and 5)."** Given the 0.178 "frequentist" null reported above, "appears to match" is charitable language for "we fail to reject the model, but with six systems the null has no discriminating power." An honest sentence would be "we cannot reject this model, but we also cannot rule out any of the other curves in Fig. 5 at this sample size."

A more defensible title would be "… as a constraint on remnant-mass models" or "… in a Bayesian comparison of DN progenitor models."

# Peer Review

**Title:** The observed neutron star mass distribution as a probe of the supernova explosion mechanism
**Authors:** Pejcha, Thompson & Kochanek (MNRAS, 2012; arXiv:1204.5478v2)

## Summary

The paper argues that the tight, narrow distribution of neutron-star (NS) masses in double NS (DNS) binaries encodes information about which coordinate in the progenitor the explosion was initiated at (i.e., the supernova explosion mechanism), not just the fallback mass. The authors build a Bayesian framework that directly links the observed two-NS-component mass pair to theoretical remnant-mass functions $M'(\mathcal{M})$ from Zhang et al. (2008), and compares a suite of models—differing in explosion energy, progenitor metallicity, piston position (O-shell base vs. delayed-core $\gamma_e$), independent-vs-genuine binary mass-ratio sampling, and the treatment of the J1906+0746 pulsar/secondary assignment. The headline result is a strong preference for models with little or no fallback, a binary (rather than independent) mass distribution, solar metallicity, and an explosion initiated at the edge of the deoxygenated core (entropy $S/N_A \approx 2.8\,k_B$).

## General Assessment

This is a genuinely clever and well-executed idea. The core insight—that the *dispersion* and *total mass* of the DNS distribution, not just the mean NS mass, probes the fallback physics and thus the explosion coordinate—is a real advance over prior phenomenological fits (Kizutani, Özel & Youn & Cantillette). The Bayesian formulation is clean, transparent, and appropriately humble about its limitations. The writing is clear, the figures are excellent, and the physical interpretation lands. This is above the average level of astrophysical-in-ference-of-this-kind work.

## Specific Points (strengths)

1. **The "narrow total-mass" argument is the paper's strongest contribution.** Fig. 5 shows the observed total masses ($M_A+M_B$) have a much narrower cumulative distribution than every fallback model produces. No-fallback, $S/N_A\approx 4\,k_B$ solar-metallicity models minimize this width. This is a physically motivated diagnostic, not just a χ² toy.
2. **Proper treatment of the primary/secondary ambiguity.** Section 3.1 and the J1906+0746 marginalization (Eq. 6) correctly handle a real systematic that most analyses gloss over, and they quantify its impact (factor 3–300).
3. **Appropriate sensitivity analysis.** The authors vary $\sigma_{\rm theo}$, $\mathcal{M}_{\rm max}$, $M_{cc}$, survival probability, and $\mathcal{M}_{\min}$, and show the main conclusions are robust. The frequentist KS cross-check (Sec. 3.2) adds credibility.
4. **Intellectual honesty.** Sec. 3.3 and the Discussion openly concede that the strong no-fallback preference might simply reflect that Zhang et al.'s models don't produce fallback at LMC solar metallicity, and that the conclusions depend on which simulations are available.

## Weaknesses / concerns

1. **Extreme model-dependence is the central liability, and it is only partially mitigated.** The entire inference rests on the Zhang et al. (2008) 1D, artificial-explosion, non-rotating tabulations. The authors acknowledge this repeatedly, but because they have only *one* set of simulations to work from, their "solar metallicity preferred" and "explosion at $S/N_A\approx2.8\,k_B$" results are effectively statements about *which of these particular simulations matches the data*, not about nature. This limits the claim from "probe of the SN mechanism" to "a probe conditional on Zhang et al.'s model set." This caveat is stated but perhaps should be more prominently a limitation in the abstract.
2. **Small sample.** Six precise mass measurements is a thin foundation. The authors themselves note (Sec. 3.3) that a 7th system could easily overturn conclusions. The inference is essentially being driven by a handful of constraints, which makes the "decisive" Bayes-factor language in Sec. 2.1 overstated relative to the N=6 reality.
3. **The two piston positions in Zhang et al. # Review of "The observed neutron star mass distribution as a probe of the supernova explosion mechanism" (Pejcha, Thompson & Kochanek)

## Overview
This is a genuine step beyond the prior phenomenological work (Kizilman et al. 2010, Özel et al. 2012). The central idea—replace the free Gaussian description of the mass function with the residual mass function from an actual supernova nucleosynthesis model (Zhang et al. 2008) and do a proper Bayesian comparison against the six precisely-measured double neutron star (DNS) systems—is a clean and well-motivated reframing. The two results you land on (binary over independent origin; little-to-no fallback, with the solar-metallicity explosion located at the edge of the Y_e core, S/N_A ≈ 2.8 k_B) are the interesting payoff. The Bayesian formalism in Section 2 is laid out competently, and the fact that the integrals are analytically reducible because of the Gaussian P(D|M) is a nice practical detail that keeps the computation honest.

## What you did well
- **The conceptual link is real.** Because NS birth masses are set by where the explosion was initiated and how much fallback occurred, the unexpectedly tight DNS mass distribution (σ ≈ 0.06 M_☉) is a genuine handle on the explosion physics, not just a demographic curiosity. Making that argument quantitatively rather than by eye is the contribution.
- **The primary/secondary assignment (Press 1997, Lorimer et al. 2006) is handled correctly.** Fixing P(p_i)=1 for most systems but treating J1906+0746 as δ-prior on the two assignments, and showing the binary models are favored by a factor ~3–300 in that case, is the right level of care. This is the part of the whole analysis that most threatens the "binary over independent" conclusion, and you do not hand-wave it.
- **Honest about the "frequentist" diversion.** The KDE-style KS comparison in §3.3 / Fig. 5 (all binary pairs inside the 75% contour, 0.75⁶ ≈ 0.178 vs. a typical 4.5 ± 1.1 systems) is a reasonable way to sanity-check a Bayes factor that is built on a small, possibly biased sample.
- **The Appendix is the intellectually honest piece.** You bring in Ugliano et al. (2012) after submission and report that it is disfavored by ~10⁹ relative to the Y_e-core no-fallback Zhang models. Reporting that you *tried* a competitor and it lost is exactly what a student should do, and it shields the main claim from the obvious "you only used one model set" objection.

## Where the argument is weaker, and what I'd want you to fix

**1. The entire inference is hostage to one model set.** You compare *within* Zhang et al. (2008) (piston position, metallicity, fallback on/off, M_min, binary-vs-independent) but the absolute preference for "no fallback" and for "edge of the Y_e core" is really a statement about how the Zhang residual-mass curve falls off, not a robust physical conclusion. Your own footnote 3 varies σ_theo from 0.01–0.1 M_☉ and the conclusion holds—that's good—but you do not vary the *shape* of M'(M) within a family. The Ugliano comparison in the Appendix shows the conclusion is sensitive to the fallback prescription (peaks shift by 0.1 M_☉), so "no fallback" is more "we didn't model the fallback that real simulations require" than "fallback is physically zero." I would reframe this headline. In fact your Discussion already gestures at this ("the preference for little or no fallback could either be a feature of the SN mechanism or a consequence of mass loss"), but the Abstract and title oversell it. A senior reader will read "no fallback" as a physical claim you do not actually have.

**2. The "S/N_A ≈ 2.8 k_B" result is the one I'd defend least confidently.** Fig. 6 shows a broad, flat plateau between 2.0 and 3.0 k_B with a maximum at 2.8, and you acknowledge the two piston positions in Zhang et al. (2008) were "somewhat arbitrary." The width of that posterior (a factor of order unity in the S/N_A coordinate) is comparable to the difference between "Y_e core" (4 k_B) and the edge of the Y_e core (~2.8). You are really distinguishing two values that are 4 k_B vs 2.8 k_B, where your posterior is flat from 2–3 and rises steeply only above ~4. Given (a) that rotation, magnetic fields, and finite progenitor rotation are all absent from the 1-D simulations, and (b) that a factor-of-two spread in S/N_A corresponds to a small mass difference, I would state this as "the explosion is consistent with being initiated near the edge of the Y_e core and disfavors a piston deep in the O/Ne shell" rather than a crisp "explodes at S/N_A ≈ 2.8 k_B."

**3. N = 6 and the IMF/upper-mass truncation dependence.** You vary M_min and M_max and show little change in relative probabilities—but the preference for binary over independent, and especially the absolute mass-distribution width, depend on drawing secondaries from a Salpeter IMF truncated at 100 M_☉ and on the Sana 2012 / Portegies Zwart mass-ratio distributions. A 25 Myr DNS with a 1.65 M_☉ pulsar near a 1.5 M_☉ companion (the "red supergiant problem" system) is exactly the kind of system at the boundary of these priors. I'd want a short sensitivity paragraph: what if the upper IMF cutoff is 30 M_☉ vs 60 M_☉? The "supergiant problem" (footnote 10, ~2.4 σ) is a symptom that your IMF and SN-mass priors are being strained, and it deserves more than a footnote.

**4. The binary-survival treatment is an afterthought.** Kolgrova (1996) kick velocities added "with no significant consequence"—fine, but the mass-transfer survival of the primary (which sets the helium-core mass and hence the NS mass) is the dominant DNS-formation channel per Belczynski. You note the primary's secondary mass at the moment of the first explosion is what matters, but you do not tie the *fallback* conclusion to whether common-envelope ejection occurred for each system. This is where a no-fallback result could be confounded: if a common envelope removed the primary's envelope *and* reduced the accretable mass, the "no fallback" you infer could be envelope loss masquerading as explosion physics. Your mass-conservation argument (M_A + M_B ≈ core mass) is the right check, but it only holds if the common-envelope mass is returned to the system; state that assumption explicitly.

**5. Minor points.** You assume the NS mass uncertainty in the theoretical models is σ_theo and the observed Gaussian uncertainties, but the Ozel (2012) DNS masses have systematic (not just σ) errors from spin/orbital fitting that are not Gaussian; your "frequentist" KDE sidesteps this but the Bayes factor does not. The choice M_min prior uniform in M_min and P(M_max) uniform in ln M_max (10–100) is reasonable, but the "best-fit" M_min values spanning 1.4–33.5 M_☉ depend on the inference method (Fig. caption) is a wide range that you should discuss rather than just tabulate.

## Verdict
This is solid, above-average student work: the framing is genuinely novel, the Bayesian machinery is correctly implemented, and the authors are commendably transparent about the model dependence (σ_theo variation, Ugliano appendix). The core weakness is that the two headline results—"no fallback" and "explosion at S/N_A ≈ 2.8 k_B"—rest on a single 1-D model family and are stated with more precision than the posterior justifies. As it stands it reads like it has discovered a physical fact; what it has really done is show that the observed DNS mass distribution is *in tension with fallback-driven expansion* and *consistent with explosions initiated near the Y_e-core edge*, within one specific class of models. Tighten the language to match that, add the IMF-cutoff sensitivity, and this becomes a persuasive probe paper rather than a fragile one.

**What I'd ask you to change before resubmission:** (1) soften the "no fallback" claim to "inconsistent with the fallback accretion in the Zhang 2008 models"; (2) quote the S/N_A posterior width, not just the maximum; (3) add an upper-IMF-cutoff sensitivity study and move the supergiant problem out of a footnote; (4) state the common-envelope / mass-return assumption behind the mass-conservation argument.
