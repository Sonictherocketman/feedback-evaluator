## [1507.08765v4-general-review] General Review


I've read the full paper (arXiv:1507.08765v4, Huang, "Study on accelerator neutrino detection at a spallation source"). Below is a critique of its argument.

## What the paper actually argues

The paper makes a single, linear feasibility claim: because the 1.6 GeV CSNS proton beam on tungsten produces a sizable, wide-band accelerator-neutrino beam (one neutrino of each of νₑ, ν_μ, ν̄_μ per ~0.17 proton, via the π→μ cascade), a MiniBooNE-like detector at L = 60 m could observe a large number of accelerator neutrinos through two channels — ν–e and ν–C reactions. It simulates production with FLUKA, applies standard 3-flavor oscillation (finding it negligible at 60 m), and multiplies flux × cross section × target number to get yearly event counts (Table 4). On that narrow level the argument is coherent and the physics is standard and applied sensibly. The flavor composition in Table 3 is the textbook π⁺→μ⁺ν_μ, μ⁺→e⁺νₑν̄_μ cascade (one of each, ν̄ₑ suppressed), which is correct.

The weakness is not that the calculation is wrong but that it stops at "we could see them," without a physics driver, a background estimate, or an uncertainty budget. Here are the specific points.

## 1. Missing physics motivation / figure of merit (the main gap)

The paper never states a scientific question the measurement would answer or a reach. CSNS is a neutron source, so the opening should justify why its by-product accelerator-neutrino beam is worth a detector: is the goal a cross-section measurement (νₑ–e CC, ν–C charged/neutral current), a probe of the νₑ cross-section with a new flux, a background characterization for other CSNS programs (oscillation / reactor / muon-beam neutrino experiments), or testing non-standard interactions? The baseline (60 m) and mean energy (~33–50 MeV) rule out oscillation physics, and the paper itself concedes this (Sec. 3) — so the oscillation section is vestigial. Framing the result with even one figure of merit (e.g., "with X events/yr and Y% systematic, we can measure σ(νₑe) to Z%") would turn a rate calculation into a physics proposal. As written, the contribution is "CSNS has a large accelerator-neutrino flux," which is stated before it is really needed.

## 2. No background estimate

For a detection study, the absence of any background discussion is the most consequential omission at this baseline. Dominant backgrounds to the cited channels are not mentioned: beam- and atmospheric-muon decays in/detector, cosmogenic and intrinsic ¹⁴C activity, radioactivity, and prompt activity from the target/beamline. The paper does correctly note the standard discrimination tools (recoil-electron forward peaking for ν–e, the 15.11 MeV mono-energetic γ for ν–C NC, and the ¹²N β⁺ delayed coincidence for ν–C CC), but without an estimated background rate the "large number of accelerator neutrinos" (Table 4) is not yet a detectable signal. Even scaling an OscNS/MiniBooNE background would make the claim testable.

## 3. Numerical inconsistency in the oscillation probabilities

The per-event conclusions are robust, but the quoted oscillation numbers do not reproduce from the paper's own inputs. Using Eq. 4 with Δm²₃₂ = 2.4×10⁻³ eV², L = 60 m, E ≈ 50 MeV, the phase is Δm²₃₂·L/4E ≈ 3.7×10⁻³ (deep in the small-argument limit; the oscillation length is ~50 km ≫ 60 m, so the rates are effectively averaged). Then P(νₑ→ν_μ) ≈ sin²2θ₂₃·(3.7×10⁻³)² ≈ 6×10⁻⁶, not the 1.27×10⁻⁶ printed. The individual P values (1.27×10⁻⁶, 1.57×10⁻⁵) look like a slipped unit or an unsquared argument across lines 1–3 of Eq. 4. The *conclusion* (oscillations negligible at 60 m) is completely safe, so this only matters for internal consistency — but reviewers readers will re-derive these and notice. Worth a footnote: because L ≪ L_osc, the cleaner statement is "oscillation corrections are O(10⁻⁵) and ignored," rather than displaying rapidly-wavy sin² that the baseline does not resolve.

## 4. Efficiencies and duty cycle treated as placeholders but central to the number

Table 4's headline numbers explicitly assume 50% detector efficiency and 50% beam-on efficiency, which the text admits "were not seriously considered." That is the right honesty, but it should be stated up front as the dominant, model-uncontrolled parameter, with at least a scaling: N̄ ∝ (L_det)(L_b-on)·Φ. Similarly, the proton numbers in Table 3 (1.23 / 6.15 ×10²² yr⁻¹) imply a specific beam-on time that is never specified (a continuous 25 Hz × 8 h/d gives ≈ 4×10²² for CSNS-I, not 1.23×10²²). The duty cycle should be given so the yield "0.17/proton" can be cross-checked against other FLUKA/GEANT sub-GeV accelerator-neutrino estimates.

## 5. Reproducibility

There are no FLUKA cards/input files, no energy binning, no definition of the "fiducial" flux integration range vs. the 15–30 MeV spectra in Fig. 3, and no explicit cross-section reference per channel. For a feasibility note this is acceptable, but the numbers as published are not independently reproducible. Publishing the FLUKA input, the source-to-detector geometry, and a table of σ(E) used per reaction would fix this.

## 6. Presentation / consistency nits

- Fig. 2(a) caption says "π⁺ and μ⁺" but the axis label reads "π⁻, μ⁻" — pick one.
- Fig. 4 caption "α = μ,τ, x = e,μ,τ" is garbled; it should name the specific flavor pairs plotted (α, x ∈ {e, μ, ν}).
- Several subscripts render oddly (e.g., the P(ν_ℓ→ν_ℓ) line in Sec. 2), and the θ symbols in Eq. 4 appear as a partial-like glyph; worth a proofread of equation subscripts.
- Table 4 lists N̄ for a mixed "νₑ(C C)+νₑ(N C)" channel as 263; it would help to state whether that sum is CC+NC for νₑ–e, which is otherwise ambiguous against the channel definitions in Sec. 3.
- N_T^(p) = 6.90×10³¹ equals the hydrogen nucleon count in 803 t of CH₂, not the total protons (~2.76×10³² including ⁶C nucleons). Since ν–p elastic is (correctly) dropped, it has no effect here, but the label "target number" of protons is misleading.

## Overall assessment

The core chain — simulate the beam with an established code, take the standard π/μ cascade flavor mix, drop oscillations at a short baseline, and apply measured sub-GeV ν–e and ν–C cross sections to get rates — is sound and internally consistent at the level that matters. The argument would be substantially stronger with (a) an explicit physics goal and a reach estimate, (b) a background budget, and (c) an uncertainty/systematics treatment (the authors themselves flag cross-section and flux systematics in Sec. 4). Without these it reads as a rate table rather than a case for an experiment. It is a competent, incremental application of a known analysis to a specific facility rather than a new method or result, so its standalone impact is modest, though it is a useful background/flux reference for anyone planning CSNS neutrino-related work.

This is a 6-page single-author article (arXiv:1507.08765v4, Huang Ming-Yang, IHEP CAS) in the European Physical Journal C layout, titled "Study on accelerator neutrino detection at a spallation source." ## Prose and grammar

The English sits at a functional but only lightly edited level. It is comprehensible throughout, and the paper reads as if it has passed through at least one round of revision (a v4 re-submission), but enough first-draft phrasing survives to keep it from feeling polished.

- **Awkward collocations.** The abstract carries "numerical flux," "the yield efficiency, numerical flux, and average energy of different flavors of neutrinos," and "detections of these accelerator neutrinos are investigated" — all three read as translationese rather than idiomatic physics prose. §1 also has "will provide a copious flux of neutrinos," where "copious" is out of register.
- **Inconsistent citation practice.** The text cites sometimes as "following [38]" and other times as "given in Ref. [33]" — the journal's own style requires consistency.
- **Dense run-on listing of results.** The three-item list of simulation results in §2 ("(i) Numerical fluxes of various particles involved decrease with the distance L; (ii) … (iii) … (iv) …") is the only place in the paper where such an enumeration is used; the corresponding paragraphs in §3 just run the same content back into prose, so the reader has to read it twice.

## Argument and internal consistency

The paper's structure is sensible (production simulation → flux → detection channels → summary), but a number of internal tensions undercut the argument:

1. **A direct typo in flavor notation.** §2 (right column, page 3) concludes: "the oscillations among νe, νμ, and **νe** can be neglected." The second "νe" should clearly be ντ; with the paper repeating the electron flavor, the sentence is literally nonsense.
2. **A numerical inconsistency in the body.** §2 states "the same yield event number per year which is 0.21×10²² for CSNS-I and **1.05×10¹²** for CSNS-II," but Table 3 shows 1.05 (×10²²) for CSNS-II, and Table 4's total event count (824+426 = 1 250; 2580+1005 = 3 585) corresponds to 10²²–scale neutrino flux with 50 % efficiency, *not* to 10¹². The body's 10¹² is a decade error that would make the paper's own detection estimate implausible.

Table 4, however, tabulates 263 νe CC events per year and 54 ν(x) CC events — a sizable fraction of the 824 νe NC and 2580 ν(x) NC events in the same table, and by no means negligible. The summary repeats the "neglect" line. 4. **Notational ambiguity.** §3 gives N_T^(p) = 6.90×10³¹, N_T^(c) = 2.76×10³², and N_T^(C) = 3.45×10³¹; the distinction between lowercase c and uppercase C is never made explicit in words, and in §4's prose the two notations are used interchangeably in reference to "12C targets."

That second sentence is close to a tautology and adds no real discussion.

Fig. 4's caption is the weakest: "**α = μ, τ, x = e, μ, τ.**" is dropped in with no explanation of what α and x parameterize (evidently the flavor of the neutrino in the cross-section formula and the type of final-state lepton, respectively). A new reader has to reverse-engineer the axes from the symbols. The other captions are functional.

## Overall editorial impression

The paper is a short, single-source application note — FLUKA plus a scaled-down MiniBooNE-style detector — aimed at the CSNS community. As English it is comprehensible but not yet publication-ready: the "νe, νμ, and νe" typo, the 10¹² vs 10²² discrepancy, and the "neglect νe" over-claim are all issues an editor should flag before the paper goes to a referee, rather than asking referees to find them. ## [1507.08765v4-critique] Critique


# Critique of "Study on accelerator neutrino detection at a spallation source" (arXiv:1507.08765v4)

## Summary

The paper simulates with FLUKA the neutrinos produced when the 1.6 GeV CSNS proton beam stops in a tungsten target, obtains per-fluid yield, flux, and spectral estimates, then scales event rates at a 60 m baseline for a hypothetical ~800 ton mineral-oil detector ("similar to MiniBooNE") through neutrino–electron scattering and neutrino/antineutrino–12C channels. It concludes that the flux is large enough that accelerator neutrinos at CSNS could be used for cross-section and flavor work.

## Major weaknesses

**1. No science case, no uncertainties, no measurement goal.** The paper is a pure pre-study: it computes event counts and stops. There is no figure of merit, no sensitivity estimate, no error budget (FLUKA's hadronic-production systematics are notoriously large and are not touched at all), no background estimate (cosmics, accidentals against the 12N delayed coincidence, radioactivity), and no statement of what observable could actually be extracted. The abstract's promise that "neutrino mixing parameters and the mass scheme may be studied" is never supported by any calculation; the 60 m baseline gives appearance probabilities of ~10⁻⁵ (as the paper itself computes), so mixing physics is categorically out of reach. The discussion section concedes the efficiencies were "not seriously considered" and that errors "also need to be considered" — i.e., the paper acknowledges its own numbers are not yet experimentally meaningful, which leaves the results with no quantitative standing.

**2. Internal inconsistency about ν̄ₑ.** Three statements conflict:
- p. 2: the ν̄ₑ yield is "a factor of 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁴ lower, and it will be neglected";
- Table 3: ν̄ₑ gets the *same* yield as every other flavor (0.21 ×10²² yr⁻¹ at CSNS-I, identical to νₑ, ν_μ, ν̄_μ — i.e., 0.17 per proton for all four);
- §3 and Table 4: the ν̄ₑ charged-current ("IBD") channel on ¹²C is then included in the reaction analysis anyway, and the claim that all flavors have "the same yield efficiency of about 0.17 per proton" contradicts the earlier neglect. A study whose stated purpose is flavor discrimination cannot both claim the antineutrino-electron component is 10⁻³–10⁻⁴ subdominant and give it the same yield as the dominant flavor. (Also, the 10⁻³–10⁻⁴ suppression factor for decay-in-flight μ⁻ in a high-intensity stopping target is not justified by any decay-in-flight calculation and is more aggressive than the few-percent ν̄ₑ contamination familiar from LAMPF/OscSNS-class sources.)

**3. Inconsistent machine parameters between Table 1 and Table 2.** Table 1 lists CSNS-I at 0.063 mA average current and "1.56(7.8) ×10¹³ ppp"; Table 2 lists 62.5 μA and "1.56 ×10¹⁵" protons per pulse — a factor of ~100 apart. The self-consistent values are 62.5 μA ≙ 3.9 ×10¹⁴ p/s ≙ 1.56 ×10¹³ ppp at 25 Hz, so Table 2's "10¹⁵" unit is a factor-of-100 typo, but it goes unflagged next to the other parameters that all depend on it (Table 3's proton-per-year figures are consistent with 0.1 MW, not with 1.56 ×10¹⁵ ppp). A two-table inconsistency of this size in a parameter-driven simulation paper is a red flag about how carefully the input chain was checked.

**4. The oscillation computation is a non-result presented as content.** §2–3 devotes a formula block (Eq. 4) and three numerical probabilities to the conclusion that oscillations are negligible at L = 60 m — which is immediate from Δm²₃₂L/4E ~ 10⁻³ and needs no three-flavor machinery. My spot check of the arithmetic (e.g., P(ν̄_μ→ν̄ₑ) at 47.9 MeV ≈ 10⁻⁵) comes out at the right order, so nothing is wrong — it just adds nothing. By the same logic the "identical yield for all flavors" claim in Table 3 deserves a real FLUKA flavor-composition table rather than a single number.

**5. Detector choice and detector physics are loose.** "A detector similar to MiniBooNE" is then described as a 6.1 m-radius sphere of 803 t of mineral oil for ¹²C IBD — MiniBooNE is a water-Cherenkov CSD, and the relevant design comparisons are LSND/LS2.0/JSNS²-class liquid scintillators. The neutrino–¹²C description is compressed: the 15.11 MeV state does not de-excite as a single monoenergetic γ (it cascades through 4.44 MeV), and the ν_μ neutral-current rate at 30–50 MeV (well below the Δ resonance and with no quasi-elastic channel on carbon) is dominated by models the paper does not cite or discuss. The paper gives no cross-section models beyond a single reference list, so the Table 4 numbers cannot be independently reproduced — my back-of-envelope estimate from the stated flux, target numbers, Fig. 4 cross sections, and 50% efficiencies lands within about an order of magnitude of Table 4, but the level of detail is insufficient to confirm it.

## Minor issues

- Notation: "E_th", the N̆/N̂ hats are never defined consistently, "protons on target" is never separated from "protons on beamline," and no beam occupancy/duty-time (e.g., 80% availability) is applied to the per-year numbers.
- No energy spectra of *observed* events, no recoil-electron or γ line simulations, no comparison with the measured ν–e cross sections (e.g., against the MiniBooNE ν_μ–e result the paper itself cites as reference [29] in the beam-source context).
- Presentation: Table 2 is almost entirely redundant with Table 1 (identical linac energy and target for CSNS-I/II), §3's four conclusions (i)–(iv) are restatements of Table 4's relative ordering, and the "Summary" largely paraphrases the abstract. The paper also never says *when* or *where* such a detector would sit relative to the CSNS beamline or what its cost/size implies for the facility.
- References mix design reports (J-PARC TDR, ESS TDR) with journals without explaining which parameter each supplies; ref [31] cites a FLUKA version report rather than the release used.

## What would make this a real paper

A defined science case (e.g., measuring the ν̄ₑ–¹²C IBD cross section, which is genuinely unmeasured and would be the interesting claim here), a flavor-composition table from FLUKA with hadronic-model systematics, an explicit detector concept (PSD, γ/e discrimination, backgrounds), and a sensitivity/uncertainty budget. Without those, this remains what it effectively is: a first-order order-of-magnitude note.

## Verdict

The methodology (FLUKA production → 1/L² flux → rate = flux × σ × N_T) is standard and arithmetically mostly self-consistent where checkable, and the flux magnitudes are plausible. But the contribution is a feasibility estimate with no new physics, no method, no data, no uncertainties, and two unexplained internal contradictions (Table 1 vs Table 2; the ν̄ₑ yield/neglect conflict). As a preliminary internal note it is adequate; as a publication it makes overblown claims relative to what it actually establishes.

## [1507.08765v4-novelty] Novelty of the contribution


# Evaluation of arXiv:1507.08765v4 — "Study on accelerator neutrino detection at a spallation source" (Huang Ming-Yang)

## The stated contribution, in my own words

The author uses **FLUKA** to simulate the neutrino beam produced when CSNS's stopped 1.6 GeV proton beam hits its tungsten target. From that simulation they extract, for the three active flavors plus the electron antineutrino, the **yield efficiency** (~0.17 per proton, identical for νe, νμ, ν̄μ), the **numerical flux versus detector distance**, and the **average energy** of each species (~33.0 MeV νe, 50.0 MeV νμ, 47.9 MeV ν̄μ). They then plug these beams into a **MiniBooNE-like** detector (803 t of mineral-oil fiducial volume, 60 m baseline) and compute the **event rate per year** for two reaction channels: **neutrino–electron scattering** (νe CC+NC vs. νμ, ν̄μ NC) and **neutrino–carbon reactions** (charged-current capture νe→e⁻ plus three NC inelastic scattering channels). They also work out that at a 60 m baseline the oscillation probabilities are ≤1.6×10⁻⁵, so oscillations can be neglected. The deliverable is a set of tables/figures: predicted event numbers (Table 4) meant to motivate measuring neutrino cross sections at CSNS.

It is, in the end, a **source+detector feasibility/characterization exercise** for one particular Chinese facility.

## Is it novel given the prior work the authors cite?

**What is *not* new (essentially all of the framework):**

- **Stopped-proton accelerator (SPN) neutrino beam concept** — introduced by Schwartz and Pontecorvo, realized at LAMPF/ISIS (LSND, KARMEN, T2K) and J-PARC. This is the entire backbone and is cited (refs [1]–[5], [19], [25], [30]).
- **Using π⁺ and μ⁻ decays to make the beam**, the neglect of π⁻/μ⁻ capture suppressing ν̄e, and the dominant π⁺→μ⁺→e⁺ cascade (Eqs. 1–2) — standard, cited.
- **FLUKA as the beam-simulation tool** (ref [31]) — the workhorse code; applying it to another facility's target is not itself novel.
- **The detector channel model** — neutrino–electron scattering (refs [40], [43]) and neutrino–carbon reactions (refs [42]–[46]) with their cross sections (Fig. 4), the MiniBooNE-style detector design (ref [24]), the event-rate formula (Eq. 5, ref [38]), and even the argument that ν–e forward peak distinguishes signal from elastic proton scatter (refs [39], [40]) — all standard, all cited.
- **Three-flavor oscillation formulas** (Eq. 4) and the parameters Δm²₃₂, θ values (refs [36], [37]) — textbook.
- **The general conclusion that cross sections can be measured with a SPN beam and a scintillator** is precisely the thrust of the OscNS white paper (ref [38]) and the SNs cross-section papers (Refs. [44], [46]).

So the **methodology, physics, and even the broad conclusion** are all restatements of established work. Nothing in the formalism or in the physics assumptions is original.

**What *is* (marginally) new:**

- The **specific, quantitative numbers for CSNS-II** — a 1 GeV / 25 Hz tungsten-target spallation source — namely the per-proton yield (0.17), the flavor fluxes (0.21×10²² for CSNS-I, 1.05×10²² for CSNS-II), the flavor average energies, and the predicted annual event counts at a 60 m MiniBooNE-class detector (Table 4: e.g. νμ/¹²C → 824+426 CC+NC vs. 2580 for νe and 1005 for ν̄μ NC). This has not previously been tabulated for CSNS; it is a **facility-specific application and benchmark**, the only genuinely additive content.
- The explicit finding that **at 60 m the short baseline suppresses oscillations** for this configuration — a useful practical observation, though it follows trivially from L/L_osc ≪ 1.

## Verdict on novelty

This is **incremental applied work**: a straightforward, competent re-use of a fully pre-existing toolkit (FLUKA beam sim + MiniBooNE detector + standard ν–e/ν–C cross sections + textbook oscillations) pointed at one more source, CSNS. ## The paper

## [1507.08765v4-logical-fallacies] Logical fallacies


## My understanding of the paper's logical argument

**Setup.** The paper is a feasibility study of the accelerator neutrino beam produced when CsNS's (~0.8–1.6 GeV) proton beam hits its tungsten spallation target, and whether a MiniBooNE-like liquid-scintillator detector (803 t CH₂, 950 m³) placed at baselines of tens of meters could observe them.

**Argument chain:**

1. **Production (Sec. 2).** The beam is dominated by pion decay at rest: π⁺ → μ⁺ + ν_μ, followed by μ⁺ → e⁺ + ν_e + ν̄_μ. FLUKA simulation gives a yield efficiency of ~0.17 neutrinos/proton in each of the three flavors (ν_e, ν_μ, ν̄_μ), i.e. ~0.21×10²² events/year for CSNS-I and ~1.05×10²² for CSNS-II (0.21 MW → 0.5 MW upgrades). ν̄_e is negligible (~10⁻³–10⁻⁴ of the others) because most π⁻/μ⁻ are captured.
2. **Flux (Sec. 2–3).** The numerical flux φ = N_year/(4πL²) scales as 1/L², with a point-source/isotropic assumption. Average energies are ~33 MeV for ν_e and ~50 MeV for ν_μ/ν̄_μ. Oscillations are explicitly shown to be negligible at L = 60 m (P(ν_e→ν_μ) ~ 10⁻⁶), so flavor content at the detector equals flavor content at the source.
3. **Detection (Sec. 3).** With a MiniBooNE-like detector, only two channels are viable: ν–electron scattering (CC/NC, recoil electron peak) and ν–carbon (CC on ¹²C with delayed ¹²N β-decay, plus NC inelastic ¹²C*; ν–proton and ν–nucleon elastic scattering are dismissed as too hard to observe). N̄_i = φ·σ·N_T gives per-year event numbers; at L = 60 m with 50% detector and beam-on efficiencies, the carbon channel dominates (~2580 + 1005 NC + 824+426 CC events/year).
4. **Conclusion (Sec. 4).** Large event numbers flow from both channels, so the CsNS accelerator neutrino beam is ample for cross-section measurements; a real detector design should then add efficiencies and errors.

## Feedback

**What holds together.** The chain "production → flux → event rate" is logically coherent, and the one non-trivial quantitative argument — that oscillations can be dropped on this baseline — is done correctly (it follows directly from the Δm²₂₃, L, and ⟨E⟩ values cited, and the ~10⁻⁶ probabilities are the right order of magnitude). The decision to restrict to two reaction channels, with explicit reasons for dropping CC on electrons and proton elastic scattering, is defensible.

**Weaknesses in the logic:**

1. **The "equal yield efficiency" claim is physically suspect.** The decay chain at rest predicts ν_μ : ν_e : ν̄_μ ≈ 2 : 1 : 1 (each decaying π⁺ gives one ν_μ in the pion decay *and* one more ν_e + one ν̄_μ in the muon decay). The paper instead reports identical yields (0.21×10²²) for all three species in Table 3 and calls this a "same yield efficiency." This contradicts the standard picture of decay-at-source beams (LAMPF, KARMEN, MiniBooNE all show the 2:1:1 structure) and is not explained or justified against the FLUKA output. It also makes claim (iii) in Fig. 2 — "the numerical flux of ν_e is larger than that of ν_μ and ν̄_μ" — puzzling: if the per-proton yields are identical by construction, the fluxes should be equal modulo trivial geometric/attenuation effects. As written, (iii) contradicts the premise that produced all three with the same efficiency.
2. **Internal inconsistencies between tables.** Table 1 lists CSNS as 0.10 MW, 1.6 mA, 16×10¹³ ppp with a **mercury** target, while Table 2 (and the whole paper) uses 0.08 MW (CSNS-I), 62.5 μA, 1.56×10¹³ ppp and a **tungsten** target. Table 1 also lists 1.6 GeV for both CSNS and CSNS-II with different extraction energies (1.0/1.6), while Section II says "the 1.6 GeV proton beam" drives all production numbers. The paper never reconciles these; Table 1 appears to be copied from an older CsNS design document while all calculations use the newer parameters. A reader cannot tell which parameter set the 0.21×10²² figure actually assumes from the text alone (a quick cross-check: 25 Hz × 1.56×10¹³ ppp ≈ 0.1 MW at 1.6 GeV, consistent with Table 2, not the 0.10 MW row — so this should be made explicit).
3. **The scientific motivation never materializes.** The introduction promises that "neutrino properties, such as neutrino mixing parameters and the mass hierarchy, may be studied," but the paper itself proves oscillations are invisible at 60 m, and a tens-of-MeV beam is nowhere near sensitive to the mass hierarchy. The realistic applications — cross-section calibration against solar/reactor ν_e cross sections, detector calibration of the kind done at LAMPF/MiniBooNE/LBNE-era programs — are never stated, and no reach, sensitivity, or measurement definition is given. The conclusion that "a large number of accelerator neutrinos can be used for measuring neutrino cross sections" rests only on raw rates.
4. **No background budget.** For a real 60 m detector on carbon, the dominant backgrounds (reactor ν_e CC on ¹²C, solar neutrinos, and for the CC channel, accidental ¹⁵N/¹⁵O coincidences from natural nitrogen in the mineral oil) would set the *usable* event count. Omitting them entirely means the quoted factor-of-10⁴ rates overstate what is actually measurable; the claim of "large numbers" is unqualified by the only thing that determines significance.
5. **Efficiency handling is loose.** Eq. (5) and Fig. 5 give raw event numbers; only at the very end are 50% detector and 50% beam-on efficiencies post-hoc applied (dividing Table 4 by 4), and ν̄ channels are dropped without quantifying the rate lost. The headline numbers are thus the optimistic upper limits, not the design values.
6. **Point-source isotropic 1/L² flux** for an extended 5 cm × 15 cm × 40 cm target is a fair approximation at 60 m but is never checked; likewise, the FLUKA flux normalization (num/year) and its systematic uncertainty relative to the FLUKA π/μ transport models in the GeV regime is not discussed, even though that is precisely the model validated by MiniBooNE.

**Overall.** The logic is a competent, internally connected "does the rate exist" calculation, with the one quantitative argument (oscillation neglect) done properly. But the equal-yield claim conflicts with the basic decay-at-rest kinematics, the two tables disagree on the machine parameters, the promised physics is never actually connected to the computed rates, and no background analysis is attempted. This makes it a useful first-pass feasibility note rather than a self-sustaining argument that anything can be *measured* with this beam.

**The single weakest link: the step from the simulated neutrino fluxes (Eq. 3 / Fig. 2 / Table 3) to the quoted detection rates (Eq. 5 → Fig. 4–5, Table 4), on which the paper's central conclusion — "a large number of accelerator neutrinos … observed … 824+2580+1005 events per year" — rests.**
