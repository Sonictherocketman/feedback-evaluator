## [1410.5653v6-general-review] General Review


I have read the full paper (Bostrom, *Quantum mechanics as a deterministic theory of a continuum of worlds*, arXiv:1410.5653v6, ~45 pages + references). Below is a general review of the paper's argument with specific critique.

## What the paper is

The paper proposes a non-relativistic interpretation of quantum mechanics in which the physical universe ("multiverse") is a **continuum of trajectories** through 3N-dimensional configuration space — a "world continuum" — where each Bohm trajectory corresponds to one world and all worlds exist equally. Three postulates define the theory: (1) the wavefunction evolves by the Schrödinger equation, (2) each world's history is a trajectory obe-ying the Bohm/guiding equation, (3) trajectory density in configuration space is ρ = |Ψ|². The Born rule is then derived epistemically: an observer who doesn't know which world they inhabit assigns probabilities by a measure-theoretic generalization of Laplace's rule over the continuum of worlds. The paper supports this with a "logical framework" treating *time* and *world* as two "modes of existence" (anchored propositions p(t, w)), a "continuous substance" measure concept, a measurement model giving the Born rule and a subjective collapse, and a lengthy comparison with Bohmian mechanics, Everett, Tipler, Hall et al. (MIW), Sebens, and Poirier–Schiff.

## Strengths

1. **Clarity and honesty of presentation.** The paper is systematically organized, its figures (the A/B/C ontology comparison, the world continuum bundle) are effective, and the author is candid about what the theory is — including the striking admission that it is "essentially the same theory" as Poirier and Schiff's, differing mainly in emphasis and in keeping the wavefunction as a generating function. That intellectual honesty is a strength few interpretive papers match.

2. **A genuinely good answer to the "why a continuum" objection.** Section 6.7's reply to Vaidman and Sebens — that a continuum of worlds is not a counting problem but a measure problem, illustrated with the explicit measure-pushforward toy model (f(x) = x², eqs. 79–81) — is well done. It defuses the "an infinite number of worlds in any finite region, so the density is meaningless" objection correctly, by distinguishing cardinality from measure, and by showing that finite-worlds theories (Sebens) require an extra, unjustified "quantization condition" postulate (his eq. 76) while the continuum variant needs none.

3. **Resolution of the empty-branch ("zombie world") problem.** This is arguably the theory's strongest selling point. In Bohmian mechanics, because the wavefunction is a real field, all branches of the post-measurement state really exist, even the empty ones — Deutsch's "chronic denial of parallel worlds." Here, the physically existing substance *is* the trajectory bundle, and since ρ = |Ψ|² vanishes on empty branches, empty branches are simply regions of configuration space with no worlds at all. Worlds don't split at measurement; the configuration space is merely partitioned into regions. This gives a cleaner ontology than Bohm (no real field plus separate particles with a one-way interaction) and a better-defined ontology than Everett (worlds as precisely defined trajectories, not vaguely branching branches).

4. **The tensor-space answer to Monton.** Recasting configuration space as the rank-two tensor R^{3×N} (eq. 17) so that spatial dimensions and particle indices are no longer huddled in one column vector is a neat, correct fix to Monton (2002)'s "which dimension is which particle" criticism, and the Hilbert product structure carries over without loss (eqs. 18–19).

5. **The logical framework is original.** Treating *world* as a "mode of existence" exactly analogous to *time* — with time-instances, world-instances, and time-world-instances as successive projections of extended objects (the "electron cloud" / trajectory / point trichotomy) — is a carefully worked-out formalism that does real work in the mind-and-world discussion (section 6.2), where the "many Joes, one world-instance each" construction dissolves the "but why do we experience only one world?" objection more cleanly than it is usually handled.

6. **Engagement with the liter-ature is substantive.** Sections 6.3–6.6 don't just list competitors; they find specific flaws in each (Tipler's ambiguous "universes vs. states," MIW's hidden non-uniform probability on configurations, Sebens's ad hoc quantization postulate, Poirier–Schiff's ambiguous "weighting") and draw a coherent map of the conceptual space. The "interaction vs. interference" distinction proposed for Hall et al.'s between-worlds influence is a genuinely useful terminological suggestion.

## Critique

### 1. The Born-rule argument is thinner than the paper presents — the central problem

The paper's headline claim (abstract, introduction) is that probability "does not rely on a quantum equilibrium hypothesis as in Bohmian mechanics" but is derived from the substantial density of the world continuum. Read carefully, however, the argument's logical structure is:

- **Postulate 3** fixes the measure on the (ontically existing) set of worlds: ρ_t = |Ψ_t|²;
- the **continuity equation** (eqs. 25–27) preserves the total amount and the relative proportions of worlds;
- the **Laplace generalization** (section 3.3) says: if region X contains c times more worlds than Y, the probability our world is in X is c times larger, so P_t(Q) = μ_t(Q)/μ_t(Q) (eq. 15);
- the **measurement analysis** (section 5.3) shows the pointer regions yield P(a) = ‖Ĥ_a ψ‖²/‖ψ‖² (eq. 65).

Two things deserve honest scrutiny, which the paper under-delivers on.

First, Postulate 3 *is* the Bohmian quantum equilibrium hypothesis, re-located from epistemic to ontic status. In Bohm, the assumption is "the actual configuration is |Ψ|²-typical, and we don't know which." Here it is "the real bundle of worlds has |Ψ|²-density, and we don't know which world." The assumption is the same distribution; only its philosophical home has moved. The paper itself concedes this in section 3.3 ("such independent justification cannot be provided by mathematics alone but has to be a *physical* justification obtained within the framework of a physical theory") — that physical justification being Postulate 3. So the abstract's "does not rely on a quantum equilibrium hypothesis" is too strong; the defensible version is "re-locates it" or "makes it ontic rather than epistemic," and the paper should say exactly that and argue for the move. There *is* a real potential advantage in the move (the ontic density is a property of reality, not of an ignorance distribution; no typicality-justification debates about what "typically" means for a non-existent actual configuration), but the paper never argues it that way.

Second, and more importantly, the **Laplace step is where all the philosophical weight sits, and it is under-argued.** The paper itself notes (section 3.3) that "Laplace did not give any *reason* why the probability should be equal to the ratio," and that the ratio rule can be derived only circularly from probability axioms. Generalizing the ratio to uncountably many possibilities via a measure is mathematically unproblematic; but the non-trivial move is the *self-locating indifference principle*: given an objective measure over an ontology of possibilities, one's credence that the actual possibility falls in X is proportional to μ(X). This is precisely the logical form of the indifference arguments used to derive the Born rule in Everett (Saunders/Wallace — which the paper cites approvingly as the literature on epistemic probability in Everett, without making the crucial observation). The paper should head-on compare its probability argument against the Everettian ones: it has the *same form*, with "world" (Bohm trajectory) standing in for "Everettian branch." What then makes indifference over *this* ontology more plausible than indifference over branches? The paper's best answers are implicit: the worlds are determinate, non-splitting, precisely defined trajectories (no branch-identification problem, no decoherence-approximation issues in the ontology itself), and "our world" as an unknown is analogous to an unknown hidden variable rather than an unknown self-location in a superposition. It should develop and state these arguments explicitly, because *the plausibility of the indifference step* is the entire difference between the "Born rule derived" claim and the "Born rule postulated in fancy clothes" accusation. As written, the word "must" in "the subjective probability to find oneself within a particular world *must* be given by (15)" is doing far more work than the Laplace generalization licenses.

### 2. Cross-world identity of macroscopic objects is hand-waved

Section 2.2 states that macroscopic-objects-across-worlds ("is Joe missing a hair in world w still Joe?") is "a highly nontrivial question" which is "luckily" not problematic because "they do not arise for elementary particles." But the theory *requires* cross-world identity of macroscopic objects to work: the measurement derivation (section 5.3) needs the observer Joe and the pointer states η_a to be the *same* objects in all worlds, with the pointer region Z_a defined across worlds; the mind-and-world section 6.2 needs world-instances of the same person; the "why a continuum" section needs observers embedded in specific worlds. The theory simply presumes that macroscopic identity across the continuum is settled ("it should be no less problematic to identify macroscopic objects across worlds," with the implication that it works out). This is a real gap: the author should at least give an explicit account (e.g., a coarse-graining/continuity criterion for cross-world identity of macroscopic systems — the same task Everettians perform when identifying decohered branches) and show that the pointer-state construction in section 5.3 is robust under it. One paragraph can be "these issues are interesting but I won't address them," but not a claim that they "do not arise" for a theory whose core derivation uses them.

### 3. Non-relativistic scope, stated but significant

Footnote 1 correctly notes that the framework treats time and space asymmetrically and that a relativistic version would need to replace the temporal anchor with a spacelike hypersurface. This is a hard problem (Bohmian mechanics faces it too, via preferred foliation or covariant reformulations), and the paper doesn't even sketch how modes of existence would extend to it. For an interpretive paper that is a limitation readers should weigh. Since the whole probability argument is parameterized by a global "present time" t, the theory inherits the preferred-time structure of non-relativistic QM wholesale; a note on how (or whether) the world-anchor/relativity issue is worse here than in Bohm would be valuable, because in this theory *all* worlds at all times exist equally, so the choice of simultaneity slices is a choice about a real substance, which may feel more ontologically committed than in Bohm where only the actual configuration exists.

### 4. Predictive content is zero — the paper should say this more plainly

The theory reproduces standard QM exactly (this is a theorem of Bohmian mechanics with the |Ψ|² density, as the formalism makes clear). Its contribution is entirely interpretive/ontological/epistemic. The paper gestures at this ("the problem is not that quantum mechanics does not yield the correct experimental predictions, but rather that there is still no consensus about the *metaphysical content*") but would benefit from being explicit early on that its evaluation criteria are internal: clarity, consistency, ontological frugality, and explanatory virtue for probability — not empirical discrimination. Readers then can't be surprised when there's no "this predicts X" section, and the paper's claims are judged on the right axis.

### 5. The relationship to Poirier–Schiff could be sharpened

The claim that Bostrom's and Poirier–Schiff's are "two formulations of the same theory" is defensible (the trajectory bundles, the densities, the flow are the same objects), but the *postulate structures* differ in kind: Poirier–Schiff derive a PDE for the trajectory function ξ_t (with a variational structure, eqs. 77–78); Bostrom takes Ψ + guiding equation as primitive and reads off the continuum. A short argument that the P–S PDE for ξ is *equivalent* to (or derivable from) postulates 1–2 in the spin-free case would make "same theory" precise rather than programmatic; as it stands the equivalence is asserted on the level of "the trajectories are the same in all three theories."

### 6. Minor points

- The claim in the introduction that probability "does not rely on a ... 'branch weight' as in Everettian mechanics" is again an under-argued contrast: the world density ρ plays exactly the role the Everettian branch weight / ψ-branch measure plays, so the right contrast is about *what the measure is over* (determinate trajectories vs. superposed branches), not about having a measure at all. The abstract would be improved by reflecting the section 3.3/6.6 analysis more accurately.
- Section 4.2's claim that the wavefunction "contains slightly less information than the wavefunction" (i.e., the continuum (ρ, ξ) is strictly less redundant than Ψ) should be stated precisely: (ρ, ξ) determines the Hilbert *ray* (R from ρ, S up to a time-dependent constant from ξ), so the continuum is a faithful representation of the ray, and only global scale and global phase of Ψ are lost. Making this a one-line lemma would strengthen the "Occam" argument, since it removes any worry that demoting the wavefunction to a generating function loses physical content.
- The phrase "first-order hyperbolic equation" for both the Schrödinger and Bohm equations (remark 2, and the collapse derivation 67–70) is standard Bohmian-speak, but the collapse argument depends only on branch-separation in the *amplitude*, not on hyperbolicity per se; the informal but correct point is that the guiding velocity at q̅ is determined only by the wavefunction amplitude in the neighborhood of q̅. A sentence to that effect would make the "subjective collapse" less magical.
- A few typographical artifacts in the published draft (stray red brackets around a label in "existence,^red" on page 8, and "world.^red" on page 23) suggest the typescript wasn't fully proofed for this version.
- The "continuous substance" coin (substance / substantial density / substantial amount / world density / world flow / world amount) is heavy. Each term is defined, but a notation table early on (world ↔ trajectory ↔ trajectory function ↔ density ↔ amount ↔ probability) would save the reader from carrying six near-synonyms through 20 pages. (Indeed, the paper's own remark 1 table (37)–(40) is excellent and should perhaps be brought much earlier, right after the postulates.)

## Overall judgment

This is a solid, well-written, unusually self-aware contribution to foundations of quantum mechanics. Its real contributions are: (i) the "modes of existence" logical framework, which is original machinery; (ii) the continuous-substance/measure treatment that answers Vaidman's and Sebens' objections to a continuum of worlds; (iii) the elimination of empty branches by making the trajectory bundle (and only the trajectory bundle) the real substance; and (iv) a careful map of competing "wavefunction-elimination" and many-trajectory approaches (Tipler, MIW, Sebens, Poirier–Schiff). Its main weakness is that the probability argument — the paper's central claim — is presented as a derivation but is really a two-part package (an ontic |Ψ|²-measure postulate that *is* the equilibrium hypothesis re-located, plus a self-locating indifference principle) whose relationship to the Everettian Born-rule arguments is never explicitly drawn, and for which the indifference step is asserted rather than defended. Combined with the hand-waved cross-world identity of macroscopic observers and the purely non-relativistic scope, the paper is a good, consistent interpretive proposal rather than a decisive argument; the "Born rule derived" headline should be softened to "the standard probability apparatus can be re-derived with a different distributional commitment, and here is why that exchange is worth making." It is clearly better than most preprint-level interpretation work — above the level of a strong foundations-journal article in clarity and literature-coverage — but the central probability claim does not quite survive the scrutiny it invites, which caps the assessment.

## [1410.5653v6-expert-collegue-review] Expert Collegue Review


I have read the full paper (Bostrom, *Quantum mechanics as a deterministic theory of a continuum of worlds*, arXiv:1410.5653v6). This is a foundations/interpretation paper that reinterprets non‑relativistic quantum mechanics through a "world continuum": the universe is the complete bundle of Bohm trajectories in configuration space, with **every** trajectory (not one, as in Bohmian mechanics; not a set of split copies, as in Everett) taken as ontologically real, and |Ψ|² read as the *substantial density* of that continuum so that probability becomes a generalized Laplacian measure of ignorance about which world one is in.

## What the paper does well

- **Internal coherence and clarity.** The three postulates (Sec. 5) plus the correspondence table (Remark 1, (37)–(40)) give a genuinely clean axiomatic skeleton, and the prose is unusually legible for a paper in this field.
- **The continuum-density objection is handled properly.** Section 6.7 is, in my view, the strongest and most original part. The standard rebuttal (Vaidman 2014; Sebens 2014) to a *continuum* of worlds is "you cannot talk about the density of worlds"; Bostrom dissolves it cleanly with measure theory (eqs. 78–81) and a concrete bijection toy model showing that a uniform distribution of worlds in one space can induce a non‑uniform density in another — which then lets P(Y) = μ(Y)/μ(Y') be well defined for uncountably many worlds. That is a real and useful contribution to the debate, and it's the piece of the paper that the finite- or countably-many-worlds competitors (Sebens, Tipper, Hall et al.) genuinely lack.
- **Honesty of positioning.** The author is candid that Poirier & Schiff is "essentially the same theory," and the comparative discussions (Tipper, MIW, Sebens) are fair and specific. This is how a careful philosopher-of-physicist writes.
- **The subjective-collapse derivation (Sec. 5.3, (66)–(70))** is presented correctly — it is the standard Bohmian effective-decoherence/effective-collapse argument, and getting it in a many-worlds (no-particle) setting without an "actual configuration" is mildly novel in its framing.

## The serious problems

1. **The headline claim — "no quantum equilibrium hypothesis" — is overstated, and this is the paper's central weakness.** The whole interpretive story requires that the *ontic* density of the actually-existing trajectory continuum equals ρ_t = |Ψ_t|² (Postulate 3, (35)). That is precisely the QEH, relabeled. The continuity equation (25) only *preserves* ρ once it is set; it does not derive why the world continuum should be distributed as |Ψ|². So the author has not eliminated an undelimited stipulation — he has demoted it from a *hypothesis to be justified* (as it is in BM, with all literature on its derivation) to an *axiom of the new theory*. The Born rule then follows from "Laplacian rule + stipulate ρ = |Ψ|²," which the author himself concedes is "merely a generalization." Calling this a derivation of the Born rule, and a contrast with BM's "controversial" QEH, misleads the reader about what has actually been accomplished. At minimum, the paper should state that it inherits the QEH's status as an independent physical postulate and either justify it or acknowledge the equivalence.

2. **Marginal physics.** Dynamically, this *is* Bohmian mechanics. The same Bohm equation, same Hamiltonian, same trajectories. The only changes are (a) interpreting ρ ontologically rather than as a probability on the actual configuration, and (b) declaring the continuum of trajectories all real. That is legitimate interpretive work, but the novelty content is modest and is largely absorbed by the existing Poirier–Schiff result — the paper says so.

3. **The "continuous substance / substantial density" apparatus (Sec. 3) is mostly measure theory in new clothing.** The child–ice-cream aside is a rhetorical flourish; the "substantial amount" μ is just a (possibly non-normalized) Lebesgue integral of the density, and the author is on solid ground but adds no mathematical tool. The careful cardinality-vs-measure distinction (pp. 14, 43) is correct but well known.

4. **The configuration-space redefinition R^{3×N} (eq. 17) addressing Monton's critique is cosmetic.** The author concedes ("any tensor space is also a linear space"); relabeling the index structure does little to rebut a critique that is fundamentally about what 3N space *means*, so it reads as a superficial dodge.

5. **Mind/world (6.2) and inter-world "interference" remain hand-wavy.** The categorical distinction the author wants ("interaction is between systems, interference is between worlds") is asserted, not analyzed, and the mind section is explicitly deferred.

## Minor issues

- The claim "there are exactly c^{2^{ℵ₀}} more trajectories in X than in Y" (p. 17) should be phrased as a *continuous* multiplicity statement; the cardinal arithmetic is a bit loose.
- Non-relativistic and spin-free, with relativistic/field extensions deferred — expected for a first pass but a real limitation for a "foundations" article.
- No empirical or predictive novelty whatsoever (by construction); the entire value is explanatory, which is fine but should be made explicit so the contribution is judged in the right category.

## Bottom line

A coherent, well-written, internally consistent synthesis that (i) cleanly unifies the Bohmian and Everettian pictures by promoting every trajectory to reality and treating |Ψ|² as an ontic density, and (ii) provides the best current answer to the "continuum of worlds has no density" objection. Its main defect is that the central selling point — "we avoid/derive the Born rule without the QEH" — is actually a rebranded stipulation, and the physical content is Bohmian mechanics with a reinterpretation. That keeps it from being a strong standalone contribution, even though the Sec. 6.7 resolution is worth preserving and citing.

# Critique: "Quantum mechanics as a deterministic theory of a continuum of worlds" (Boström, 2015)

## What the paper does

Boström proposes a non‑relativistic interpretation whose ontology is: a **continuum of worlds in configuration space**, with each *world* identified with a single **Bohm trajectory** (a solution of the Bohm equation), the **wavefunction** treated not as a physical field but as a *generating function* of the whole trajectory bundle, and **probability** understood as *epistemic* (the observer's ignorance of which world they inhabit). The central result is that the Born rule follows from a measure $\mu_t(Q)=\int_Q dq\,|\Psi_t|^2$ on the space of worlds, together with a "generalisation of Laplace's rule" to a continuum of possibilities, and that the "collapse" is merely a subjective, per‑world re‑anchoring. Sections 2 (logical framework) and 3 (probability/continuous substance) do the conceptual heavy lifting; Section 4 demotes the wavefunction; Section 5 gives a minimal axiom set; Section 6 benchmarks against Bohmian mechanics, Everett, Tippler, the many‑interacting‑worlds approach, Sebens, and Poirier–Schiff, and answers Vaidman's cardinality objection to "continuum of worlds."

## Strengths

1. **The measure‑theoretic defence of a "continuum of worlds" is the genuine technical contribution.** The objection (Vaidman 2014; Sebens 2014) is that with a continuum of worlds every region has *infinitely many* worlds, so "more worlds" is meaningless and a "density of worlds" has no referent. Boström reframes it via **substantial amount/density**: you don't count points, you integrate a spatially varying density, so one region can host *more* of the substance than another even when both have equal *cardinality* (the $[0,1]\xrightarrow{x\mapsto x^2}[0,1]$ bijection with induced measure $\rho_Y=1/(2\sqrt y)$ in eqs. 80–81 is the right toy). This is a correct, clean resolution of the cardinality objection and is one of the few parts of the paper no earlier reference handles as explicitly.

2. **The logical "modes of existence" framework is a useful formal device.** Treating *time* and *world* as two independent anchors $p(t,w)\equiv"F o@(t,w)$, with *time‑instances*, *world‑instances*, and *time‑world‑instances* as the ways of collapsing those modes (and identifying a trajectory as a *world‑instance*, a configuration as a *time‑world‑instance*) gives Everett's notoriously vague "worlds" a precise object and answers the "worlds are ill‑defined" objection head‑on. It is not deep, but it is a legitimate clarification contribution.

3. **A genuinely careful, honest literature comparison.** The discussion of Poirier–Schiff, MIW, Sebens' Newtonian QM, and Tippler is fair and sophisticated, correctly locating each approach on the "trajectory bundle" phase space. In particular the point that MIW *conceals* a non‑trivial, unjustified probability assumption about configurations, and that Sebens' finite‑worlds theory needs an extra "quantization" postulate (eq. 76) precisely because it dropped the wavefunction, are perceptive.

4. **Removal of the quantum equilibrium hypothesis (QEH).** Because $\rho_t=|\Psi_t|^2$ is *the* density of the world continuum and the trajectories are *all* the Bohm solutions, equivariance is not a dynamical hypothesis to be recovered (à la Valentini's sub‑quantum $H$‑theorem) but a structural feature. That is a real, if modest, improvement over Bohmian mechanics and is correctly drawn against the "chronic denial" of empty branches — by realising *every* trajectory, the theory has no "zombie worlds" and no separate wavefunction acting one‑way on particles.

5. **Empirical correctness is maintained and the derivations are sound.** The measurement analysis (5.3), pointer‑basis separation (48–61), the Born‑rule reduction (62–65), and the "subjective collapse" (66–70) reproduce standard QM in the usual way, and the internal logic is tight. The paper is well written and mathematically correct where it makes claims.

## Weaknesses

1. **The probability "derivation" does not actually derive anything — it postulates the Born density and then quotes it back.** The argument is: pick a measure $\mu$ on the space of worlds; by the trivial ratio step (11)→(12), $P(A)=\mu(A)/\mu(\Omega)$. Then *Postulate 3* asserts $\rho_t(q)=|\Psi_t(q)|^2$ as the "substantial density of trajectories." But the entire Born‑rule content lives in that second step. Equations 14–16 are logically valid *given* the measure; the physically substantive choice of measure is stipulated, not shown. The "continuum substance — where it is more densely packed, it is more probable" intuition (needle‑in‑a‑haystack) is a consistency story, not a justification for why *this* measure. The author acknowledges only obliquely ("the physical theory yields the justification for a measure $\mu$") that the hard question is not *how to turn a measure into a probability* but *why that measure*. The measure problem is not solved; it is relocated to "Postulate 3," which is then given no independent argument.

## The stated contribution, in my own words

Boström proposes a deterministic, non-relativistic interpretation of quantum mechanics — the **"world continuum" theory**. Its core commitments are:

1. **Ontology.** The only fundamental physical entity is a single, physically-existing *continuum*: the whole bundle of all trajectories (Bohm solutions) through 3*N*-dimensional configuration space. One trajectory = one "world". There is thus an uncountable (continuous) multiverse, but worlds do not *split* (unlike Everett), and there is no separately-existing pilot-wave field (unlike Bohm) and no discrete set of worlds (unlike Sebens/Hall). Time and world are treated as two mutually independent **"modes of existence"**, formalized in a small logical framework: *anchored* propositions of the form "object *o* has property *F* at time *t* and in world *w*", with *time-instances* (collapse the world mode), *world-instances* (collapse the time mode = a trajectory), and *time-world-instances* (= a point in 3-space).

2. **The wavefunction is a generating function, not an entity.** Ψ is not ontic. Its amplitude |Ψ|² gives the *substantial density* ρ of the trajectory-continuum in configuration space, and its phase gives the flow via the Bohm/guiding equation; the pair (ρ, ξ), the trajectory function, completely determines the world continuum. Global scale and global phase are physically redundant.

3. **Probability as epistemic, Laplacean, continuum-grade.** The observer simply doesn't know which world they inhabit. Boström generalizes Laplace's ratio rule to a continuum via a Lebesgue measure μ(*A*) = ∫_A ρ dq (the "substantial amount"), so P(*A*) = μ(*A*)/μ(Ω). This "derives" the Born rule from the density ρ = |Ψ|², *without* Bohm's quantum-equilibrium hypothesis and *without* an Everettian branch weight. "Collapse" is then shown to be purely subjective (the observer's trajectory, post-measurement, lives in the region corresponding to a single eigenvalue).

The paper argues this is cleaner than Bohm (no empty-branch/zombie-world ontology, no wave-particle dualism, no QEH), than Everett (worlds are precisely defined trajectories, not vague branches), and than the finite-many-worlds accounts of Sebens and Hall.

## Novelty relative to the cited prior work

**What is genuinely (or at least distinctly) new here:**

- **The "world continuum as a single substance" ontology.** Recasting the entire universe as one continuous *material-like* substance (a "fluid" of trajectories), with density ρ interpreted as *substantial density* rather than as a probability density, is the theory's signature move. - **The two-mode logical framework** (time and world as independent modes of existence, with *anchored* propositions and the instance vocabulary) — a tidy, if modest, formalization of the ontology.
- **The "substantial density/amount → Laplace" restatement of the Born rule for an uncountable set**, plus the small f(*x*)=*x*² toy model used to rebut Vaidman's and Sebens' "you can't have a *density* of uncountably many worlds" objection. That rebuttal is a legitimate and useful argument within the debate.
- A conceptual distinction — **interference** (a relation *between* worlds) vs **interaction** (a relation *between* physical systems) — and a reasoned case for the continuum (vs one world, vs *K* finite worlds).

**What is *not* new — already present in the work the author cites:**

- **The wavefunction as a non-ontic bookkeeping device for an ensemble/continuum of paths** is the hydrodynamic / ensemble reading Holland and Poirier already make; the global phase/scaling redundancy is standard.
- **The continuum vs *K*-finite distinction** is, at best, a response to Vaidman (2014), Sebens (2014), and Hall et al. (2014) — an argument *within* an existing debate (Boström even presents the world continuum as the K→∞ limit of Hall's MIW), not a newly-invented object.

**A caveat on the flagship claim ("we don't need the quantum-equilibrium hypothesis"):** the theory must still *postulate* ρ = |Ψ|² (it is a generating-function postulate, alongside the Bohm equation). The only difference from Bohm is that Boström dresses this up as a *definition* of the continuum rather than a statistical hypothesis about a single actual configuration. That is a defensible and arguably more elegant reframing, but it is partly cosmetic — nothing new has been derived; the same numerical assumption is doing the work under a new name.

**Net judgment:** A clear, honest, well-organized foundations paper. Its real contribution is *conceptual and synthetic* — unifying Bohm + Everett + Poirier/Schiff + Laplace into one "world continuum" ontology, adding the two-mode logical framework, and re-presenting the Born rule in continuum measure theory. It is *not* novel in physics or mathematics: it introduces no new equations beyond Bohm's and no new predictions, and the author himself places it very close to Poirier/Schiff. As against the cited prior work it is best described as an incremental reinterpretation with a distinctive (and mildly original) ontological packaging, rather than a substantive new theory.

# Assumptions the methodology papers over or leaves unjustified

1. **Born-rule bootstrapping / circularity.** The wavefunction is stipulated to evolve by the *Schrödinger equation* (Postulate 1) and trajectories by the *Bohm equation* with ρ = |Ψ|². These are exactly the standard QM dynamics, so "deriving" the Born rule from a measure μ built out of |Ψ|² assumes almost all of QM already. 2. **The observer is "in a world".** The whole argument presumes the observer exists as a well-defined world-trace — i.e., that Joe's world is one specific trajectory γ. But observers are macroscopic objects; the paper concedes (footnote and §2.2) that defining macroscopic objects *across worlds* is "highly nontrivial" and simply asserts it "can be settled"; the measurement derivation requires it but never establishes it.
3. **Wavefunction ≠ continuum (redundancy of global scale/phase).** In Bohmian mechanics the *actual* configuration of particles is fundamental; here all trajectories exist and density/flow are derived from Ψ — but the author keeps Ψ while claiming it is "merely a generating function." The argument that this avoids ontological waste (vs. "zombie worlds" in Bohm) is philosophical, not demonstrated; the wavefunction's branches all remain mathematically required.
4. **Quasi-orthogonal pointer states as assumption.** Born rule recovery relies on approximating |ψ'|² ≈ Σ|ψ'ₐ|² and ηₐηₐ' ≈ 0, which hold only for strong, idealized short interactions with large g. Non-ideal, weak, or degenerate measurements are excluded without a no-go analysis.
5. **Neglect of back-action and apparatus degrees of freedom.** The measurement model assumes the apparatus stays in the pointer-coordinate configuration space, ignores gravity, spin, and any back-reaction of the continuum of worlds on the wavefunction, and treats the apparatus as effectively classical via wave-packet separation.
6. **Determinism and "subjective" randomness are assumed, not derived.** The claim that "it only appears random to the individual observer" requires an account of observer-branching and no cross-communication; the paper acknowledges (mind-and-world discussion) these are "outside the scope."
7. **Continuum vs. finite/uncountable worlds, and density meaning.** Why worlds form a *continuum* is argued by fiat ("a continuum of trajectories solve the Bohm equation"); for a *finite* set, a density function ρ = |Ψ|² (continuous) cannot represent a discrete world ensemble (a finite sum of deltas), so the very core object (substantial density) silently assumes the continuum and the Lebesgue structure of ℚ.
8. **Rigid designators / fixed N / no particle creation.** The ontology requires N fixed globally across all worlds and times, excluding field-quantum effects, reactions, relativistic processes; this is stated, but the generality of the "derivation" is limited to non-relativistic spin-free systems, which is a large swathe of physical QM.
9. **Configuration space is physical space** (ℝ^{3×N} as "the real space"), against which the author explicitly argues in §4.1 (the Monton point) — a strong ontological assumption not derivable from the formalism.
10. **Equivalence with Bohmian/Everettian mechanics is used as a feature, but the "no equilibrium hypothesis" advantage is overstated.** Bohmian mechanics needs an equilibrium assumption to recover Born statistics *for the actual configuration*; here the observer's world is one random point drawn from the |Ψ|²-weighted continuum — which is *formally the same assumption* (a uniform measure over worlds equivalent to |Ψ|² over configurations). So the claim of avoiding the QE assumption is arguably cosmetic, not a genuine derivation.

**Net assessment.** The formal apparatus is coherent and self-consistent; the "derivation" is transparent and reproducible on paper. But the methodology is an *exegesis* of Bohmian mechanics with repackaged probability: it assumes the Schrödinger and Bohm equations, the continuum, the measure μ, the observer-as-world, and the idealized measurement model, and then recovers the Born rule it already put in. The genuinely original content (the "world continuum" ontology, the modes-of-existence logical framework, the continuity-equation/continuity-of-substance framing) is philosophical rather than a new empirical or derivational achievement, and several load-bearing steps are asserted rather than proved.

Bundled with this is the explicit contrast claim (§3, §6.1): that the theory's predictions are identical to standard QM **without** relying on the quantum equilibrium hypothesis (QEH) of Bohmian mechanics, and that it therefore "avoids objective probability in favor of an epistemic account."

## Audit of the evidence

**The "derivation" rests on a postulate that is the QEH itself.** The paper is unusually candid about this in §3: the link between the world-density and the wavefunction "cannot be provided other than by *postulating* that the substantial density of trajectories … is given by ρ_t(q) = |Ψ_t(q)|²" (Postulate 3). That postulate, read into Bohmian-mechanics language, *is* the equilibrium condition ρ_t = |Ψ_t|². What the paper does is re-label an assumption that Bohmian mechanics motivates via observer ignorance plus dynamical equivariance, as a fundamental stipulation and then calls it "derived from ontological facts." So the headline selling point (no QEH) is overclaimed: the assumption is not eliminated, only demoted from hypothesis to postulate. The Born-rule recovery (eqs. 62–65) is then a genuine but thin theorem: once you postulate ρ = |Ψ|² and take the Bohm flow (whose equivariance is the standard continuity equation, Remark 3), P(A) = μ(A)/μ(Ω) follows almost immediately. That is not an empirical achievement.

**The approximation is never bounded.** The derivation to (65) uses "≈" twice for non-quasi-orthogonal pointer states (eq. 50), with the paper's only error statement being that the approximation is "limited by the technological state of the art" (§5.2). No bound on the overlap terms in (63) is given, no explicit error term, no worked numerical case. For a paper whose quantitative core is an approximate equality, this is the weakest part of the case.

**Comparison fairness is mixed, with several selective or strained readings.**
- *Bohmian mechanics*: fair on the mechanics (the Bohm equation, continuity equation, equivariance are handled correctly), but the dismissal of the QEH justifications (Valentini's subquantum H-theorem, Dürr–Goldstein–Zanghì typicality) as "not satisfactory" is asserted with no argument — it simply defers to "reasons … to be outlined separately."
- *Hall et al. (MIW)*: the "you need a probability assumption to start" criticism is valid, but it misrepresents Hall et al.'s actual contribution, which was to show the *dynamics* can be rewritten as inter-world interaction and was numerically tested (they ran simulations of double-slit-type systems). Boström's comparison is purely conceptual and never engages with the numerical evidence that MIW reproduces QM statistics in the K→∞ limit — which is arguably the same limit the world-continuum theory explicitly claims to be ("the limiting theory obtained from MIW by taking K to infinity," §6.4). Given that, the "three criticisms" are modest.
- *Sebens*: the critique (finite-worlds theory "blows up," his quantization condition (76) is unpreferrable) is fairly stated, though characterization-level ("Sebens admits").
- *Poirier–Schiff*: the claim that the two are "essentially about the same theory" is made early (§1) with appropriate hedging and is well argued in §6.6 — though this also undercuts the paper's originality story.
- *Vaidman* (continuum objection): answered with a clean measure-theoretic toy model (eqs. 79–81); that's the strongest argument in the discussion.

**Are baselines current?** For the paper's vintage (v1 Oct 2014, v6 Aug 2015), yes: it engages the 2014 papers (Hall et al. PRX 2014, Sebens 2014, Vaidman 2014) that define the current debate, and correctly attributes prior art (Poirier 2010, Schiff & Poirier 2012) — arguably too correctly, since it concedes its own theory was "essentially the same" as theirs. Nothing in the core literature is missed for that period. (As of 2026, of course, the PIW and related literature has moved on, but the audit is of the paper as written.)

**Error bars / statistical significance? None at all.** There are no experiments, no simulations, no numerical comparisons, and therefore no error bars, confidence intervals, or significance tests anywhere in the paper. That is *normal* for an interpretation paper — but the paper positions itself somewhat stronger than its genre warrants, making claims of predictive parity ("reproduce the predictions of standard QM to a degree that is only limited by the technological state of the art") and of superiority over rival no-collapse theories on conceptual grounds only.

## Verdict

The strongest quantitative claim (Born rule from substantial-amount counting) is **formally valid but evidentially thin**: the "derivation" imports the QEH as Postulate 3, the realistic-measurement result is an unquantified approximation, comparisons with rivals selectively ignore their strongest (numerical) results, and there are zero error bars because there is zero empirical content. The paper is clean, honest where it matters (prior art, postulates), and adds genuinely useful pieces — the logical "modes of existence" framework and the toy model answering Vaidman — but its core physics is Bohmian mechanics / trajectory-formulation QM in new packaging, and its main advertised advantage (no QEH) does not survive scrutiny.

Scores: originality modest (recombination of PIW-like ideas with a novel logical framing, with the author's own concession of near-equivalence to Poirier–Schiff); soundness adequate for the genre but overclaimed; impact minimal (preprint, no new predictions, no empirical content).

## [1410.5653v6-weak-point] Weakest link in the argument


This paper (Bström, arXiv:1410.5653) proposes "world continuum theory": the universe is a continuum of worlds, each world one Bohmian trajectory in 3N‑dimensional configuration space, with trajectory density ρ_t = |Ψ_t|²; the wavefunction is only a generating function, and the Born rule is said to follow from an observer's ignorance of which world they inhabit, generalized from Laplace's rule to a continuum of possibilities.

**The single weakest link is the step that converts the objective measure of worlds into an epistemic probability — the "re‑thinking of Laplace" bridge of §3.3, realized in Eqs. (14)–(15) and then plugged into the measurement derivation (62).**

**The structure of the chain up to that point is solid in its own terms.** Postulates 1–3 (Schrödinger equation, Bohm equation, ρ_t = |Ψ_t|² as substantial density) fix an ontology: every trajectory exists, worlds never split or multiply, and μ_t(Q) = ∫_Q dq ρ_t is an objective bookkeeping quantity of how many worlds cross region Q. All of that is data — statements about what exists and how it is arranged.

**The step that fails:** from "there are c times more worlds in X than Y" (a fact of the ontology) the paper infers "it is c times more probable that *our* world lies in X" — i.e. it identifies the subjective probability P_t with the physical measure:

  P_t(X) = μ_t(X)/μ_t(Q)  (Eq. 14–15)

and thereby obtains the Born rule in §5.3. This inference is exactly where the conclusion stops being entailed by the data. The justification is the "fancy" added to Laplace's rule (p. 15): "if there are c times more possibilities in A than there are in B, then it should be expected that P(A)/P(B) = μ(A)/μ(B)." But this principle is empty precisely in the setting it's needed, for two reasons:

1. **All the "possibilities" are already realized.** Laplace's rule counts physical possibilities, one of which will be realized; the agent's ignorance is about which. Here nothing is left to chance — every world actually exists, and the theory is deterministic. The only residual uncertainty in Joe's world is *indexical*: "which of these existing worlds is mine?" That is self-locating uncertainty, not a Laplacian case. The principle that an agent's credence over self-locating possibilities must track the physical measure of those possibilities is a genuine additional principle (a self‑indifference/typicality assumption of exactly the kind Wallace, Saunders–Wallace and the decision‑theoretic approaches wrestle with in Everettian mechanics — which this paper cites without closing this specific gap). It is not derived, and the paper never states or argues it.

2. **Choosing μ as the "count" of possibilities is circular for an uncountable set.** For a continuum, "how many possibilities" has no answer short of a measure; the paper *defines* the number of possibilities in X to be μ(X), where μ is the (fixed by Postulate 3 and 1) |Ψ|²‑density. With that definition, "it should be c times more probable" *just restates* P ∝ μ ∝ ‖Π_a Ψ‖² — the Born rule itself — as a premise. The "expected that" of p. 15–16 is doing the work of an unargued equivalence between a physical statement ("more substance exists in X") and an epistemic one ("I am more likely in X"). Bström himself concedes the point (p. 16): "for different measures μ we would obtain different probability measures P, so there needs to be an independent justification why a certain measure μ is the relevant one." The justifications actually offered — the continuous‑substance picture, the "Alice's ice cream" and "needle in a haystack" analogies (pp. 12, 38) — explain how to *compare amounts* of substance, but no such analogy shows that *a particular observer's world* is a μ‑typical element of the continuum. The needle argument implicitly treats the needle's (i.e. the observer's) location as if it were drawn uniformly from the haystack, which is precisely the assumption begging the question.

Everything downstream — the measurement outcome probability (62)–(65), the "subjective collapse" — is a valid computation *given* (15); none of it supplies what (15) lacks. Nor can the gap be closed by continuity of the Bohm flow (Eq. 25): that shows μ_t is time‑independent, which makes the identification stable, not justified. So the paper's headline claim — that probability is "derived" without the Bohmian equilibrium hypothesis or Everettian branch weights — is not a derivation but a re‑labelling: the quantum equilibrium content reappears in the guise of the self‑locating probability principle, hidden behind "generalized Laplace."

That is the single weakest link: **the unentailed identification of the observer's credence over worlds with the objective world‑measure μ_t (the generalized‑Laplace step, Eqs. 10→11→12→14→15).** The data (a continuum of worlds with density |Ψ_t|²) entail μ exists and is conserved; they do not entail any fact at all about an agent's probabilities.

Broader assessment: the paper is clearly written, mathematically careful in its formal details, and unusually candid about its debts (it concedes it is essentially a reformulation of Poirier–Schiff). Its genuinely novel contributions are the "modes of existence" logical framework (§2) and the ontology of the world‑continuum as a continuous *substance* rather than counting worlds. But the physical content is Bohmian trajectories with the |Ψ|² density — the formal apparatus is that of classical Bohmian mechanics with one world replaced by all of them — and its central selling point (a gap‑free Born‑rule derivation) is the very step that does not go through.

# Reproducibility evaluation — Böström, "Quantum mechanics as a deterministic theory of a continuum of worlds" (arXiv:1410.5653v6)

## What kind of paper this is (framing the question)

This is a **foundations/interpretation paper**, not an empirical or machine-learning paper. It proposes a "world continuum theory" — a non‑relativistic interpretation of QM that keeps Bohmian trajectories and the Schrödinger wavefunction but reinterprets |Ψ|² as a *substantial density* of a continuum of coexisting worlds, from which the Born rule is then "derived" by a continuous generalisation of Laplace's rule. There is no experiment, no dataset, no model training, no inference step.

**Therefore the ML‑specific artifacts named in the question — code, data, seeds, compute, prompts, pretraining details — are not applicable, and none are missing because none are part of the claimed contribution.** There is no code to release and no data to drop on a reviewer; the artefact is entirely the textual formalism on the page, and that formalism is in fact *complete*: the postulates (5.1), the Hamiltonian (32), the Bohm trajectory equation (33/34/35), the density (23), and the derivations of the continuity equation (25), the Born rule (4), and subjective collapse (5.3) are all written out in a form an engaged reader can re-derive line by line.

So in the narrow sense the paper is *mathematically* reproducible: you can reconstruct every equation from its stated postulates. But the question of whether an independent re‑implementation is **blocked** has a more substantive answer, and for a foundational paper the relevant blockers are the following.

## Gaps that impede independent re-implementation

**1. The load-bearing postulate is asserted, not derived — so the "explanation" of the Born rule is circular rather than reproducible from deeper premises.** Every step is internally consistent *once* Eq. (15) is applied to μ_t, but μ_t is then defined (Postulate 3, Eq. (23)/(35)) to *be* ρ_t = |Ψ_t|². The paper is candid about this ("This link cannot be provided other than by postulating that the substantial density of trajectories … is given by ρ_t(q)=|Ψ_t(q)|²"). An independent implementer therefore cannot *derive* the Born rule from more basic postulates; they can only accept the Bohmian quantum-equilibrium distribution under a new name and then re-derive Laplace counting on top of it. The central advertised result (probability from a continuum) rests on a postulate that is empirically and formally identical to the Bohmian quantum‑equilibrium hypothesis the paper sets out to remove. This is the single biggest hurdle to an honest re-implementation of the *argument*.

**2. No concrete worked example.** The theory is never instantiated on a specific solvable system (double slit, oscillator, 2‑level von Neumann model) and no number is produced that an experimentalist could check. The measurement model (5.3) is structural — pointer states, quasi‑orthogonality, the coupling Ŵ_SM = −g(Â ⊗ p̂_z), the conditions (48)–(52) — but the approximations that turn the exact expression (62) into the Born rule (63)–(65) are left at the level of "sufficiently large g" / "sufficiently small overlap," parameterised by the "technological state of the art." There is no reference computation to sanity‑check the apparatus against.

**3. The genuinely new claim has no operational content a re-implementation could confirm.** The paper's novel contribution over plain Bohmian mechanics is interpretive: that ρ is an *a posteriori* substantial (physical) density rather than a *probability* density, and that worlds form a genuine continuum. There is no operational criterion offered that distinguishes the two in prediction or measurement; the theory is empirically equivalent to standard QM (and to Bohmian mechanics) *by construction*. So "re-implementation" cannot distinguish this theory from its Bohmian ancestor or from Poirier–Schiff — and the author concedes it is "very close" to the latter and "formally equal to Bohmian mechanics in many aspects."

**4. Under-specified corner cases** that an implementer must guess at:
- **Spin** is deferred to "the same manner as in Bohmian mechanics" (spinor wavefunction, Ortolani & Mompard) without the actual extension being set down.
- **Relativity** is relegated to a footnote (footnote 1: replace time t by a spacelike hypersurface), deferred to a future publication — so the relativistic version simply does not exist here.
- **Initial/boundary conditions and measure calibration** (the reference region Q_0 in §3.2, well‑behaving Ψ_0 and γ_0 in Remark 2) are gestured at but not fixed; nothing pins down a unique numerical theory.
- **The no‑signalling / epistemic locality** argument (§4.1) is asserted by citing Valentini (1991) rather than proven in situ.

## Fairness caveat

It would be wrong to dock the paper for "missing code/data/seeds/prompts": this is a theory paper where the full formalism and argument *are* the deliverable, and they are presented clearly, self‑consistently, and with the derivations spelled out. Its weakness is not omission of computational detail but that its headline explanatory claim is a postulate wearing the clothing of a derivation, that it offers no worked example or empirical discriminator, and that several branches (spin, relativity, locality) are left as pointers to future work.

---

**Net:** The paper is *formally* fully reproducible — every equation follows from the three written postulates. But it is *not* re-implementable in the stronger sense of recovering, from more primitive premises, a probability derivation or an empirically distinct prediction, because the Born rule is postulated in the act of being explained, the distinguishing (interpretive) content is non‑operational, and there is no worked numerical case or empirical signature to target.

I've read the full paper — Boström, "Quantum mechanics as a deterministic theory of a continuum of worlds" (arXiv:1410.5653v6, 2015). ## An adversarial reviewer's first paragraph

"This paper repackages Bohmian mechanics under a new ontology: the quantum-equilibrium density |Ψ|² is renamed the 'substantial density' of a continuum of physically existing worlds (trajectories), and Born-rule probabilities are re-described as ignorance about which world one inhabits. The dynamical content is identical to Bohmian mechanics point for point — same Schrödinger equation (31), same guiding/Bohm equation (33) — so the theory makes no predictions that differ from any of the empirically equivalent alternatives it claims to supersede, and the paper offers no argument, empirical or otherwise, for why this ontology ought to be preferred; section 6.7, the one place this is addressed, answers the strongest published objection against a continuum of worlds (Vaidman 2014, quoted at length and explicitly targeted) with the non-sequitur 'I do not share these worries' plus a reminder that measure theory exists. The advertised achievement — a derivation of the Born rule that 'does not rely on a quantum equilibrium hypothesis' — is a relabeling: postulate 3 asserts, without argument, that the trajectory density *is* |Ψ|², which is precisely the quantum-equilibrium hypothesis, here stripped of even Bohmian mechanics' equivariance framing. Worse, the actual derivation on p. 30 only shows the probabilities agree with the Born rule *approximately*, with the error set by pointer-packet overlap, a caveat that is absent from the abstract, the introduction, and the concluding sentence of §5.3. The 'logical framework' of section 2 is a naming convention (time-/world-instances), not a logic: no syntax, no inference rules, no nontrivial proposition is ever handled with it. The proposed fix to Monton's particle-labeling objection via the tensor space R^{3×N} requires labeled, distinguishable particles and therefore fails exactly where the theory's claimed ontology of the *universe* is needed — systems of identical particles, for which no 'j-th particle' exists. Between the approximate Born rule, the unengaged Vaidman/Sebens objections, the missing identical-particle case, and the bare re-postulation of |Ψ|², the paper's central claims substantially overstate its deliverables."

## Overclaiming sentences, located

1. **Abstract: the theory "explains (1) the subjective occurrence of probabilities, (2) their quantitative value as given by the Born rule…".** Overclaim on both counts. (i) The quantitative value is derived only in the idealized limit; the actual result, eq. (65) preceded by the sentence "So the probability that Joe obtains the measurement result 'a' **approximately coincides** with the probability given by the Born rule," is approximate for every realistic (finite-separation) measurement, and the approximation is inherited entirely from the stipulated pointer-packet separation (49)–(50). "Explains the quantitative value as given by the Born rule" asserts exactness the derivation does not establish. (ii) "Explains" for (1) is rhetorical; no mechanism or argument is given for why self-locating ignorance over a continuum is the *right* epistemic stance — it is assumed.

2. **Introduction, p. 2: "The theory that I am going to propose offers a transparent and consistent interpretation…"** "Transparent and consistent" are evaluative assertions, not results. Consistency of an interpretation that is dynamically identical to Bohmian mechanics is at most inherited, and the paper never argues transparency; it is asserted in the first paragraph of claims and never defended.

3. **Abstract/Introduction: "precisely defined postulates from where the predictions of standard quantum mechanics can be derived."** Two problems. "Precisely defined" fails for postulate 3, whose operative clause — "The trajectories form a continuous substance" — is an ontological declaration, not a mathematical condition (its mathematical content, µ_t(Q) = ∫_Q |Ψ|², is what does the work, and the paper does not present it that way). "Can be derived" elides that the predictions are reproduced only approximately (pointer overlap) and only for spin-free, fixed-N Hamiltonians of the exact form (32); the spin extension is a one-sentence hand wave ("Spin can be included in the same manner as in Bohmian mechanics").

4. **Introduction, p. 2: "The quantitative form of such epistemic probability does not rely on a 'quantum equilibrium hypothesis' as in Bohmian mechanics, or a 'branch weight' as in Everettian mechanics, but is derived from the concept of a substantial density of trajectories in configuration space."** This is the paper's central overclaim, and it is not sustainable from the paper's own content. Eq. (35), restated as postulate 3/eq. (36), *postulates* ρ_t = |Ψ|₂₂ — as the author concedes at the end of §3.3, "This link cannot be provided other than **by postulating** that the substantial density of trajectories… is given by ρ_t(q) = |Ψ_t(q)|²." That is the quantum equilibrium hypothesis verbatim, renamed. The derivation of P(A) = µ(A)/µ(Ω) (eq. 12) is Laplace's rule with a chosen measure; *all* of the content is in the choice of µ, and the choice is stipulated. The QEH has not been avoided; it has been re-ontologized, and the paper's comparison with Bohmian mechanics ("I do not consider these justifications of the quantum equilibrium hypothesis satisfactory… In the world continuum theory, there is no quantum equilibrium hypothesis") is therefore misleading.

5. **Abstract/Introduction: "Elementary particles have at all times and in all worlds a well-defined position in 3D space; there are no such things as 'probability clouds' and 'objective uncertainty'."** Presented in the declarative. These are *postulates of the theory*, which (being empirically indistinguishable from alternatives) do not license assertions about how Nature is. A theory that no experiment can distinguish from Copenhagen, Bohm, and Everett cannot be reported as settling the existence of objective uncertainty.

6. **§4.1, p. 20: "Monton's criticism can straightforwardly be addressed by separating spatial dimensions and particle associations into the rank-two tensor space R^{3×N}."** Overclaim. The whole fix depends on there being well-defined "j-th particle" labels; for systems of identical particles the physical configuration space is the quotient (ℝ³)ᴺ/S_N, in which the label "particle j" is physically meaningless, so Monton's charge ("nowhere… is it specified which dimensions correspond to which particles") revives precisely in the case that matters for real matter (electrons). The postulates (§5.1) are in fact stated only for a system with distinguished masses m₁…m_N and a potential V(q̂), and the paper never confronts identical-particle QM, despite repeatedly (and only in the introduction) offering an account of the *entire universe*.

7. **§4.2, p. 23: "Probability is in the world continuum theory a derived concept rather than a fundamental one."** Follows the same pattern as #4: the probability *functional* is derived; the measure it integrates — the density — is a postulate doing exactly the work a probability postulate does in the rival theories. The sentence is not literally false but presents a relabeling as a derivation.

8. **§6.7, p. 43: "I do not share these worries. The mathematical formalism that I… tacitly refer to… is measure theory."** Vaidman's quoted objection is not that no measure-theoretic formalism *exists* — he explicitly works within measure theory in his own framework — but that |Ψ|² is a *weight* already doing probabilistic duty, so reading it as a "density of worlds" imports the very assumption in question. Sebens' quoted objection is the same: that ρ loses the ratio-of-sets reading "if you can no longer… understand ρ as yielding the proportion of all worlds." A one-sentence dismissal plus "measure theory exists" is a non-sequitur, and since the paper goes out of its way to cite both critics verbatim and then not answer the substantive point, an adversarial reviewer will read this as the paper's weakest moment.

9. **§6.1, p. 31: "As for Everettian mechanics… the worlds are precisely defined and do not split… in that the worlds are precisely defined and do not split (Figure 1)."** (Abstract/p. 3.) The no-splitting claim is textually true (µ is conserved, eq. 26) but framing-wise overclaimed: post-measurement, the world continuum partitions into the regions Q_a = Q_S × Z_a which — by construction — decohere and never re-interfere. Those non-interacting clusters of worlds *are* Everettian branches; the difference from Everett is that the splitting event is replaced by the absence of one, not by any difference in the structure of what exists. The paper's claim that the Everett "conundrum of the splitting of persons does not show up" (p. 31) is a claim about narrative discomfort, not about structure.

10. **Remark 2, p. 26: "Since (31) and (33) are first-order hyperbolic equations, they have a unique solution for every valid initial condition."** The Schrödinger equation is dispersive/parabolic, not hyperbolic; more consequentially, the Bohm equation is singular on the nodal set of Ψ, and uniqueness/definition of the flow there is a genuine technical point the remark's sweeping sentence papers over (the ρ₀(γ₀) > 0 clause in (43)'s lead-in quietly concedes the domain restriction).

11. **§6.4, p. 37: "It is impossible to conceptually derive probability from the MIW" and §6.6's claim that Poirier & Schiff leave "how to interpret the density function… too vague to be considered a complete equivalent."** These dismissals are stronger than the citations support; the quoted passages show Poirier & Schiff explicitly considering the "all trajectories equally real" reading and deferring it, not failing to see it. And the MIW argument in the second paragraph of §6.4 ("it is just the limiting theory… by taking the number of worlds to infinity") silently assumes MIW's own initial |Ψ|² postulate, so the limit argument inherits the very probability assumption it is meant to dispense with.

12. **Recurring: "all worlds equally exist" / "the physically existing universe (or multiverse, if one prefers to say)."** The hedged "(or multiverse, if one prefers to say)" alongside flat ontological assertions elsewhere ("physically existing entity") is a framing inconsistency: the paper at once declares ontology (so an adversarial reviewer can attack it) and gestures that it is optional (so a second reviewer can attack that nothing is actually committed).

- **Register drift.** The prose oscillates between careful foundations prose and digressive idiom: the ice-cream/Alice parable (§3.1), the needle-in-haystack aside (p. 39), traffic lights (§6.2), and self-described "sloppy talk" (p. 11) undercut the precision the abstract claims. For a paper whose selling point is precision of ontology, extended colloquial analogies work against it.

The Born-rule computation of §5.3 re-proves, for one idealized measurement, what §3.3 already established in general; neither location flags that (65) is the *only* concrete derivation and an approximate one at that.

The framework is internally coherent and the math is largely correct (the one point I checked that could have been wrong — the push-forward eq. (45) — is in fact right). The problems are framing: the paper presents a re-ontologization of Bohmian mechanics as a derivation and an explanation, hides the approximateness of its only concrete Born-rule computation from its own abstract, re-postulates the quantum-equilibrium assumption under a new name and then celebrates not relying on it, and points at the literature without answering the two objections it explicitly quotes. ### Summary of the proposal
The paper proposes an interpretation of non-relativistic QM in which the universe is a *continuum of worlds* — an uncountable bundle of non-crossing trajectories through configuration space, one trajectory per world. - **A genuinely novel framing:** the "substantial density of worlds" (|Ψ|² as an *empirical spatial distribution* of a real substance, not a probability density) is a genuinely different ontological reading from both QEH-Everett "branch weights" and Bohm's equilibrium hypothesis, and the R^{3×N} tensor re-encoding is a neat touch.

### Weaknesses (why not higher)
1. **Empirically and mathematically it is standard Bohmian mechanics** (for spin-free particles, plus the Poirier–Schiff trajectory formulation). The Schrödinger + Bohm equations are inherited unchanged, so there are **no new predictions or tests**; the value is purely interpretive/ontological. The near-duplication of Poirier–Schiff, openly acknowledged, limits the scope of the contribution.
2. **The "derivation" of the Born rule is circular / assumes what it purports to derive.** The headline claim is that this theory avoids Bohm's *quantum equilibrium hypothesis*, deriving Born's rule from a Laplace generalization. But the crucial step — "an observer's world is more likely to lie in a region with more trajectory-density" (the "needle in a haystack" analogy, p. 38) — is *exactly* the |Ψ|² sampling assumption, i.e. the QEH in different clothes. The author even concedes this when, in §6.4, he criticizes Hall et al. for "concealing a non-trivial and not justified probability assumption concerning the configuration of the world" — an argument he must apply to himself. The "Re-thinking Laplace" section (3.3) is rhetorically appealing but does not escape this. The paper never supplies a non-circular justification (a dynamical/typicality or principled-indifference argument) for why the observer samples the trajectory measure μ uniformly.
3. **The logical framework (§2) is over-engineered.** The *modes-of-existence* / *anchored-proposition* / *merological-sum* notation does real bookkeeping work but yields no non-trivial consequence; it could largely be stated in plain language and compressed.
4. **The "wavefunction is not a physical entity" claim is asserted, not defended**, against the fact that it remains a free object in Postulate 1. Its "generating function" status is posited without excluding the competing ontology.
5. **Minor:** the §6.7 "why a continuum" discussion and the Vaidman/Sebens measure objections rest on a 1-D measure-pushing toy model (p. 44) and are not fully confronted; there is redundancy (the probability point is made in several forms).

### The three changes that would most improve the paper
1. **Break the circularity in the probability argument.** Either (a) supply a genuine, non-derivative justification for why the observer's world is distributed according to the trajectory measure μ = |Ψ|² (a typicality/dynamical argument or a principled indifference argument over the continuum), or (b) honestly re-label the contribution — i.e., state explicitly that ρ_t = |Ψ|² is *still* an assumption and that the achievement is a measure-theoretic reinterpretation of it (an ontic "substantial density" of a real substance) plus the Laplace rule, rather than a derivation that avoids the equilibrium hypothesis. As written, the paper is at risk of the exact criticism it levels at §6.4.
2. **State the relationship to Bohmian mechanics / Poirier–Schiff up front and sharpen the delta.** A single early, unmistakable statement that, for spin-free particles, the formal dynamics is identical to standard Bohmian mechanics (and a reformulation of Poirier–Schiff), so that *the entire contribution is the ontology plus the measure reading of |Ψ|²*, would let readers judge the genuine (modest) contribution. Pair this with a compressed or removed §2 "anchored-proposition" machinery, which is heavy and contributes little.
3. **Address the discriminating content and the continuum objection head-on.** (a) State explicitly what, if anything, is empirically or conceptually *distinguishable* from Bohmian mechanics (currently nothing is); if the claim "the wavefunction is not a physical entity" is central, defend it rigorously against retaining Ψ as a free postulate. (b) Strengthen §6.7 and the responses to Vaidman/Sebens beyond the 1-D toy model — this is precisely the objection to which the "continuum" framing (vs. a large finite number of worlds) is vulnerable, and it is where the paper's distinctive claim rests.

A competent and honest re-framing of an existing theory, with a novel ontological reading of |Ψ|², but with no empirical novelty, an acknowledged near-duplication of Poirier–Schiff, and a central "derivation" of the Born rule that is at best thinly non-circular. Solid as writing, insufficient as a standalone contribution to earn stronger support.

# Peer review

**Paper:** Kim Joris Bostrom, "Quantum mechanics as a deterministic theory of a continuum of worlds" (arXiv:1410.5653v6, [quant-ph])

## Summary of the contribution

The paper proposes a non-relativistic interpretation of quantum mechanics in which the universe is a *continuum of worlds*, each world being one trajectory of the universe through configuration space (so that worlds ≅ Bohm trajectories). The wavefunction Ψ_t is not a physical field but an abstract *generating function* for a "world continuum": its modulus squared ρ_t = |Ψ_t|² is the *substantial density* ("world density") of the trajectory bundle, and its phase generates the Bohm trajectories. The logical core is an extended propositional framework in which **time** and **world** are treated as two analogously treated *modes of existence*; propositions are "anchored" in both. Probability is then derived by generalizing Laplace's indifference rule to a continuum of possibilities via a measure μ_t on configuration space, with the Born rule recovered as `P(Q) = μ_t(Q)/μ_t(ℚ)`. Measurements are ordinary Schrödinger evolutions with a von-Neumann-type apparatus, the "collapse" being merely a subjective, world-relative reinitialization; the author argues (via a rank-two tensor configuration space ℝ^{3×N}) that the theory escapes Monton's criticism of wavefunction realisms, and it needs neither the quantum equilibrium hypothesis nor branch weights.

## Strengths

- **Clear, honest, and well-motivated writing.** The relationship to Bohmian mechanics, Everett, Tipler, the MIW approach (Hall et al.), Newtonian QM (Sebens), and especially Poirier & Schiff is laid out with uncommon candor — the author concedes that their formal theory is "essentially about the same theory," which is commendable intellectual honesty.
- **The logical "modes of existence" framework** (Sec. 2) is a genuinely original device. Treating *world* as a mode on the same footing as *time*, with anchored propositions `F o @ (t, w)` and the instance/world-instance/time-world-instance distinctions, is a novel and internally consistent formal apparatus that sharpens the otherwise vague notion of a "world."
- **The response to Monton** (Sec. 4.1) is a substantive, correct contribution: replacing the vector configuration space ℝ^{3N} with the tensor space ℝ^{3×N} dissolves the "3N lumped dimensions" objection cleanly, and the Hilbert inner product carries over without loss of structure. This is a real point that applies against wavefunction realisms more broadly.
- **The measurement section (Sec. 5.3)** is competently done: pointer states, quasi-orthogonality from compact supports, Born-rule recovery (Eq. 62–65), and the derivation of a *subjective* collapse (Eq. 66–70) are all handled clearly.
- **The measure-theoretic re-interpretation of Laplace** (Sec. 3.3) is legitimate, and the toy model `f(x)=x²` giving `P([0,a]) = √a` (Eqs. 79–81) is a nice, concrete illustration that epistemic probability over a continuum is well-behaved and not reducible to counting.

## Weaknesses (the ones that matter for a prestigious venue)

1. **The Born rule is effectively a postulate, not a derivation.** The paper's headline claim is that the Born rule "emerges" from insufficient knowledge of which world one inhabits. But the chain of argument rests on Postulate 3, which *stipulates* ρ_t = |Ψ_t|² as the world density, so μ_t(Q) = ∫_Q dq |Ψ_t|², and then `P = μ_t(Q)/μ_t(ℚ)` is *trivially* the Born rule. What generalizes Laplace's rule is not a probabilistic principle but the observation that "if there is a measure on the possibility space, probability is the ratio of measures." The heavy lifting — that the *correct* measure counting worlds is the one with density |Ψ|² — is inserted by fiat, not derived. The author is partly transparent, noting the physical theory must supply the justification for *which* measure is relevant; here that justification is only the postulate. So the comparison to Bohmian mechanics (which avoids the quantum equilibrium hypothesis) is fair, but the comparison to Everett is more delicate: BM trades the QEH for this postulate, and it is at least as ad hoc. The paper would be considerably stronger if it either (a) stated plainly that the Born rule is a postulate and argued only that it is a *coherent epistemic* one, or (b) genuinely derived the |Ψ|² measure from non-probabilistic premises (decision theory, envariance, self-locating uncertainty). As written, it is "the Born rule wearing epistemic and measure-theoretic clothing," which is a weaker object than the framing suggests.

2. **No empirical novelty.** The theory predicts exactly standard non-relativistic QM (up to standard measurement approximations). It makes no new, independently testable contact — an expected feature of any interpretation, but it means that for a top *physics* journal the empirical case is nil. This is better received by a foundations venue (e.g. *Studies in History and Philosophy of Modern Physics*, *Foundations of Physics*) than by PRL/PR. A "prestigious journal" framing in the physics sense is hard to satisfy.

3. **Novelty is undercut by the Poirier–Schiff concession.** Because the author grants that the formal core ("the same trajectories, the same continuum") is Poirier and Schiff's, the genuine increments are: the ontological claim that all Bohm trajectories are co-equal *real* worlds with no privileged actual configuration; the measure-theoretic probability reading; and the logical framework. These are real but comparatively modest conceptual contributions, and the paper would fare better positioning itself expressly as an *interpretation* of the Poirier–Schiff formalism rather than a standalone new theory.

4. **The "continuous substance / substantial amount / substantial density / substantial flow" terminology** is heavy and does not buy much over standard measure-theoretic language ("density," "measure," "flux"). Recurring appeals to children's intuition (Alice and the ice-cream heaps) are charming but sit slightly awkwardly in a technical paper and do not materially advance the physics.

5. **Scoping is limited.** Non-relativistic, spin-handled only by an aside (spinor Ψ, spin interactions "in the same manner as in Bohmian mechanics"), no QFT, no gravity. A strong submission would at least sketch the relativistic/field-theoretic extension or argue concretely why it is expected to carry over (it does in Bohmian mechanics, and here worlds are trajectories, so it is plausible — but it is asserted, not argued).

6. **Minor presentation issues.** Occasional typographical slips ("in my approach is is not an issue," "the the theory"), and the elaborate modal-logic notation (rigid designators, Kripke analogy) is introduced but only lightly used. A few sections (2.1–2.3) could be tightened. These are cosmetic.

## Bottom line

This is a **sound, carefully written, and intellectually honest foundations paper** that assembles existing ideas (Bohm trajectories as real worlds, the Poirier–Schiff trajectory formalism) into a consistent whole with two genuinely nice contributions — the "modes of existence" logical framework and the clean tensor-space rebuttal to Monton. Its principal weakness is that the central claim — an *epistemic derivation* of the Born rule — is weaker than advertised: the measure that yields the Born rule is itself postulated, so the paper delivers a *coherent re-reading* of the Born rule rather than an independent derivation, and it offers no empirical novelty. As a result it is best suited to a philosophy-of-physics venue, where it is an acceptable, above-average contribution, rather than a high-impact physics journal, where the absence of new predictions and the Poirier–Schiff overlap limit its reach.

**Recommendation:** Minor-to-major revisions for a foundations venue; the author should (i) temper the "derivation" language or derive the |Ψ|² measure from explicitly non-probational premises, (ii) sharpen the claim of novelty over Poirier–Schiff, and (iii) either sketch the relativistic extension or clearly delimit the domain.

# Review: "Quantum mechanics as a deterministic theory of a continuum of worlds" (Bostrom 2015)

I've read the full manuscript carefully (all 49 pages). Here is my assessment.

## What the paper is

The paper proposes "world continuum theory": the universe is a continuous bundle of de Broglie–Bohm trajectories in 3N-dimensional configuration space, each trajectory *being* a world, with a time-dependent "world density" ρ_t = |Ψ_t|² saying how densely worlds are packed in configuration space. Probability is recast as *self-locating* epistemic uncertainty — an observer does not know which world's trajectory they live on — and the Born rule is obtained by generalizing Laplace's rule of indifference from a count of finite alternatives to a measure μ on a continuum of possibilities. A "logical framework" treats time and world as two "modes of existence" with anchored propositions, the wavefunction is demoted to a non-physical "generating function" of the trajectory bundle, and measurement is treated as ordinary (short, strong) interaction with pointer states.

## Strengths

1. **Genuinely well-crafted and honest.** The postulate structure (Section 5) is clean and complete: everything is either a postulate or derived, and the author explicitly flags where he must *postulate* that the substantial density is ρ_t = |Ψ_t|² (p. 17). This is the right posture.

2. **The literature discussion is outstanding.** The comparison with Bohmian mechanics (§6.1), Tipler (§6.3), MIW of Hall et al. (§6.4), Sebens' Prodigal QM (§6.5), and above all Poirier & Schiff (§6.6) is careful, fair, and instructive. It is to the field's credit that he concedes candidly that Poirier–Schiff independently arrived at essentially the same formal core, and says precisely what his contribution adds (the physical/ontological reading of ρ). Most interpretation papers get this badly wrong.

3. **The "continuous substance" / substantial-amount / substantial-density apparatus (§3.1–3.3) does real work.** It is the right measure-theoretic language to answer Vaidman's and Sebens' objections to a continuum of worlds — "infinite worlds in any finite region" is not a reason the density picture is incoherent, and the toy embedding model in §6.7 (X = [0,1], f(x) = x², yielding P([0,a]) = √a) is a nice, accessible demonstration. I would promote that example from the discussion into §3 proper, where it belongs.

4. **Real technical content, not just philosophy.** The response to Monton's configuration-space criticism by replacing 𝔼3ᴺ with the tensor space 𝔼3ˣᴺ (§4.1, eq. 17–19) is a clean, non-trivial move that dissolves the "which dimensions belong to which particle" objection while preserving the Hilbert structure. The claim of epistemic vs. ontological nonlocality in §4 is a genuinely interesting reframing of Valentini's signaling results.

5. **Coherent ontology.** Demoting the wavefunction to a generating function removes both the "zombie worlds" objection to Bohmian empty branches and the one-way wavefunction–particle interaction problem. The theory is, in a sense, *simpler* than Bohmian mechanics: one entity (the continuum) whose generating function is Ψ.

## Weaknesses — and what I'd want fixed

1. **The "derivation" of the Born rule is weaker than advertised, and you should say so plainly.** The Born rule enters entirely through Postulate 3: the world density *is* |Ψt|². The Laplace generalization only then converts a *physical* measure into an *epistemic* one. Compare Bohmian mechanics: there, ρ = |Ψ|² is the quantum equilibrium hypothesis about the *actual configuration*; here, it is a postulate about the *world density*. Both are non-dynamical, and both enjoy equivariance from the Bohm continuity equation (your eq. 25). You claim (p. 5, 32) that your theory "does not depend on a quantum equilibrium hypothesis" — but that sells your advantage short. The real, defensible advantage is a *categorial* one: the measure in your theory is an objective, ontological fact about the distribution of worlds (the needle-in-a-haystack argument of p. 38–39), not a statistical postulate about an actual configuration's typicality. Make that the explicit claim, and note that a non-equitvariant ρ would predict deviations from the Born rule — a Valentini-style observable fact your theory thereby shares with Bohmian mechanics.

2. **The logical framework (§2) is underdelivering for the space it takes.** In practice it reduces to "propositions can be anchored in a world variable, and worlds ↔ trajectories" (eq. 3). It is notation, not logic — there are no inference rules, no consistency conditions, no theorem. Either develop the framework further so it earns its status as a "logical framework" (e.g., show the world anchor handles the identity-of-objects-across-worlds problem for macroscopic objects, which you gesture at on p. 8 but set aside), or compress it to a short "notational conventions" section and make the time/world analogy a discussion point rather than a foundation.

3. **Uncountable worlds deserves a sharper defense of its cost.** Each individual world has measure zero; an observer's self-locating credence is a measure-theoretic object, never a count. You handle this correctly, but I'd want you to confront the fact that your ontology is *more* than Tipler's or even Everett's (Everett's world count tracks branching dynamics; yours is the entire solution bundle of the Boem equation for all time, for all worlds, with no splitting or recombination). "Continuum because the trajectories form one" (end of §6.7) is a decent *negative* argument (rejecting finitely many worlds is more arbitrary, not less); I'd want a positive one too — e.g., what breaks if one postulates a finite K and takes the limit, beyond the "extra postulate" objection?

4. **Scope limitations are stated but under-weighted.** Non-relativistic, spin-free, non-interacting particle number fixed across all worlds and times (p. 9). The relativity footnote (p. 8) is a single paragraph; the tensor configuration-space construction doesn't obviously extend to the relativistic or QFT case. For a foundations paper this is survivable, but the abstract should carry the caveat rather than letting it lurk in footnotes.

5. **Minor:** "world flows through the time mode" / "world mode" terminology is introduced without ever pinning down the "mode" mathematically; the claim "quantum mechanics is a *many-times* theory" (p. 7) is intriguing but asserted, not developed; a typo on p. 8 ("modes of existence□").

## Verdict

This is a competent, above-average foundations paper: clean formal core, an unusually generous and accurate placement among neighboring theories, and genuinely interesting re-framings (epistemic actual configuration, demoted wavefunction, tensor configuration space). Its main conceptual contribution — reading ρ_t = |Ψ_t|² as an objective *substantial density of a continuum of worlds* rather than a probability density — is a real advance in clarity of the measure's status, but the theory is empirically equivalent to Bohmian mechanics and its logical novelty is more presentational than structural. I would not, however, reject it for being "just" Bohmian mechanics in new clothes: the clothes change which questions come up, and several of the answers (Monton, Vaidman, Tipler, Poirier–Schiff placement) are better.

**Originality: 4** — new conceptual framework and several original moves (logical anchoring, substantial-density probability, tensor configuration space), though the core dynamics is standard Bohmian and the closest neighbor (Poirier–Schiff) is well-documented.

**Overall quality: 4** — sound, coherent, well-argued, honest about its postulates; held back from a 5 by the over-claimed Born-rule "derivation," the thin logical framework, and the non-relativistic scope.

**Impact: 2** — a respectable addition to the interpretation literature with some citations, but no new predictions and a core shared with better-cited neighboring work; unlikely to be field-shaped on its own.
