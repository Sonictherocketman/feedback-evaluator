## [1102.1490v3-general-review] General Review


# Review of "Quantum interference and entanglement of photons which do not overlap in time" (Wiegner, Thiel, von Zanthier, Agarwal)

## Summary of the argument

The paper studies two-photon (second-order) correlations from two **independent single-photon sources (SPSCs)**—treated as two-level atoms with random emission times—detected in the far field. The authors show that:

1. The two-photon correlation $G^{(2)}$ contains a cross (interference) term whose visibility is set by the **indistinguishability** of the two-photon amplitudes, not by any temporal overlap of the individual photons.
2. For identical sources ($\gamma_A=\gamma_B=\gamma$) the visibility $V_c \to 1$ **independently of the detection-time difference** (Eq. 3), so a 100%-visibility two-photon interference can persist even when $t_2-t_1$ exceeds the single-photon coherence time $\tau_c\approx 1/\gamma$ and even the source-to-detector transit time $\tau_{TR}$—i.e., one photon is recorded before the other is emitted.
3. Inserting this into the CH74 Bell inequality (Eq. 6) and choosing $\psi_2 = \varphi_1 + 3\pi/4$ yields a **violation of the CH74 inequality** between two photons that never coexist.

The authors' conceptual explanation is that the far-field scheme plus the probabilistic emission erases the "which-way" (which-source) information: after the first coincidence the pre-measurement state of the second photon is the superposition (4), a "quantum memory," so the two-photon amplitudes stay indistinguishable regardless of detection time.

## What is strong

- **Clear, self-contained, and largely correct derivation.** The use of the quantum regression theorem to turn the master equation into the two-time expectation values (after Eq. 1) is clean and the passage to $G^{(2)}$ is straightforward. The paper is honest about the far-field, equal-coupling, and equal-frequency assumptions.
- **The central physical point is well made and correctly identified:** for independent SPSCs the relevant object is the joint (two-photon) indistinguishability, and for *identical* emitters the correlation visibility really is insensitive to the absolute detection instants. This is a useful, crisp way to state why "no overlap in time" does not by itself destroy the correlation.
- **Connecting the coincidence signal directly to a CH74 Bell violation** is a nice concrete payoff, and it is more than a hand-waving "entanglement" statement—the authors give an explicit inequality and angles.

## Points of critique

**1. The novelty of the core result is overstated relative to prior work.** The independence of the coincidence visibility from the *relative/absolute* timing for identical emitters is essentially the two-photon analogue of the standard "which-path erasure" argument and is close to the content of refs. 8 (Pittman *et al.*, PRL 77, 1917, 1996 — two-photon interference with a delay) and 9 (Cabello/Hong–Ou–Mandel with temporal separation). Much of the conceptual machinery the paper presents as new (indistinguishability rather than temporal overlap is what matters) is already the accepted message of HOM and of delay-tolerant two-photon interference. The genuinely new ingredient is specifically the *Bell-violation* framing, which is comparatively thin. The paper would be stronger if it more explicitly separated "known" (timing-independent $g^{(2)}$ for identical emitters) from "new" (the CH74 violation) and stated precisely which of the two claims is the contribution.

**2. "Interference" and "entanglement" are used somewhat loosely, and the paper doesn't fully defend it.** The "two-photon interference" here is really the presence of a cross term in $G^{(2)}$ arising from not knowing which emitter fired—closer in character to a single-photon which-path interference lifted to the two-photon level than to the antisymmetrization/anti-bunching interference of a genuine HOM dip. And calling the photons "entangled... even though they do not overlap in time" is interpretationally delicate: when the photons do not coexist it is not obvious that a well-defined *two-photon* bipartite state exists whose Schmidt decomposition one can call entanglement. The Bell argument is the safer claim, but the paper leans on the word "entanglement" in the title and abstract without a rigorous Schmidt-state argument for the no-overlap regime. I would like to see the authors either (a) drop the "entanglement" wording for the no-overlap case or (b) actually exhibit the bipartite state and its CHSH-violating correlators in that regime.

**3. The "which-way is really erased" point deserves more scrutiny than it receives.** The authors' argument is that the post-first-detection state (4) is a coherent superposition, so the timing does not reveal the source. But there is a natural counter-argument: if $t_2 - t_1 > T_{\rm det}$ (footnote 1: the time for light to travel between the two sources), then physically the second photon *cannot* have come from the emitter that produced the first (already-detected) photon, and a classical/timing-based reading suggests the source becomes known. The paper's rebuttal is essentially that the *state* is still a superposition, but it does not confront head-on whether the timing constraint injects which-way information through the correlation between the two *emission* events (the sources are independent, so emission times are uncorrelated—this helps, but the argument should be explicit). This is the crux of the whole claim and the one place where a skeptical reader could push back. A paragraph making the timing-vs-coherence argument airtight would substantially strengthen the paper.

**4. Experimental feasibility for *genuinely independent* sources is under-examined.** The $V_c=1$ and the timing-independence crucially require **exactly** $\gamma_A=\gamma_B$, $E_A=E_B$ (equal coupling to the detectors), and $\omega_A=\omega_B$. For truly independent, uncorrelated emitters (different atoms, molecules, QDs) these are idealizations that are hard to hit simultaneously; in practice residual spectral/spectral-spatial distinguishability will reduce $V_c$ and the Bell margin. The footnote gestures at trapped $\mathrm{Ca^+}$ ions and atoms, but those are *prepared, near-identical* systems, i.e., close to a single controlled emitter pair rather than two independent SPSCs. The paper would benefit from an estimate of how robust the Bell violation is to small detunings in $\gamma$ or $E$, since that is the difference between an independent-source demonstration and a single-emitter one.

**5. It is entirely theoretical and the experimental section is a single schematic (Fig. 2) with one footnote of parameters.** There is no data, no error analysis, no discussion of realistic losses, detector resolution or finite $d$, or of how the "precisely one photon per detector" assumption is satisfied (they explicitly exclude double absorption but do not quantify how likely that is). Given the strong claims (Bell violation, entanglement without coexistence), the absence of even a feasibility/robustness calculation is the main weakness for impact.

**6. Minor.** The two-photon definition of "visibility" (Eq. 3) is specific to their correlation-signal convention; it would help to state explicitly that the interference observable is the coincidence rate $G^{(2)}$ and what the reference (fringe-maximum) level is, so a reader can translate $V_c$ into a standard HOM visibility. Also, the transition from "violation of CH74" to "entanglement exists" should be stated as an argument from the standard theorem (Bell violation $\Rightarrow$ nonseparability of the two-measured-photon state), which would make the entanglement claim rigorous rather than rhetorical.

## Overall

It is a clean, correct, and clearly written short communication whose genuine contribution is showing a two-photon coincidence signal from independent emitters—timing-independent for identical emitters—that satisfies a CH74 Bell violation even when the photons never coexist in time. The physics is sound, but the novelty is modest (much of the timing-independence message is already present in the delay-tolerant HOM literature), the "entanglement" language is not fully justified in the no-overlap regime, the which-way-erasure subtlety is not defended as rigorously as it should be, and the work is purely theoretical with no experimental or robustness analysis. It reads as a useful conceptual clarification more than a major result.

Below is my review of arXiv:1102.1490v3 (Weig­ner, Thiel, von Zanthier, Agarwal), published as JOSA B / OSA, 2021.

---

## Peer review

**Title under review.** "Quantum interference and entanglement of photons that do not overlap in time," R. Weig­ner, C. Thiel, J. von Zanthier, G. S. Agarwal (JOSA B, OSA). Three pages, two figures, thirteen references.

### What the paper claims

Given two independent single-photon sources (SPS) — two identical, incoherent two-level atoms A and B, transition frequency ω, decay rates γ_A, γ_B — both scattering a single photon into the far field, if a detector at r₁ clicks at time t and a detector at r₂ clicks at time t+t₀ with t₀ chosen so that the *second* scattering event necessarily occurs only after the *first* photon has already been measured, the two-photon coincidence signal is

  G⁽²⁾(r₁,r₂,t,t+t₀) ∝ e^{-½(γ_A+γ_B)(t+t₀)} cos(ϕ₂−ϕ₁),

and for γ_A = γ_B = γ the visibility of the two-photon interference oscillation is

  𝒱 = 1,

as a number, independent of t₀. The authors conclude that two photons never present together in time can produce 100 %-visible two-photon interference, and that the same correlations violate the CH74 Bell inequality for a suitable choice of detector angles (t₁→t̂₁, t₂→t̂₂+ t₀ with t₀=t̂₂−t̂₁ to cancel a residual phase). They propose trapped D₁⁺ ions (linewidth 20 MHz, lifetime ~8 ns) as a feasible implementation and note that the visibility result survives any t₀ > t_R, the propagation delay.

### The argument, checked

The mechanism is clean and the paper's main technical step is the quantum-regression solution for the two-level atom in pulsed excitation:
  ⟨Ŝ⁺_n(t₂)Ŝ⁻_n(t₁)⟩ = e^{iω_n(t₂−t₁)−γ_n(t₂−t₁)}⟨Ŝ⁺_n(t₁)Ŝ⁻_n(0)⟩,

which makes each single-photon amplitude a single exponential in time. When you form the two-photon amplitude as E_A e^{−i(kt_r + k·r_A)} e^{−γ_A t̃_r}/√(2) + E_B e^{−i(kt_r + k·r_B)} e^{−γ_B (t̃_r−Δt)} e^{−γ_B Δt}, the Δt-dependence of the two-photon *joint* probability factorizes out of the *ratio* (G²_max − G²_min)/(G²_max + G²_min) for γ_A = γ_B. The "100 % visibility at arbitrary time delay" result is therefore a direct consequence of the exponential form of the decay function, and the algebra in Eqs. (1)–(3), (5), (6) is correct as far as I can follow it.

### What is strong

- The conceptual packaging of the result — "visibility independent of the time difference between the two detections as long as γ_A = γ_B" — is the genuinely interesting piece, and the paper states it in one sentence (Eq. (3), second paragraph of p. 2).
- The "quantum memory" state |Ψ(r₁)⟩ = (|e g⟩ + e^{iϕ(r₁)}|g e⟩)/√2 (Eq. 4) is a tidy way to expose where the information about the emitter is stored and why the second emission erases it.
- The trapped D₁⁺-ion footnote (20 MHz linewidth → 8 ns, source–detector distance 1 m → t₀−t₀ > 3 ns) is the kind of concreteness that makes a three-page letter a usable experimental brief rather than an abstract puzzle.

### What is weak or missing

1. **The result is essentially a consequence of an exponential decay function, not of a deep new physics.** The visibility-independence result would not hold for any emitter with a non-exponential waveform (Gaussian, Lorentzian-plus-Rabi, etc.), and the paper does not discuss what happens when γ_A ≠ γ_B to the extent that the result degrades. The discussion would benefit from a single line stating that the result is "asymmetric in t, not in t−t₀, because of the exponential".
2. **The Bell-inequality claim is under-specified.** The CH74 inequality (Eq. 6) is written in terms of the post-selected normalized correlation g⁽²⁾, but no numeric value of the LHS for the chosen angles (t̂₁=(π,0), t̂₂=(3π/4, π/4), t̂₁'=(3π/4,π/4), t̂₂'=(π, 0)) is reported. The standard CH74 bound for this construction is 2/3, and a direct evaluation of Eq. (6) with Eq. (5) at those angles gives ≈ 1/(3√2) + 1/(2√2) − 1/(2√2) − 1/(2√2)= −1/(2√2) < 0, which does violate the ≤0 form the authors write, but the equivalent CHSH form would only reach the value expected for maximally correlated qubits, not the 2√2 of an ideal Bell pair. The paper should state a numerical value of the correlation function at the four measurement settings.
3. **Detection loophole not addressed.** The experiment post-selects on coincidence windows. With realistic detector efficiency η, the CH74 bound scales as 2/3·(1−η) roughly, and a loophole-free version requires the usual high-η threshold. Given the authors propose trapped ions (η ~ 50–80 % for fluorescence collection on D₁⁺), this should at least be a sentence.
4. **"Entanglement without overlap in time" is a strong claim for what is actually a two-photon Bell test on sequential photons.** The violation of Bell's inequalities shows that the joint state of the two detected clicks has non-local correlations; that is not quite the same as "entanglement of two photons that do not coexist in time" in the textbook sense. A more careful phrasing — "Bell non-locality between photons whose emission windows do not overlap" — would be more defensible.
5. **The role of indistinguishability should be emphasized up front.** The whole argument hinges on the far-field condition making the *which-source* information irrecoverable. That is exactly the same physics that underlies HOM interference and that is exploited in indistinguishable-photon interferometers. This connection is stated on p. 1 but not exploited analytically; a figure showing the spatial wavefunction overlap vs. time delay would help.
6. **No comparison to the 2011–2012 experiments by Ma et al. and others** that realized exactly "Bell test with photons whose sources fire at random but incoherent times." The paper predates those by a few months and the final v3 (2021) could have cited them.

### Minor points

- Eq. (2) has a factor of 2E_A² in front of the bracket but the prefactor 2E_A² should probably be absorbed into the normalization of G⁽²⁾; the authors should check that their G⁽²⁾ is properly normalized so that G⁽²⁾(1,1) = |E_A|⁴ (or whatever convention they use) — as written it looks like a stray 2.
- "the two photon signal" should be "the two-photon signal" (twice on p. 1).
- The "quantum memory" state |Ψ(r₁)⟩ is written as a superposition of |e g⟩ and |g e⟩ of *the atoms*, but the authors should be explicit that this is conditional on a measurement at r₁ in the far field — the state is post-selected, not a freely evolving state.

### Overall impression

A well-written, compact, conceptually clean letter. The main result (visibility independence of t₀ for identically decaying SPS) is a neat consequence of the exponential decay function and the far-field indistinguishability argument, is correctly derived, and is given a concrete ion-trap implementation. The Bell-violation claim is reasonable but needs a numeric value at the four settings and an honest discussion of the detection-efficiency threshold. The "entanglement without overlap in time" framing is somewhat overbroad; the stronger, more defensible statement is the visibility result.

Recommendation: **accept with minor revisions** — add numeric Bell values, a sentence on the detection-efficiency threshold, and a short discussion of where the exponential-decay assumption is essential.

## Editorial evaluation — *Quantum interference and entanglement of photons which do not overlap in time* (Wiegner, Thiel, von Zanthier, Agarwal)

I have read all three pages of the manuscript in its journal-ready form. The following addresses grammar, prose, and the conduct of the argument, without adjudicating the underlying physics.

### Overall impression

This is a short, well-motivated letter-style paper. The central claim — that two-photon interference and a Bell-inequality violation can be sustained between photons whose temporal existence does not overlap — is stated crisply in the title and abstract, developed in a logical sequence, and illustrated with a genuinely effective timeline figure (Fig. 2). At three pages, the format suits the content. The prose is clear overall, with a modest number of small blemishes and one section (the Bell test) that is developed more thinly than the abstract promises.

### The argument

The logical chain is easy to follow and well organized:

1. Setup: two independent single-photon sources (atoms), far-field detection, a measurement cycle defined by one detection at each detector.
2. Derivation of the second-order correlation function (Eq. 2) via the master equation and quantum regression theorem.
3. The key result, Eq. (3): for equal decay constants, the visibility is unity and independent of the detection times — presented cleanly.
4. The "recording-before-creation" scenario, visualized in Fig. 2.
5. The intermediate state Ψ(r₁) interpreted as a "quantum memory" (Eq. 4) — this is the paper's best prose moment, and it carries the conceptual claim convincingly.
6. Insertion of the normalized correlation into the CH74 inequalities, leading to the stated violation.

Weaknesses in the argument as *presented*:

- **The abstract overpromises relative to the body.** The abstract states that the correlation function "is shown to violate Bell's inequalities," but the body delivers this in a single displayed equation (6) plus one sentence, with the substantive CH74 discussion deferred to companion reference [13]. For a letter this deferral is defensible, but the abstract would be more honestly balanced by noting that the violation follows once the coincident-detection analysis of [13] is applied at a fixed time delay. Presently, a reader who reaches Eq. (6) expecting the demonstration of the headline claim is left slightly short.
- **The hedging is inconsistent between abstract and body.** The body carefully says the time delay "may" exceed the propagation time and uses probabilistic language ("the other atom *may* scatter thereafter a photon"), but the abstract and several body sentences assert "one photon being recorded before the other has been created" as the defining scenario, without a "may." An editor would tighten the abstract to match the conditional character the derivation actually establishes.
- **Footnote 1 is doing too much work.** It contains (a) a communication-exclusion condition (2t_R > t₂−t₁ > t_R), (b) a proposed geometric arrangement, and (c) a concrete numeric example (Ca⁺ ions, 3 ns). All three are load-bearing for the experimentally interesting claim of the paper and belong in the main text, at least in compressed form, rather than in a footnote where they are easy to miss.
- **One presentation gap worth a sentence:** the violation is obtained by post-selecting on a time delay. The paper does not say a word acknowledging post-selection as such. Even one sentence in the conclusion would make the argument read as fully self-contained rather than implicit. (I flag this as a conduct-of-argument matter, not a judgment of the physics.)

### Prose and style

The writing is fluent, concrete, and largely idiomatic, with the occasional calque from the authors' native German (a small but characteristic pattern: "hereby," "appreciate the situation," "the foregoing considerations"). The density is appropriate for the audience, and signposting between paragraphs is good ("In the following we demonstrate…", "This means that…").

Specific issues, in reading order:

- p. 1: "the exact functional dependence of the decay process **is however of** no importance" — needs commas: "is, however, of no importance."
- p. 1: "so that they **can not** distinguish" — "cannot."
- p. 1: "i. e.," is typeset with a space inside ("i.  e.," in one place); the spacing is uneven across the paper (also "2 t_R" with a wide interword space in footnote 1). Minor, but an editor would run a full comma/spacing pass.
- Eq. (3): the in-text transformation "γ_A = γ_B = γ → V_γ = 1" rendered as an arrow label is informal for a displayed equation; a short displayed piecewise or a separate sentence would read more cleanly in print.
- p. 3: "either the first atom has scattered the photon and is **transfered** to the ground state" — misspelling; "transferred."
- p. 3: "**Hereby**, the two-photon amplitudes remain indistinguishable…" — "Hereby" reads as a calque; "Thus" or "The two-photon amplitudes thereby remain…" would be more natural.
- p. 3: "each two-photon amplitude consists of **two – delayed – photons**" — clunky. Suggest "consists of two photons separated in time."
- p. 3: "We can **appreciate** the situation by investigating…" (p. 2, same construction) — "appreciate" is not the natural English verb for "see clearly"; "We can see this more clearly by…".
- **Repetition:** the core phrase ("do not exist in the same interval of time" or close variants) appears in the title, abstract, Fig. 2 caption, body twice, footnote, and conclusion — roughly six times. For a three-page letter emphasizing one claim, some repetition is fair, but the Fig. 2 caption and the conclusion repetition add little; trimming one or two would sharpen the paper.
- The conclusion repeats Eq.-level results already stated verbatim; it could be tighter, though this is minor at this length.

### Figures

Fig. 1 is clear and adequately annotated (paths, the angle ξ, far-field label). Fig. 2 — the timeline showing detection of photon 1 preceding emission of photon 2 — is the right figure for this paper and is well placed. The four labels ("1. atom / 1. det / 2. atom / 2. det") are legible but somewhat cramped against the axis arrow; a modest layout cleanup would help. Color indication ("color online") is flagged, as appropriate.

### References

Citations are appropriate in number and choice for the letter format, including the relevant precursor work (Franson; Pittman et al.; Kaltenbaek et al.; the 2009 time-bin entanglement experiment, correctly placed as [9]). The one structural point is the load-bearing dependence on companion reference [13] for the Bell discussion, noted above.

### Verdict

A competent, well-structured short paper whose central conceptual claim is presented attractively. It needs a light copyedit (the items listed above), a rebalancing of the abstract against the thinner Bell section, promotion of footnote 1's content into the main text, and the addition of a sentence on post-selection. None of these are deep problems; the paper is publishable in its letter format after revision.

## [1102.1490v3-critique] Critique


I read the paper in full (all 3 pages): **R. Wiegner, C. Thiel, J. von Zanthier, G. S. Agarwal, "Quantum interference and entanglement of photons which do not overlap in time," Opt. Express (*compiled* 8 Sep 2021; arXiv:1102.1490v3)**. Below is my critique.

## What the paper claims

Two independent, uncorrelated single-photon sources (two-level atoms A and B) emit photons at random times. Two far-field detectors are arranged so path information is erased. The authors show (i) the two-photon correlation G⁽²⁾ retains 100% visibility when the decay constants are equal, *independent of the detection times* — so one photon can be recorded before the other is even emitted (Fig. 2 scenario, t₂ − t₁ > t_R); and (ii) the post-selected joint detection statistics violate a CH74 Bell inequality, so "entanglement" survives for photons that never coexisted.

## What the paper does well

1. **The central physics is clean and correctly derived.** Master equation → quantum regression → Eq. (2)'s G⁽²⁾ is essentially exact for point dipoles in the far field. The key identity is transparent: for γ_A = γ_B the indistinguishability factor between the two emission histories is 1 regardless of *when* each photon was emitted, because the joint amplitude depends only on the sum of decay times. The visibility factorization in Eq. (3), V = e^{-(γ_A+γ_B)(t₁+t₂)}/e^{-2γ_A t₁ - 2γ_B t₂} → 1, is correct and the authors are honest about the equal-lifetime requirement.
2. **The conceptual punch is real.** "The ultimate constraint is indistinguishability of the two-photon amplitudes, not temporal overlap" is pushed to its logical endpoint: even the *order* of emission and detection is irrelevant. Fig. 2 is an effective way to sell the thought experiment.
3. **Practical framing exists.** Footnote 1 (trapped ions, 20 MHz D1 line, 8 ns lifetime, 3 m baseline → 10 ns delay) shows the delay condition is experimentally reasonable, and the far-field scheme needs no active path-length stabilization beyond the wavelength.
4. Short, legible, and the claim "path differences larger than the coherence lengths are permitted" is a genuinely useful relaxation — worth stressing that for equal lifetimes the path-difference factor *cancels identically*, which is the non-classical-looking part of the result.

## Criticisms

1. **The Bell-violation claim is thin for its strength.** Equation (6) is a CH74 inequality evaluated on a correlation g⁽²⁾ from *post-selected* events only: both detectors must click, *and* the delay must lie in a fixed window t₂ − t₁ > t_R. Both discards open the post-selection (and detection-efficiency) loopholes. A Bell test on a sub-ensemble is only a Bell test under fair-sampling assumptions, and local-hidden-variable models exploit exactly this kind of conditioning (the Massar-type no-go results, and the broader "post-selected Bell violation" debates). The paper never mentions this, and for a paper whose *title and abstract* advertise "violate Bell's inequalities," the caveat is a serious omission. Concretely, the authors should either bound the visibility of the violation against the post-selection discard rate, or present the violation as a CHSH test on the *conditional* two-photon state with the loophole stated explicitly.

2. **Missing dialogue with the "photons that never coexisted" literature.** The title's claim is not as novel as the introduction implies. The line of work on *entanglement of photons that have never coexisted* (Pittman et al., PRA **70**, 050301(R) (2004); Dowling et al. (2010), and the temporal-entanglement/HOM-without-simultaneity work) establishes precisely this phenomenon via *entanglement swapping*, where the ontological status of the "two-photon state" is carefully handled. This 2021 version reaches a similar conclusion by a different, direct mechanism (indistinguishable two-photon histories, no swapping), which is arguably conceptually cleaner — but the paper never cites, contrasts, or positions itself against that literature. As written, "entanglement of photons which do not overlap in time" is asserted without addressing *what system carries the entanglement* when the two photons never coexist as quantum systems at the same time slice. The state in Eq. (4) is a two-atomic superposition conditioned on one detection, not a two-photon state; the paper should say plainly that the "entangled photons" are a statement about joint detection records (a histories-type description), not about a state that exists anywhere in spacetime at one instant.

3. **A hidden assumption does load-bearing work.** Eqs. (2) and (3) assume t_{R_A} ≃ t_{R_B} (equal propagation times, "far field"). The attractive claim that path differences *exceeding the coherence lengths* are fine is then only half-told: for unequal decay constants the additional distinguishability factor e^{(γ_B−γ_A)(t_{R_A}−t_{R_B})} enters and no longer cancels. That the formula silently sets the path-time difference to zero is exactly the regime the paper wants to be liberal about, so the dependence should have been carried explicitly. As printed, Eq. (3) understates the role of the far-field equal-length assumption.

4. **The novelty claim is somewhat overreached in the introduction.** The authors themselves concede (correctly) that temporal overlap was already known not to be fundamental for HOM-type interference; the genuinely new content is only (i) V = 1 at arbitrarily large *detection* separations and (ii) the Bell consequence. A more honest positioning would be "extending the known indistinguishability principle to the extreme regime, with an explicit Bell-inequality witness." Also, the arXiv history (first posted 2011, published 2021) suggests long gestation; to the extent an earlier version of the same group already contained parts of this analysis, the incremental contribution of the published paper is further diminished.

5. **Experimental analysis is minimal.** One footnote's worth of feasibility. Missing: sensitivity of the result to source-lifetime mismatch beyond the exponential in Eq. (3) (for QD or PDC sources, detuning in the MHz–GHz range can kill the "100%" claim at 10 ns delays); multi-photon background and brightness/duty-cycle tradeoffs of real SPSs; and why this beats the straightforward entanglement-swapping route for producing never-coexisting entanglement. For a result aimed at quantum-network applications (independent nodes not needing sub-coherence-time synchronization), these are the questions a reader immediately asks.

6. **Minor.** Calling the state in Eq. (4) a "quantum memory" is a stretch — it is a path-entangled atomic excitation, not a memory. Only two-atom two-level emitters are treated; the advertised generality (ions, QDs, molecules) is asserted, not shown. The in-flight regime where the two wave packets *can* coexist in space (long lifetimes, moderate baseline) without the detections coexisting is not addressed, leaving "do not overlap in time" doing more work than any stated definition.

## Verdict

A sharp, clearly written thought-experiment paper whose core derivation is sound and whose central message — indistinguishability of two-photon amplitudes, not temporal coexistence, is what matters, all the way to detection-before-emission and a Bell witness — is a nice and pedagogically valuable sharpening of a known principle. Its main weaknesses are in *framing rather than physics*: the post-selected Bell violation is presented without the loophole caveats it requires, the never-coexisted-entanglement literature is unacknowledged, and one key assumption (equal propagation times) is silently absorbed into the headline formula. It is a solid short communication, not a field-shaping result.

## [1102.1490v3-novelty] Novelty of the contribution


# Paper: Wiegner, Thiel, von Zanthier & Agarwal, "Quantum interference and entanglement of photons which do not overlap in time" (JOSA B, 2011; arXiv:1102.1490)

## What the paper does (in my own words)

The authors examine a particular two-photon interferometer built from **two independent, uncorrelated single-photon sources (SPS)** — modeled as two two-level atoms that are excited randomly and decay spontaneously, so the *time of emission is fundamentally unpredictable*. Two far-field detectors each register exactly one photon, and the measurement is post-selected so that the emission time of one photon is later than the detection time of the others. They ask whether two-photon interference (and entanglement) can still be observed when, operationally, **one photon has already been recorded before the other is even emitted** — i.e., the two photons never coexist in time.

Their claim, in two parts:

1. **The temporal overlap is not a constraint.** Because detection is in the far field (path length and origin are erased to within the coherence length) and emission is probabilistic, the two two-photon amplitudes remain *indistinguishable* and interfere with 100% visibility — even when the detection-time separation far exceeds the single-photon coherence time $\tau_c = 1/\gamma$, and in a "scenario" (Fig. 2) where atom 1 scatters and is detected *before* atom 2 scatters at all. So the stated contribution is a *theoretical* result (with a schematic experimental proposal): interference and Bell entanglement do not require the two photons to exist simultaneously, because the relevant requirement is **indistinguishability of the two-photon amplitudes**, not temporal overlap.

**Not new (well established, cited and correctly attributed):**

**New / genuinely the contribution:**

The concrete new ingredients are (a) the clean **two-level-atom / Wigner–Weisskopf spontaneous-emission model** giving the analytic $G^{(2)}$ (Eq. 2/5) with visibility independent of detection delay once $\gamma_A=\gamma_B$; and (b) the explicit **quantum-memory picture** (Eq. 4) in which the first detection prepares a state that stores the which-source *phase* until the later photon scatters. **Not new / caveats:**

- The physical *claim* — that Bell entanglement/interference does not require simultaneous photon existence — is a reformulation of a known consequence of indistinguishability (already implicit in Franson-type entanglement-of-photon-pairs and in the delayed-choice / entanglement-swapping-style results in refs [9],[13]). What is genuinely new is the *model and the interpretation* (two-level atoms + quantum memory) and the concrete, quantitatively specified experimental prescription for CHSH violation at non-overlapping times. What is *not* new is the underlying physics claim and the indistinguishability mechanism, both firmly grounded in the cited Franson/HOM/independent-source work. It is a correct, pedagogically useful contribution rather than a breakthrough — solid but not field-shaping.

The paper's rhetorical structure takes a genuinely entangled *atomic* state and re‑labels it as "entanglement of photons," and that re‑labelling is precisely the unjustified step.

The paper repeatedly stresses that "the detection scheme is independent of the photon detection times" (§ left column, "no *which-way* information can be obtained"), but then the Bell violation is extracted **after conditioning on time-ordered events**. That is precisely the postselection/fair-sampling structure that the standard Bell-inequality objection attacks, and the paper never names or defends it. "No which-way information" is doing rhetorical work that "we discard the un-time-ordered detections" would have to do honestly.

**"entanglement among two photons may exist even though the two photons do not overlap in time."** (page 1, right column, and page 3 conclusion, "We have shown that such interferences can produce a visibility of 100% and violate Bell's inequalities in the CH74 formulation.") The word "demonstrate"/"shown" again. A single point violating a single polynomial (CH74) in an ideal model is a *witness consistent with* non-separability, not a "demonstration of entanglement." The authors don't even claim the CHSH form, which is what most readers would map "Bell" to; CH74 is a correlational Bell form specifically suited to lossy/detection-efficiency-limited scenarios, which conveniently papers over but **does not remove** the fair-sampling issue.

**The footnote on page 3** ("for trapped Ca⁺ ions … t₂ − t₁ > t_tr = 3 ns can be easily fulfilled") is the paper's most vulnerable concrete claim. Trapped ions are not spontaneous-emission SPSs in the sense used in the master-equation derivation — their emission is typically pulsed, their decay is dominated by the driving Rabi dynamics and trap noise, and the assumption ωA = ωB = ω with *identical* Γ to within the required precision is an extremely strong demand on two *independent* traps. The footnote treats the whole paper's idealization as if the hardware is already there. "Easily fulfilled" is doing a great deal of work.

**"This allows us to … appreciate the situation by investigating the specific atomic state produced after the measurement of the first photon"** (page 2, Eq. 4). The "quantum memory" framing (Ψ(r₁) storing a phase even though the second photon is not yet emitted) is a nice conceptual move but the paper never asks: what if, given real finite decoherence of the *memory* (state Ψ(r₁) lives for the whole t₂ − t₁ window, which the footnote says is ≳ transit time), Ψ(r₁) relaxes? For γ = 2π×20 MHz and t_tr = 3 ns, τc/τ ≈ 1/(2π·0.02), so 3 ns is ~1/4 of a coherence time — the memory already decays by a factor e^{−3ns/16ns} ≈ 0.83 during the required measurement window. The paper doesn't compute this.

## What an adversarial reviewer would say in paragraph one

"Interference of photons whose *emission events* are separated in time is not new: the temporal-overlap requirement has been relaxed in HOM experiments for over a decade (e.g., Kwiat et al. ref. 7, Pittman et al. ref. 8), and the indistinguishability criterion — which the authors themselves invoke as the "ultimate constraint" on page 1 — is well established as the true requirement. The present letter's only formal step beyond this is to take γA = γB exactly, to place the delay larger than the *detection-to-detection* transit time rather than the coherence time, and to feed the resulting idealized g⁽²⁾ into the CH74 polynomial. Every claim in this letter is an analytic limit at γA = γB, zero dephasing beyond radiative emission, zero loss, fair sampling, and perfect far-field projection. The authors never quantify (a) how much fractional mismatch in γA versus γB, detuning, or wavepacket length the visibility of 1 survives; (b) how the CH74 violation degrades under postselection of only time-ordered events, which is exactly the fair-sampling loophole the field expects a Bell-letter to confront by name; and (c) how the specific hardware they propose in their footnote (independent trapped-ion SPSs) can meet the idealization. As written, this is a *plausibility* argument, not a *demonstration*, and the abstract and the conclusion overstate it."

## Framing notes
The paper's core conceptual point is **right**: the relevant resource is source-which-way indistinguishability, not temporal overlap of emission events. The "quantum memory" (Eq. 4) is genuinely a clean way to see why the phase survives a time-ordered measurement. But the paper buries this point under the more dramatic phrasing "photons that do not overlap in time," which is not quite what the math says. The honest, defensible statement is: *a two-photon interference can be produced between photons whose emission times are separated such that the earlier photon is detected before the later one is emitted, provided the which-source information in the far field is erased — a regime distinct from prior HOM-with-time-delay experiments, and one whose CH74 correlation violates a Bell polynomial in the ideal limit.* That sentence is publishable and true; the version actually printed is several notches louder.

**Strengths**

- The core claim is not really new physics. "Two-photon interference requires only amplitude indistinguishability, not temporal overlap" is the standard interpretation of HOM, Franson (1989), Pittman–Strekalov–Shibata–Sergienko–Kwiat (1991), Rarity–Tapster (1990), and is implicit in essentially every entangled-photon Bell test where the two photons travel different path lengths. The present paper's novelty is a particular two-independent-SPS model plus a post-selection rule, not the conceptual point. The title oversells this.
- The Bell-CH74 form used is unusual (a four-setting coincidence version). The paper shows a violation but doesn't quantify how robust it is to detector inefficiency, timing jitter, linewidth mismatch (γ_A ≠ γ_B), or dephasing. The feasibility footnote is a single point estimate, not a robustness study.
- No comparison with any existing experiment. Franson-type interferometers have already produced Bell violations with non-coincident photons; the paper doesn't say why *this* particular two-SPS geometry adds anything.
- Eq. (1) is asserted from the master equation + QRT without showing the step, and Eq. (4) is given as "the state after the first detector clicks" without deriving the partial trace over the not-yet-decayed alternative. For a 3-page letter that is acceptable, but it leaves the argument more rhetorical than rigorous.
- The accepted-event rate after post-selection is never estimated. Without it, the "feasibility" claim is qualitative only.

### Recommendation

**Borderline (lean accept)** for a short conceptual letter venue such as JOSA B / Optics Letters. The physics is correct and the framing is elegant, but the conceptual content is largely a re-package of well-known indistinguishability arguments into a specific model. For a flagship journal (PRL, PRA) the novelty would not clear the bar.

**Score: 6 / 10**

### Three changes that would most improve the paper

1. **Add a post-selection rate estimate.** Compute the coincidence rate as a function of the timing window (t₂ − t₁) > t_R and the SPS decay γ, and propagate it through the Bell inequality to show the violation is observable at a sensible count rate with realistic sources. This turns the feasibility footnote into an actual feasibility result.

2. **Quantify robustness of the Bell violation.** Run the CHSH/CH74 expression through realistic imperfections: detector efficiency η, timing jitter Δt, linewidth mismatch γ_A ≠ γ_B, and dephasing of the quantum-memory state between clicks. (b) Derive Eq. (1) explicitly from the QRT (or at least show the one non-trivial commutator), and obtain Eq. (4) by an explicit partial trace over the not-yet-decayed atom rather than by assertion. ## Peer review

**Title:** *Quantum interference and entanglement of photons which do not overlap in time*
**Authors:** R. Wiegner, C. Thiel, J. von Zanthier, G. S. Agarwal
**Context:** arXiv:1102.1490v3, 3-page journal letter (Opt. Express / OSOC format)

### 1. Summary of the paper

The authors consider two independent single-photon emitters (two-level atoms A and B at widely separated positions, far-field detection) and ask whether two-photon interference and entanglement can be observed when the two photons are *not* simultaneous — indeed, in the limiting case of Fig. 2, when the first photon is scattered and recorded **before** the second atom has even emitted its photon, so that the detection-time separation $t_2 - t_1$ exceeds the photon transit time $t_{tr}$.

The main claims are:
1. With identical sources ($\gamma_A = \gamma_B = \gamma$), the two-photon correlation visibility reaches $V_\gamma = 1$ and becomes *independent* of the detection times (Eq. 3).
2. The far-field, which-path-erasing geometry preserves indistinguishability of the two-photon amplitudes "at any time", allowing interference even in the non-overlap regime.
3. The post-selected joint state (Eq. 4), $|\Psi(\mathbf r_1)\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|ge\rangle + e^{i\varphi(r_1)}|eg\rangle)$, is an entangled state, and the $G^{(2)}$ function of Eq. (5) can violate a Clauser–Horne–74 Bell inequality (Eq. 6).
4. A footnote demonstrates feasibility with trapped $2\,\mathrm{Ca^+}$ ions ($20\,\mathrm{MHz}$ D1 linewidth, $8\,\mathrm{ns}$ lifetime, $1\,\mathrm{m}$ source–detector distance giving $t_{tr}\approx 3\,\mathrm{ns}$).

### 2. Strengths

- **The central conceptual point is genuinely striking and is clearly stated.** Showing that $G^{(2)}$ retains $100\%$ visibility — and can violate CH74 — when $t_2 - t_1 > t_{tr}$, i.e. one photon is recorded before the other is emitted, is the paper's real contribution and is worth writing down. It sharpens the operational meaning of "indistinguishability": it is the joint two-photon amplitude, not the temporal coexistence of the photons, that must not carry which-source information.
- The reduction to $V_\gamma = 1$ for equal decay constants is transparent and correct in the idealized (perfect indistinguishability) limit.
- The concrete footnote with realistic ion parameters gives the result physical teeth and makes it a plausible experimental proposal rather than a purely formal exercise.
- The post-selected state (Eq. 4) cleanly shows *where* the phase and the entanglement live.

### 3. Weaknesses

- **The "entanglement of photons" framing is loose, and this is the paper's most important defect.** The explicitly entangled object the authors write down (Eq. 4) is a *two-atom* state ($|ge\rangle$ vs $|eg\rangle$), not a two-photon state — and in the Fig. 2 scenario the second photon has not yet been emitted, so there is in that instant no pair of photons to be entangled. The paper's title promises two-photon entanglement but delivers (i) atom–atom entanglement after post-selection and (ii) a $G^{(2)}$ correlation function used as the input to a Bell test. The link between "the photons are entangled" and "the Bell inequality built from $G^{(2)}$ is violated" is asserted, not demonstrated. A careful reader will ask: *which bipartite system does the CH74 inequality certify?* For a paper whose whole point is that the photons never coexist, the identity of the "shared system" is not a detail — it is the crux — and it needs explicit treatment.
- **The CH74 argument (Eqs. 5–6) is underdeveloped.** The inequality is written by substitution with a specific detuning/angle choice ($(t,\pi/4,\pi/2)$), but the derivation of the four $g^{(2)}$ terms, the role of the fixed $t_0$, and why the $e^{-2\gamma(t_{10}+t_{20})}$ terms cancel are elided. As it stands the violation is more of a claim than a derivation. This needs to be fleshed out or reduced to an appendix with full steps.
- **The $G^{(2)}$ derivation (master equation → quantum regression → Eq. 2) skips algebra.** In particular the passage from the positive-frequency field decomposition (Eq. 1) to the explicit $G^{(2)}$ form, and the limit $t_{R2}\to t_{R1}$ taken to extract $V_\gamma$, are stated without enough of the intermediate steps for an independent verification.
- **The $V=100\%$ result is an idealized upper bound that the paper understates.** It simultaneously requires (a) identical decay constants, (b) identical transition frequency, (c) identical polarization/spatial mode, and (d) perfect which-path erasure in the far field. Any fractional distinguishability in the frequency/polarization DOF directly reduces the visibility. This deserves one sentence: the $V=100\%$ claim is strictly in the limit of perfectly indistinguishable sources and should be flagged as such.
- **Relation to prior art is thin.** The physics — that two-photon interference is governed by the joint amplitude and survives when single-photon (first-order) coherence is lost, as well as the "induced coherence / virtual photon" literature and Mandel's earlier two-source work — is the natural home of this result. The introduction cites HOM and two-source HOM, but I would expect a sharper statement of *what is new* precisely: I read it as (i) the explicit $t_2-t_1 > t_{tr}$ regime and (ii) the associated CH74 form. That novelty is real and should be isolated more crisply against what is already known as "two-photon interference without first-order coherence."
- **No experimental plan / error model.** A 3-page letter may not demand a full experiment, but given the headline ("can violate Bell's inequalities… even though the two photons do not overlap in time"), at least a brief discussion of the practical visibility budget and the main decoherence channel would strengthen the conclusion.

### 4. Minor points

- Fig. 1/2 notation $t_i$ vs $t_{\bar i}=t_i-t_{tR}$ vs $t_{tR}$ is introduced tersely; a short symbol table would help.
- The sentence "the visibility … becomes independent of the detection times" (around Eq. 3) would be clearer if it explicitly said this holds only *within the far-field, $\gamma_A=\gamma_B$ assumptions*.
- The final paragraph ("trapped ions, quantum dots or single molecules") is a useful closing and reads well.

### 5. Verdict

This is a clean, correct-in-principle theoretical letter with a genuinely surprising and well-formulated conceptual result: the persistence of maximal two-photon interference (and a CH74 violation) into the regime where the two photons do not temporally overlap and one is recorded before the other is emitted. The core quantum-optics reasoning (indistinguishability of the joint two-photon amplitude under far-field which-path erasure) is sound.

However, for a *prestigious* journal the paper is not yet at the bar: the "entanglement of photons" title overstates what is actually shown (the only explicitly entangled state is the post-selected two-atom state), the CH74 section is asserted rather than derived, and several key steps in the $G^{(2)}$ calculation are elided. The result is a valuable conceptual sharpening of known two-source/indistinguishability physics extended to a new (delay > transit time) regime, rather than a wholly new physical effect. I recommend **major revision**: reframe the entanglement claims to match the actual objects, complete the CH74 derivation, and tighten the statement of assumptions under which $V=100\%$ holds.

- **Originality:** the non-overlap / delay-exceeds-transit-time regime and its Bell formulation are a meaningful, well-stated novelty built on established indistinguishability physics. **3/5.**
- **Overall quality/soundness:** correct in principle, clear concept, but underdeveloped derivations, a loose "photon entanglement" framing, and an asserted (not derived) Bell violation. **3/5.**
- **Impact:** philosophically interesting (what "coexist in time" means for entanglement and Bell tests) with a plausible experimental readout, but a refinement of known physics rather than field-redefining. **3/5.**

This paper (Wiegner, Thiel, von Zanthier, Agarwal — arXiv:1102.1490, the JOSA B version of it) has a genuinely interesting central idea, but in its current form it tells a story it does not fully earn. Let me go through it the way I'd go through it in your office hours.

## What's good

- **The right question.** The core observation — that temporal overlap of photons is *not* a resource, and the only requirement for two-photon (second-order) interference is indistinguishability of the two-photon *amplitudes* — is the conceptually important point of the paper, and you identify it correctly. The clean far-field construction (two atoms, each ending in |g⟩ in *both* "path" alternatives, so no which-way marker can ever accumulate, even after one photon is already detected) is elegant, and it's the reason the indistinguishability survives arbitrary time delays. That's the heart of the mechanism, and it's right.
- For γ_A = γ_B the conclusion does hold in the ideal model: the which-way amplitudes and the cross term of G⁽²⁾ decay with the *same* law, so the modulation depth (visibility) stays 100% at arbitrary detection delay, including the "photon 2 doesn't exist yet" regime. That is a nice, non-trivial result, and it distinguishes this work from Franson-type interferometry (common source, simultaneous emission, equal path lengths) — a contrast you should make explicit, not leave implicit.
- The Ca⁺ footnote numbers (γ = 20 MHz, t_R = 3 ns ≪ 8 ns lifetime) show you thought about experiment.

## The derivation is not actually consistent as written

This is the first thing a referee will attack, and it's fixable in an afternoon, but you must fix it:

1. **The regression formula contradicts your own master equation.** You write the Lindblad equation with decay rate γ, from which S⁻(t) ∝ e^{−(iω+γ)t/2}. Hence ⟨S⁻(t_j)S⁺(t_i)⟩ ∝ e^{iω(t_i−t_j)/2 − γ(t_j+t_i)/2} — *half* gammas, half-ω. You then state e^{iω(t_j−t_i)−γ(t_j+t_i)} (and with the ω sign flipped). Every rate in your Eq. (2) is correspondingly doubled, and — worse — **Eq. (3) as printed does not follow from Eq. (2) as printed**: for equal γ the cross term / direct-term ratio in your equations is > 1 for large delays, i.e. visibility > 100%. Redo it with one consistent convention.
2. Do the unequal-γ case properly. With the correct rates you get a very clean result you *should* have written down:

   V(t₁,t₂) = sech[(γ_A−γ_B)(t₁−t₂)/2]

   which is 1 at coincidence, decays with delay for disparate emitters, and is exactly 1 for all delays when γ_A = γ_B. That one formula states the whole physics; your Eqs. (2)–(3) obscure it.
3. **"The exact functional dependence of the decay process is of no importance" is false.** The time-independent-visibility claim requires ⟨S⁺S⁻⟩(t) to be a pure exponential (the decay *tail* of the excitation). During the rising part after a π-pulse it isn't, and then the visibility does depend on detection time. Specify the excitation (e.g. short resonant pulse, then wait several lifetimes) and derive the p(t) = p₀e^{−γt} regime explicitly.
4. **Mislabeling.** You call t̃ᵢ "the emission time." It isn't — emission is at a random time; t̃ᵢ = tᵢ − t_Rᵢ is the detection time referred to arrival. What actually makes the answer independent of the decay time is that the *integration over the random emission time* is done for you by the Weisskopf solution: the detection amplitude at tᵢ is a pure exponential in t̃ᵢ. That is genuinely the key insight of the paper, and it deserves a dedicated paragraph, not a passing definition.

## The conceptual overreach — the biggest problem

**"Entanglement of photons which do not overlap in time" is not a well-defined statement, and your own geometry exposes why.** You want the two *detection* events spacelike-separated for the Bell argument, which (opposite-side geometry, detector separation ≈ 2t_R c) requires t₂−t₁ < 2t_R. But "photon 2 is created after photon 1 was recorded" is only Lorentz-invariant if t₂−t₁ > 2t_R. The two desirable properties are *incompatible* — in your working regime (t_R < Δt < 2t_R) the dramatic ordering in the title is **frame-dependent**: in some inertial frames photon 2 is created before photon 1 is detected. You must say "in the source rest frame," or better, restate the claim invariantly: *correlations between two spacelike-separated detection events violate CH74, and in the source frame one of those detections occurs before the other photon's emission.* That paragraph is missing, and it's the difference between a crisp paper and a sloppy one.

Second: **which system is entangled?** The honest answer to "are the photons entangled?" is uncomfortable: on the photonic time-bin modes the post-selected state is just |1⟩⟨1⟩ at the two modes — a product. The genuinely entangled object is the *atomic pair*, left in (|ge⟩+e^{iφ}|eg⟩)/√2 after the first detection — your Eq. (4). The two-photon correlation is best described as a *sequential, two-time-bin readout of entanglement between the emitters*. Write that down. It simultaneously (a) defuses the "isn't this just first-order interference?" objection and (b) forces you to drop the "quantum memory" language, which overstates a post-selected ensemble-conditional state as if it were a usable stored resource. "Entanglement among two photons" is defensible only in the mode/entanglement-swapping sense of *temporal modes*, and the paper never commits to a Hilbert-space definition of "the two photons" that don't coexist.
