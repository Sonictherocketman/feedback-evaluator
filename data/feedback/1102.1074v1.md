## Review: "Multilevel Analysis of Oscillation Motions in Active Regions of the Sun" (Abramov‑Maximov, Gelfreikh, Kabanov, Shibasaki, Chupin; *Sol. Phys.*, 2011; arXiv:1102.1074v1)

### Summary
The authors combine ground‑based optical LOS‑velocity measurements (Sayana SO: photospheric Fe I λ6569 and chromospheric Hα) with Nobeyama 1.76 cm (17 GHz) radioheliograph data of the same active region (NOAA 10661, Aug 2004), and cross‑compare the two via wavelet analysis. The headline findings are (i) the three‑minute oscillation in the Hα LOS velocity and in the radio source are similar in frequency and amplitude modulation, with the radio lagging the optical by ~40–50 s, which they interpret as an MHD wave propagating from the chromosphere up the umbral magnetic tube (giving a chromosphere–corona transition region height of ~2700 km); (ii) five‑minute oscillations are more similar for the center/disk‑facing train; and (iii) longer 8, 15, and ~30–32 min modes are detected, with the 15‑min mode suspected to be a harmonic of the 32‑min mode.

### What the paper does well
- **A worthwhile observational combination.** Simultaneous optical + NOBEYAMA‑class microwave observation of the *same* sunspot, analysed consistently with the standard Torrence & Compo wavelet package (6th‑order Morlet, COI masked, 95% significance), is a clean demonstration of the multi‑level cross‑analysis approach.
- **Correct, quantitative field determination.** The B ≈ 2020 G for the radio layer follows cleanly from the 3rd‑harmonic gyroresonance at 17 GHz (8.4 B MHz = 17 GHz → B ≈ 2024 G), a nice concrete anchor for identifying the emission height.
- **Honest about its own limitations.** The authors candidly flag the short records (40–60 min), the instability of the wave trains, and the strong dependence of the radio–optical match on the AR's position on the disk (with a plausible, if qualitative, geometric/LOS explanation).
- Clear figures; reproducible method.

### The main weaknesses

**1. The 50 s delay → "MHD wave propagation" → 2700 km is the weakest and most over‑claimed result.** The cross‑correlation feeding the 45–50 s shift (Fig 6d) is computed between the *wavelet‑power envelopes* (power averaged over 6.0–6.5 mHz), i.e. the modulation/envelope of the wave packets, not the underlying oscillation phases. A peak in a cross‑correlation of two periodic signals' envelopes measures an *envelope/group* lag, and one in a phase‑domain cross‑analysis measures a *phase* lag; neither is automatically the one‑way physical travel time of a pulse generated at one height. Presenting this single number as the "time of propagation of the MHD wave from the chromosphere to the corona" and then dividing by it to get a tube height (~2700 km) stacks an interpretation (single upward‑propagating source at the photosphere) that the data do not require and do not verify. The paper needs to (a) state exactly what quantity the correlation measures, (b) rule out the alternatives (independent sources, different filtering at the two levels, source offset, resonance phase), and (c) give an error bar on the lag rather than a bare 40–50 s.

**2. The inferred 60 km s⁻¹ phase speed is in tension with the stated 2000 G field, and the paper never confronts this.** They take the 45 s over ~2700 km to be ≈60 km s⁻¹ and note it "is equal to" the chromospheric propagation speed (~60 km s⁻¹). But in a 2000 G field the Alfvén speed is c_A = B/√(μ₀ρ) — for any reasonable coronal/CCTR density this is hundreds to thousands of km s⁻¹, i.e. a fast‑mode speed of order c_A (≫ 60 km s⁻¹). A *slow* (acoustic‑gravity/filtered) mode can be slower, but a generic "MHD wave" moving at 60 km s⁻¹ through a 2000 G flux tube is physically a *specific* mode (essentially acoustic/evanescent‑filtered), and identifying it as a propagating MHD mode without computing the expected fast/slow phase speed from the field and sound speed is a real gap. This is the one place the physics does the reasoning for the measurement rather than checking it.

**3. The "similarity" is asserted, never metricised.** The central claim — radio and optical spectra "agree well," "are similar," "consequently this is certainly not a noise" (their words) — rests entirely on visual inspection of the wavelet scalograms (Figs 1–6). No wavelet‑coherence number, no correlation coefficient with its significance, and no quantitative goodness‑of‑match is ever reported. The wavelet coherence (Fig 5b) is shown but not quantified. A single reported coherence value (and its confidence) per oscillation train would turn the paper's main argument from "looks the same" into a testable statement and would immediately show how strong the agreement really is.

**4. Statistical power is essentially one sunspot, a handful of trains.** Everything — the disk‑position dependence, the 5/8/15/32‑min mode inventory, the harmonic hypothesis — rests on one AR, three ~40–60 min optical sessions and ~42 min of overlap. With three 3‑min trains, a scattering of 5‑min trains, and a 32‑min signal that shows only ~2 periods in 42 min (Fig 9), the "conclusion that the 16‑min mode is the harmonic of the 30–32‑min mode" (frequency ratio explicitly "not exactly 2, about 1.9") is speculative. The generalisations in the conclusion ("this work illustrates the recent progress in a new approach…") substantially outstrip the n≈1 evidence.

**5. Minor but telling details.**
- "95% confidence level (2σ)" conflates a percentile with a sigma; the T&C 95% level is a percentile of the null, not 2σ.
- The long‑period "oscillations" that appear at ~8–15 min in the radio (Fig 8) are, per the authors, "essentially non‑stationary with minor variations" and "of low coherence" over the single overlap — calling them identified oscillation modes is a step beyond the data.
- The geometric explanation for the disk‑position dependence (central tube vs. ring/horseshoe emission, LOS projection) is a reasonable post‑hoc rationalisation but is asserted, not examined (e.g., against a quantitative LOS/visibility model).

### Bottom line
This is a competent, well‑executed *demonstration* of a genuinely useful method (simultaneous optical + microwave wavelet cross‑analysis of the same sunspot) and a solid addition to the QPO data set. But it is primarily descriptive, its two most quotable numbers (the 50 s delay and the resulting 2700 km height / 60 km s⁻¹ "MHD wave") are under‑supported by the analysis actually performed and by the physics of the field, and the conclusions lean on one active region. The most impactful fixes would be: (i) quantify the radio–optical similarity with wavelet coherence and state precisely what the delay measures; (ii) compute the expected fast/slow MHD phase speeds for B ≈ 2000 G and reconcile them with the ~60 km s⁻¹; and (iii) either gather additional ARs/time to make the mode inventory and harmonic claim statistically meaningful, or scale the claims to "one illustrative AR."

It is a solid, mid‑tier observational study: sound technique, honest limitations, but a modest novelty footprint and a couple of over‑interpreted highlights that pull it short of being a strong result.

## [1102.1074v1-expert-collegue-review] Expert Collegue Review


# Review of "Multilevel Analysis of Oscillation Motions in Active Regions of the Sun"
(Abramov-Maximov, Gelfreikh, Kobanov, Shibasaki, Chupin; Solar Physics, arXiv:1102.1074)

## Summary

The authors compare wavelet spectra of 3–5 minute (and longer-period) sunspot oscillations measured simultaneously at two atmospheric levels: Hα line-of-sight (LOS) velocities at the Sayan Solar Observatory (chromosphere) and 17 GHz (1.76 cm) gyroresonance radio emission at the Nobeyama Radioheliograph, which probes the B ≈ 2000 G chromosphere–corona transition region (CCTR). For AR 10661 ("AR661") observed on 15, 16, and 18 August 2004, optical and radio wavelet spectra agree well only on 18 August, when the region was near disk center. A cross-correlation of wavelet power curves gives a 40–50 s radio lag relative to the optical signal, interpreted as an upward-propagating MHD wave, from which a CCTR height of ~2700 km is inferred (using 60 km/s, the horizontal umbral phase speed). Longer 8–16 min and 30–32 min oscillations are also reported, with the 16-min mode proposed as a harmonic of the 30–32-min mode.

## Overall impression

This is a competent, honest observational study whose main virtue is the *data combination*: simultaneous Hα Doppler and gyroresonance radio of the same magnetic tube, with an explicit geometric (disk-center) explanation for when the two views ought to agree. However, the central quantitative claim — the 45 s delay and the derived 2700 km CCTR height — rests on a chain of assumptions that the paper should examine more critically, and several interpretive leaps outrun the data.

## Strengths

1. **Genuinely new observational pairing.** To my knowledge this is indeed the first comparison of chromospheric velocity oscillations with co-spatial 17 GHz sunspot sources on the same timescale. The gyroresonance channel is uniquely valuable because the source height is anchored by the magnetic field (B ≈ 2000 G), unlike EUV lines.
2. **The disk-center effect argument is physically sensible.** LOS velocity measures vertical motions only when the tube is pointed at the observer; the ring/horseshoe geometry of the radio source explains why the central umbra is invisible in radio at disk center. This framing (Section 4, Discussion) is the paper's most sound intellectual contribution.
3. **Good honesty about limitations.** The authors repeatedly flag low coherence, short durations, and the need for longer optical campaigns and modeling. The cross-wavelet/coherency plots (Fig. 5) are the appropriate tools for such unstable, low-Q oscillations.
4. Wave-train consistency between the two bands, on the one day they should agree, is a reasonably convincing demonstration that the oscillations are physical and shared by the magnetic tube, not instrumental.

## Major concerns

1. **The 40–50 s delay is derived from cross-correlating *power curves*, not time signals.** Cross-correlating frequency-averaged wavelet power envelopes (Fig. 6c–d, following Bloomfield et al. 2004) is an indirect measure: two trains can share a frequency band yet have misaligned envelopes, and the envelope lag is not the same as the phase/wavefront propagation lag one needs for a height inference. The standard tools — the phase arrows of the cross-wavelet (Fig. 5a apparently shows the magnitude only) or a cross-correlation of the band-pass filtered (6.0–6.5 mHz) raw signals — should be used. No error estimate is given: over a 42-minute segment the width of the correlation peak should define the uncertainty in the lag, and the "40–50 s" looks like the peak's visual extent rather than a measured value. The correlation is computed on a visually selected "fragment" (dashed lines in Fig. 6); the authors should show the lag is stable when the fragment is varied or the full 42 minutes are used.

2. **The velocity used to convert delay into height is the wrong kind of velocity.** The 60 km/s is the *horizontal* phase speed of the umbral 3-min structures (Kobanov et al. 2006), while the delay between chromospheric Hα and CCTR radio measures *vertical/along-tube* propagation. Along an 2000 G tube the relevant speed is closer to the Alfvén speed (hundreds of km/s in the lower corona); the paper concedes "the actual velocity may be even higher" but then presents 2700 km as *the* CCTR height estimate in the abstract. It should be presented explicitly as a lower bound with an order-of-magnitude range, alongside a simple model check: at what height does B = 2000 G in a super-equipartition umbra, and above or below the Hα line-formation height is it? That check (which could be done with a published tube model, e.g., an extension of the Kobanov & Makarchik setup) would either support or undercut the interpretation and is currently missing.

3. **Inconsistent geometry between the two measurements.** The radio signal is the sum of emission over a ring/horseshoe around the umbra while the optical signal is a 1-D slit cut. The authors acknowledge this in the Discussion as a possible source of spectral differences — but it equally affects the *timing* of the averaged signals: the ring-averaged radio signal is a superposition of oscillations with different local delays. A delay measured on such an averaged quantity carries a systematic spread that should at least be estimated from the radio map geometry (the maps are there; a simple per-source-position analysis would localize the emission and its delay).

4. **The 5-min delay (~2–3 min) is not reconciled.** If the same wave population propagates to the same CCTR, the 5-min oscillations should show a comparable delay. A ~3 min lag implies ~10,000 km at 60 km/s — roughly four times the height inferred for the 3-min oscillations. The abstract's "the time delays seem to be similar" understates a factor of six discrepancy that needs a physical discussion (different source regions, different filtering depths, damping, or simply that the 5-min delay is poorly determined from one train).

5. **Small, confounded sample.** One active region, one favorable day. The disk-center day (15 Aug: E 58°, 16 Aug: E 46°, 18 Aug: E 20°) is also the day with the longest, best optical series near midnight local time — quality effects and geometry effects are entangled. The "similarity depends on disk position" claim would need at least a couple more regions at varied positions to be stated beyond this AR.

## Minor concerns

- **The 16-min harmonic claim is weak.** A frequency ratio of ~1.9 and "similar variations" over a 42-minute overlap (≈ 1.3 periods of the 32-min mode) is not evidence for a harmonic relationship; common amplitude modulation would produce the same visual similarity. The authors hedge, but the word "harmonic" appears in the abstract-level conclusion and deserves stronger justification or removal.
- **The in-phase intensity–Doppler 8-min oscillations.** The authors note the in-phase velocity–intensity pair "in contrast to the quarter-period phase lag for acoustic oscillations." This actually points to longitudinally propagating (slow-mode) motion, where velocity and density perturbations are in phase — a physically informative statement that is not drawn. A sentence of interpretation would turn this observation into a result.
- **Figure 3 caption** mislabels the middle panels ("(d)... and (d)"); should be (c) and (d).
- The title says "Active Regions" while the study is a single sunspot; the low-frequency radio analysis (several hours) covers the whole day and is not tied one-to-one to the optical interval — the paper could be cleaner about which conclusions rest on the simultaneous (42-min) overlap versus the longer radio series.
- It would help to state the Hα line-formation height explicitly and compare it to the inferred CCTR height; as written, the reader must assume the delay is "chromosphere to corona" without knowing how much of the 2700 km lies between the two formation heights.
- No significance testing of the cross-correlation peak (e.g., Monte-Carlo under noise) is given.

## Recommendation

The paper reports a real, previously unavailable multi-wavelength measurement and a plausible physical story, and it is honest about its limits — but its headline number (2700 km) is less robust than the abstract implies, and the paper would be stronger if it (i) re-derived the lag from the filtered time series (or cross-wavelet phase) with an explicit uncertainty, (ii) presented the height as a lower bound with a range over plausible propagation speeds, (iii) reconciled the 3-min vs 5-min delays, and (iv) located the B = 2000 G level relative to the Hα formation level using a simple tube model. With those additions this becomes a solid first step in a genuinely promising multi-wavelength seismology program; as it stands, I consider it a worthwhile but modest contribution whose quantitative centerpiece is currently under-determined.

**Suggested decision:** Minor-to-majors revision (accept in principle, subject to the four points in "Major concerns").

The manuscript is publishable **after a dedicated English-language edit pass and a moderate trim** (mainly deduplicating the Discussion/Conclusion against §4). # Critique: "Multilevel Analysis of Oscillation Motions in Active Regions of the Sun"

**Authors:** Abramov‑Maximov, Gelfreich, Kobanov, Shibasaki, Chupin
**Source:** arXiv:1102.1074v1 → *Solar Physics* (2011)

## What the paper does

Single active region (NOAA 10661, 15/16/18 Aug 2004), observed simultaneously in two regimes: chromospheric line‑of‑sight (LOS) velocity in Hα from the 640 mm horizontal telescope at Sayan, and 1.76 cm (17 GHz) gyrorosonance radio maps from the Nobeyama Radioheliograph (upper corona / C–CCIR, B ≈ 2000 G). Both time series are decomposed with 6th‑order Morlet wavelets (95% confidence as the reliability criterion); a cross‑wavelet / wavelet‑coherence analysis and a Bloomfield‑et‑al. (2004) cross‑correlation are then used to compare the optical (lower) and radio (upper) levels. The headline finding is that the radio oscillations lag the optical by ~45–50 s for the three‑minute mode, interpreted as an upward‑propagating MHD wave, giving a C–CCIR height estimate of ≈2700 km; 5‑min, 8‑min and 15‑/32‑min modes are also reported.

This is a real, well‑motivated observational program, and the *combination* — the same oscillations measured at two genuinely different heights simultaneously — is the genuinely new element. The rest is a careful, conservative execution of standard tools (Torneille & Compo wavelets, cross‑wavelet transform).

## What it gets right

- The premise is sound: to untangle *which* oscillation mode is seen where in the atmosphere you need coincident multi‑level data, and direct chromosphere–corona pairs are rare. The 15+ year Nobeyama 1.76 cm record is a real and underused asset that the authors are justified in foregrounding.
- Using wavelet **coherence** to quantify the optical–radio relation, rather than eyeballing matching wavelet power (Fig. 4), is the right choice and mostly delivers on it.
- The authors are unusually honest about the geometry: 1D slit LOS velocity in the optical versus a 2D/summed‑along‑ring radio source, and the disk‑center dependence of the agreement. They spend a real paragraph (Sec. 6) explaining why the spectra differ by position. That candor is a strength.

## Main problems

**1. The central quantitative result rests on one segment.** The 40–50 s delay → 2700 km height chain is built on the 2004 Aug 18 run, the only day the AR was near disk center and the spectra actually agreed (the authors are explicit that the other two days do *not* show the similarity). That is essentially a single cross‑correlation of a single ~42‑minute segment, used to quote a height to three significant figures. The paper reads its own caveats ("our sets of observations lasted 40–60 minutes … limited possibilities") but then the abstract presents "a MHD wave traveling upward … implies MHD" as a firm inference. It is a plausible pilot result, not a measurement.

**2. The height is over‑interpreted.** h = v·t ≈ 2700 km uses a **horizontal** phase velocity of ~60 km s⁻¹ (a sunspot‑umbra value from Kobanov et al. 2006) applied to a *vertical* propagation time. The vertical group/phase speed of the mode up the tube is not the horizontal umbral phase speed and depends on height, field, and fast/slow branch. The resulting 2700 km is an order‑of‑magnitude estimate; presenting it as "the height of the C–CCIR is approximately 2700 km" oversells it. (Minor internal slip too: 45 s × 60 km s⁻¹ = 2700 km, 50 s = 3000 km; the abstract's "about 50 seconds" and the text's "45 sec → 2700 km" are not the same number.)

**3. Directionality is inferred, not shown.** A cross‑correlation peak gives a lag, not a causal direction; the "wave travels *upward*" conclusion leans entirely on the prior that 17 GHz emission is higher than Hα. It would at least be worth ruling out reflection / shared driver / instrumental offsets, and stating plainly that the coherence phase alone cannot fix direction.

**4. Figure 6d and the text disagree on the lag.** The stated delay is 40–50 s, but the cross‑correlation curve in Fig. 6d has its peak sitting visibly closer to ~100 s on its own axis. This should be reconciled (or the figure relabelled) because the whole height argument hangs on the exact lag.

**5. The long‑period "oscillations" (8, 15/32 min) are marginal.** At 42‑min baselines a 32‑min mode is < two cycles and sits at the bottom of the wavelet plane, dominated by COI; the authors themselves call it "low coherency" and concede the optical side "does not allow us to study the 32‑minute wave," so that mode rests on radio alone (Fig. 8). The "16 min is the harmonic of 32 min" claim is thin: the frequency ratio is 1.9, which is *within* uncertainty of both 2.0 (harmonic) and independent origin — yet the same paragraph notes "not exactly equal to 2" as if it argued against, then concludes harmonic. The logic is muddled, and "testing many periods at many positions" without a multiple‑comparisons correction inflates these detections.

**6. "3D structure" in the conclusion is overstated.** The data are a 1D slit LOS velocity plus a 2D radio brightness map. Saying this "results in new estimates of the 3D structures and dynamics of the oscillations in a sunspot" is a step beyond what a single‑slit, single‑component velocity measurement supports.

**7. Small / non‑independent sample.** One AR, three days, of which one is the only usable day. No error bars on the delay, no significance test on the coherence magnitude, and no simple 1D propagation/reflecting‑wave model even as an order‑of‑magnitude check. These are the three things that would most convert the pilot into a result.

## Verdict

A coherent, well‑motivated, and *honest* pilot study that opens a genuinely useful multi‑level (chromosphere + radio corona) program, and which is the first simultaneous optical–radio comparison of these modes. The methods (Morlet wavelets, cross‑wavelet, coherence) are appropriate and correctly applied. But the single standout quantitative claim — upward propagation, ~50 s lag, 2700 km height — is built on one short baseline, uses a velocity of the wrong geometric component, and is contradicted somewhat by its own Fig. 6d; the long‑period modes are statistically marginal; and the conclusion overstates what 1D+2D data can claim. It is a solid and interesting "here is a new approach and one encouraging case" paper, not yet a measurement that will change the community's numbers on C–CCIR height or oscillation mode identity.

**Bottom line:** valuable as a direction‑setting, proof‑of‑concept contribution; the specific physical inference needs a longer simultaneous baseline, coherence significance, a component‑correct speed, and the Fig. 6d / text lag discrepancy fixed before it can be treated as a firm result.

6. **Sample size and stationarity.** Each time series is 40–60 min, so each identified 3-minute train contains only a handful of periods; QPOs are explicitly unstable in both frequency and amplitude on those timescales. 7. **"2σ = 95 %"** conflation of Gaussian tails with the χ²-based white-noise background used in the actual COI convention is stated loosely and never operationalized.
8. **Radiative transfer ignored in both directions.** The Hα LOS oscillation is taken face value as a plasma motion (Hα is formed in a broad range of heights); the radio "brightness temperature oscillation" is taken face value as a plasma temperature motion in the CCTR, with no transfer-function or filling-factor/gradient correction. Equating these two proxies with "the same wave observed at two levels" is an assumption.
9. **Disk-center selection bias.** The methodology's success criterion (matching spectra) is known in advance to depend on heliographic position, and the single disk-center day carries the headline result (the 50 s delay, the height estimate). The other two days are shown to fail the comparison, and the paper treats "the AR was not at disk center" as a complete explanation.
10. **Harmonic identification of low-frequency modes.** The 15–16 min mode is called a "harmonic" of the 30–32 min mode because the frequency ratio is 1.9 and amplitude/period *variations* look similar — asserted on pattern similarity, with no phase-lock or commensurability test over the limited overlapping baseline (only 42 min of simultaneous optical–radio data for these periods).
11. **Direction of the wave train.** Wave-train inclination in space–time diagrams is equated with a propagating wave without checking standing-wave signatures beyond a qualitative "possible standing wave" remark.
12. **Unresolved resolution mismatch.** ~1″ optical vs. 10–15″ radio: no deconvolution or spatial-matching, so the radio "above the umbral ring" source and the slit-scan sub-region are not verified to be colocated beyond visual superposition on the radio map.

The method (Morlet CWT + cross-wavelet + banded cross-correlation of two simultaneously observed altitudes) is standard, clearly described at the level of a textbook exercise, and in principle reproducible given the two observatories' public data (Nobeyama archives + Sayana reduced series, which are not public). The qualitative conclusions (both methods see 3-min and 5-min QPOs; radio lags optical by ~50 s; low-frequency modes present up to ~30 min) are credible but under-quantified, and several competing non-propagation explanations for the delay are acknowledged without being excluded.

# Critique: Writing and Framing of Abramov-Maximov et al., "Multilevel Analysis of Oscillation Motions in Active Regions of the Sun"

## Overclaims Beyond the Evidence

The paper's central empirical content is a cross-wavelet comparison of Hα line-of-sight velocity and 17 GHz radio flux from a **single active region** (NOAA 10661) on **three days**, with total observation windows of 40–60 minutes. Several sentences elevate this modest data set to broader physical conclusions it cannot support:

**1. The causal MHD-wave interpretation (Abstract + p. 9):**

> "A comparison of the wavelet spectra for optical and radio observations showed a time delay of about 50 seconds of the radio results with respect to optical ones. This implies a MHD wave traveling upward inside the umbral magnetic tube of the sunspot."

The word "implies" does enormous work. A ~50 s lag between two wavelet-transformed time series does not uniquely identify an upward-propagating MHD wave. Alternative explanations are not excluded: (a) different filtering heights of the *same* wave in the two formation regions could produce an apparent delay without a physical signal traveling between them; (b) a systematic instrumental or solar-cycle drift common to both channels; (c) the delay could equal a *downward* propagation if one mis-assigns the causal direction. The paper never quotes an uncertainty on the 50 s value, gives no significance test on the cross-correlation peak (Figure 6d shows the curve but no confidence band), and draws the result from one 42-minute interval. "Suggests a possible upward propagation" would be the honest phrasing.

**2. "Consequently this is certainly not a noise" (p. 4, §3):**

> "We calculated the cross-wavelet transform and wavelet coherency between optical and radio time series (Figure 5). Obviously the wave-trains of the optical and radio data agree well. Consequently this is certainly not a noise."

"Certainly" is an absolute. Coherence between two signals of the same solar object does not rule out (i) a common-mode instrumental artifact at the site, (ii) a spurious coupling through the analysis pipeline (both use the same Morlet kernel, same 95% FAP threshold), or (iii) a genuine oscillation that is *not* the MHD mode the authors attribute it to. The phrase "Obviously … consequently" is a rhetorical assertion, not a statistical proof. A single pair of observations cannot distinguish the intended physical signal from any correlated common driver.

**3. Deriving the CCTR height (p. 9):**

> "the delay of 45 sec is interpreted as the time of propagation of the MHD wave from the chromosphere to the corona and suggests that the height of the CCTR is approximately 2700 km."

The 60 km s⁻¹ propagation speed is borrowed from Kobanov, Kolobov, and Makarchik (2006) and is not measured in this paper. Multiplying an unmeasured speed by an un-uncertainty-quoted 45 s delay to claim a "height of the CCTR" of 2700 km conflates a model-dependent inference with an observational finding. Moreover, the CCTR height is spatially variable (thousands of km across a sunspot); quoting a single value from one event at one location over-generalises.

**4. The "first time" claim (p. 14, §6):**

> "To our knowledge, we present here for the first time the study of these MHD waves based on simultaneous optical and radio observations, which refer to coronal and chromospheric levels of the solar atmosphere."

The Nobeyama 17 GHz sunspot-oscillation literature (Gelbreikh et al. 1999, Shibasaki et al. 2001a,b — all cited in the authors' own introduction) had already studied *simultaneous* optical and radio sunspot oscillations. What is genuinely new here is the use of *wavelet coherence and time-delay cross-correlation* to extract a propagation delay. The "first time" phrasing overstates the novelty relative to what the authors themselves cite.

**5. The harmonic attribution of 16-min vs 32-min oscillations (p. 13):**

> "Although the ratio of the frequencies of the two modes is not exactly equal to 2 (about 1.9), the similarity of the temporal variations of the two modes lead us to the conclusion that the 16-minute oscillations are the harmonic of the 30–32-minute oscillations."

Acknowledging the ratio is 1.9, then concluding "the harmonic" anyway, is internally contradictory. A ratio of 1.9 (rather than 2.0 within measurement error — and no error on the period measurement is given) is equally consistent with two independent modes. The word "harmonic" implies a specific nonlinear generation mechanism that is not demonstrated.

**6. Grandiose framing vs. actual evidence (Introduction + Conclusion):**

The Introduction opens with "The study of oscillatory motions is one of the most significant methods of plasma structure analysis of the solar atmosphere" and the Conclusion says "This work illustrates the recent progress in a new approach." The study that actually appears involves one AR, three 40–60 min windows, a 1-D spectrogrograph slit, and a single radio source. The framing promises a field-advancing methodology; the delivery is one illustrative case. This gap between the abstract's general claims ("simultaneous observations in different layers") and the specific, single-event nature of the analysis is the paper's biggest framing problem.

**7. "It is well known" (p. 5):**

> "It is well known that most of oscillation processes observed in sunspots are unstable with a low level of the quality of oscillations."

No reference is attached to this sweeping generalisation. "Most," "well known," "unstable" are all doing load-bearing work without citation or quantification.
