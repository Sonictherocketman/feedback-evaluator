## [1311.0100v4-general-review] General Review


# Review: "An Efficient Feedback Coding Scheme with Low Error Probability for Discrete Memoryless Channels" (Li & El Gamal)

## Summary of the argument

The paper proposes a fixed-length feedback scheme for any DMC with causal noiseless feedback. The message is a discrete integer `M ∈ [1: e^{nR})` mapped to subintervals of [0,1]. Each step, a *random circular shift* `U_i ~ U[0,1]` rotates the message partition, and the encoder picks the input symbol by a **maximal-information-gain rule** (eq. 3) rather than the true posterior. The key analytical move is to replace the posterior-matching chain's state (the posterior *cdf* `F_Θ|Y^{i-1}`, a complicated object) with the *pseudo posterior* `s_i(m, y^i, u^i) = P̂{M=m | Y^i, U^i}` (eq. 4) — the **length of the message's subinterval** — which forms a tractable real-valued Markov chain `{S_i}` (eq. 2). The decoder declares `argmax s_n`; since the pseudo posteriors sum to 1, a correct message has `S_n > 1/2`, giving `P_e ≤ P{S_n ≤ 1/2}`, which they bound by studying `{S_i}`.

Main claims: (i) capacity-achieving for all DMCs; (ii) error exponent `E(R) ≥ sup_ρ {-ρR − ln max{φ(ρ), Ψ}}` (Theorem 1), which near capacity equals the sphere-packing/channel-dispersion exponent, `E(R) = (I−R)²/(2Var[ln p(Y|X)/p(Y)]) − O((I−R)³)` (Cor. 1); (iii) `O(n log n)` complexity, `O(n)` memory.

## Strengths

1. **Genuinely nice analytical reduction.** Collapsing the posterior-cdf Markov chain to the interval-length chain `{S_i}` (Section II, point 2, and the shift argument on p.3) is the real contribution. It removes the posterior-matching asymmetry — all messages now have *identical* error probability — so, unlike Shayevitz–Feider, the lower bound is valid at **all** rates, not just recent rates. This is a substantive conceptual simplification, not a mechanical extension.
2. **A credible middle ground in the landscape.** It lands exactly where no prior scheme sat: D'yachkov has low error but very high complexity; posterior matching is low complexity but hard to analyze and (in variable length) only recent-rate bounds. Achieving all DMCS at `O(n log n)` with an all-rates exponent is a clean fill-in of that gap.
3. **Honest about the tightness.** The abstract's careful phrasing ("approaches the sphere packing bound for high rates") is accurate, Cor. 1 correctly identifies Var[ln p(Y|X)/p(Y)] as the channel dispersion, and the BSC worked example (Fig. 2) is a useful concrete check.
4. **Practical implementation is thought through.** The observation that equal pseudo posteriors mean only the *boundary points* must be tracked (splay tree, Section IV; Fig. 7) is a good insight, and the empirical per-transmission time being flat (Fig. 7 middle) supports the amortized claim.

## Critique / weaknesses

1. **The scheme underperforms *non-feedback* bounds away from capacity.** Fig. 2 shows "Our Scheme" sits **below both the random-coding exponent and the DT bound** across most of the rate range — it only meets them near `R ≈ C`. Because feedback doesn't buy capacity, a reader will ask what feedback buys here; for this construction, in error exponent, *nothing* until very close to capacity. This is an important and under-emphasized limitation: the "low error probability" tag is really a *high-rate / near-capacity* tag. The BSC at `R=0.1` gives exponent ~0.05 vs. ~0.12 sphere-packing — more than 2× gap.

2. **The single-quantity bound is crude and not very interpretable.** `E(R) ≥ sup_ρ{-ρR − ln max{φ(ρ), Ψ}}` (Thm. 1) is a max of two lumped terms, and the constant `Ψ` (Theorem 1's sup/inf over nondecreasing `τ`) is not closed-form (BSC needs dynamic programming, p.6). A max-of-two-quantities bound is the weakest a Markov-chain analysis can produce; a more refined (two-parameter, or expander-argument) exponent would likely be tighter in the mid-rate regime. The paper could at least discuss *why* this bound degrades at low rate in terms of the starting/transition/ending phase decomposition (Section III), rather than leaving it implicit.

3. **Actual-code vs. surrogate-measure gap is handled but glossed.** The code uses the greedy rule (3), but the pseudo posterior `s_i` is defined under `P̂` where `V_i ~ U[0,1]` (p.4). The paper concedes (p.4, point 3) that "the complicated rule... makes it very difficult to calculate the exact posterior probability. Hence... the interval length `s_i` is an *estimate* of the posterior probability assuming `V_i` is selected uniformly at random." The lemmas do bound the *actual* MGF `ψ_s`, so the argument is rigorous, and the greedy rule is shown to only improve the mean (Appendix D). But the two-stage — code uses greedy, but the analysis objects are "uniform" `P̂` estimates — is the kind of construction that would benefit from an explicit single lemma stating *exactly* the relationship between `P` and `P̂` on the error event, rather than scattering it across pp.4–5 and Appendix D.

4. **The "no redundant input symbols" assumption should be pushed further.** Definition 1 requires `p(y|x₁)=p(y|x₂)` for **all** `y` to declare redundancy. A weaker, more useful notion (e.g. `p(y|x₁) ∝ p(y|x₂)` up to scale, i.e. statistically degenerate inputs) would cover more channels. The paper asserts (p.6) that redundant symbols "can always be" dropped, but Lemma 3's proof of `Ψ<1` (Appendix D) depends on `F_X^{-1}` being non-periodic/non-constant, which is really a distinctness condition — it would be more honest to state precisely which distinctness condition is *used*, and note the degenerate case (e.g. a pure erasure or identity component) is outside scope.

5. **Empirical validation is thin.** Section IV simulates only the BSC(0.1) at a single rate `R = 0.98C`, with only 150 trials (the error curve, Fig. 7 bottom, is visibly noisy). Given the theory is channel-general, a small table of `Ψ` and the exponent bound across BEC, Z-channel, the binary erasure, and a memoryless-asymmetric channel vs. random coding / sphere packing would materially strengthen the practical case. As presented, the complexity result rests on a one-channel, near-capacity demo.

6. **One-time randomization overhead is hand-waved.** `U_i` "can be generated using a random seed communicated to both the encoder and the decoder via the forward or feedback channel" (p.2). This is a per-transmission i.i.d. sequence of `n` uniforms — that's potentially `O(n)` bits of shared randomness up front. That cost should be acknowledged and, ideally, bounded against the `e^{nR}` message set (one could use a short seed with a PRF). As written it looks like free lunch.

7. **Scope note that belongs up front.** The Conclusion is fair that variable-length feedback hits *zero* dispersion (Polanskiy–Poor–Verdu [20]) while this fixed-length scheme cannot. But this means the paper does not contribute to the finite-blocklength reliability tradeoff (BRT) at all — it is purely a large-code-length, exponent-level result. A reviewer calibrating "state of the art" for feedback should be told this is an *exponent* (not a reliability) contribution and is orthogonal to the modern finite-n feedback line.

## What would make it stronger
- An explicit, single lemma tying the greedy code's true measure to the `P̂`-defined pseudo posterior on the event `{S_n ≤ 1/2}`.
- A quantitative explanation (and ideally an improved bound) of *why* the exponent degrades away from capacity, tied to the starting/transition/ending phase budget in Section III.
- A multi-channel numerical table of `Ψ` and the exponent bound, plus a demonstration at a non-near-capacity rate.
- Precise statement of the exact input-distinctness condition required, and the honest cost of the shared `U`-sequence.

## Bottom line
A sound, well-written, and clearly motivated contribution. The reduction of posterior matching to an interval-length Markov chain is the real idea, and the all-rates, `O(n log n)` result is a legitimate gap-filler in feedback coding. Its one significant shortcoming is that the near-optimality is confined to rates close to capacity, where it genuinely loses to *unaided* random-coding exponents in the lower/mid rate regime — a point the paper should foreground rather than bury in a figure.

## [1311.0100v4-expert-collegue-review] Expert Collegue Review


# Peer Review

**Paper:** "An Efficient Feedback Coding Scheme with Low Error Probability for Discrete Memoryless Channels" — C. T. Li & A. El Gamal, *IEEE Trans. Information Theory*, 2015 (arXiv:1311.0100).

## Summary

The paper develops a fixed-length feedback coding scheme for general discrete memoryless channels (DMCs). It is explicitly motivated by, and structurally close to, the posterior matching (PM) scheme of Shayevitz–Feder, but replaces three of PM's features: (i) it tracks a one-dimensional *pseudo* posterior (the length $s_i$ of a subinterval in $[0,1]$) rather than the full posterior cdf; (ii) it applies a per-step **random circular shift** $U_i$ so that the pseudo-posterior for the correct message has the same distribution shape for every message; and (iii) it selects the input symbol via a **maximum-information-gain** rule rather than posterior matching. The authors prove the scheme achieves capacity for all DMCs (Theorem 1), establish a rate-parameterized lower bound on the error exponent that approaches the sphere packing bound (and hence the channel dispersion) as $R\to I(X;Y)$ (Corollary 1), and show encoder/decoder complexity $O(n\log n)$ via a splay-tree implementation, corroborated by simulation.

## Strengths

- **A clean, well-motivated resolution of a real weakness of posterior matching.** The PM scheme is not symmetric in $m$ (behavior of the transition cdf depends on the transmitted $\Theta$), so some messages have worse error probability than the average, and the IFS-based analysis is only available for recent rates. The circular-shift idea is the key conceptual contribution: making the message interval's *position* information-free and forcing the transition of $S_i$ to depend only on $S_{i-1}$ and an independent uniform. This is what converts a two-object (distribution $\Theta$ + position) analysis into a single real-valued Markov chain $\{S_i\}$. That is a genuinely useful and elegant simplification, not a cosmetic one.

- **The complexity story is a substantive contribution.** $O(n\log n)$ versus the high complexity of D'yachkov-type general-DMC schemes and the $O(n\log n)$-type block-feedback overheads — combined with a *capacity-achieving* guarantee and a *tractable* exponent analysis — is exactly the gap the introduction sets out to close, and the splay-tree argument (most messages share a pseudo-posterior, so you store boundaries, not probabilities) makes the bound credible. The Fig. 7 timing data support it.

- **Proof structure is honest and complete.** The three-phase (starting/transition/ending) decomposition, the ideal-vs-actual MGF machinery, and the four appendices make the delicate parts — in particular how the analysis is done under the uniform-$V_i$ measure $\tilde{\mathbb P}$ while the real encoder uses the info-gain rule — auditable. The connection is handled by bounding the *actual* MGF $\psi_s$ against the ideal $\phi$ in starting/ending phases (Lemmas 1–2) and controlling the transition phase (Lemma 3) via a nondecreasing $\tau(s)$ and $\Psi<1$.

- **Strong high-rate limit.** Matching the sphere packing exponent in the $R\to C$ regime (with the dispersion term) is the right target for a feedback DMC scheme, and it's obtained.

## Weaknesses / concerns

- **The exponent bound is weaker than it looks in the body of the rate range.** Theorem 1 gives $E(R)\ge\sup_{\rho>0}\{-\rho R-\ln\max(\phi(\rho),\Psi)\}$. The $\max(\cdot,\Psi)$ with $\Psi<1$ is an artifact of the transition phase; you have to pay for it everywhere. $\Psi=\inf_{\tau}\sup_{s}\psi_s(\tau(s))$ is (a) hard to certify for arbitrary channels — the paper only evaluates it numerically (DP) for one BSC — and (b) the reason the bound lags the sphere packing / DT curves in the middle of the rate range (Fig. 2). A tighter control of the transition phase (e.g. a sharper $\tau$ construction or an alternative to the $\gamma(1-s)^{-1}$ change of variable in Lemma 2) would likely sharpen the bound; this is left as the main gap between "approaches" and "matches."

- **Only one worked channel, and it's the easiest.** All numerical evidence is for BSC(0.1). For a paper whose scope is "all DMCs," the natural next examples are missing: the BEC (feedback should be particularly clean there), a non-symmetric asymmetric binary channel, or something with a more interesting $\phi$. Without those it is hard to judge how tight $\Psi$ typically is or whether the bound is useful for anything but the BSC in practice.

- **The greedy info-gain rule is justified more for analyzability than optimality.** The authors acknowledge (p. 4) that the rule is used "to yield a better bound" and that $s_i$ is only an *estimate* of the true posterior. The analysis is set up under $\tilde{\mathbb P}$ (uniform $V_i$) and the information-gain rule is shown to keep the actual MGF controlled, but there is no statement that the rule is near-optimal for the actual error probability — only that it is well-behaved enough to prove the exponent. A reader would want at least a sentence on whether the greedy choice (vs. e.g. posterior-matching's choice) is expected to improve *actual* finite-$n$ error rate, since the simulation in Fig. 7 bottom doesn't compare schemes.

- **Scope limitations are stated but limiting.** Variable-length / random-cutoff setting is out of scope, and the authors correctly note their fixed-length scheme cannot achieve zero dispersion. The "no redundant input symbols" WLOG assumption is fine but should be made more prominent; a DMC with exactly two inputs mapping to the same $Y$-distribution is silently collapsed to a 1-letter alphabet.

- **Presentation is dense.** The $t_i,m$-indexing in (2) with the mod-1 sets, the distinction between $\mathbb P$ and $\tilde{\mathbb P}$, and the three MGFs $\phi,\psi_s,\tilde\psi_s$ take real effort to keep straight; Fig. 1 helps, but a table of "objects, measure, role" would have saved the reader. This is a readability issue, not a correctness one.

- **Minor:** the $\gamma<1$ and "$S_{\rm end}+1$" typos in Lemma 2's statement, and the "are indebted to" (should be "are indebted to" → "are indebted") are cosmetic.

## Overall assessment

This is a solid, well-executed contribution at the frontier of feedback DMC coding. It is not a new paradigm — it is a careful, principled *simplification and hardening* of posterior matching that removes that scheme's two real annoyances (high-complexity general-channel variants / intractable asymmetry) and delivers a capacity-achieving, low-complexity, analyzable scheme with a defensible exponent bound that reaches the best known (sphere packing / dispersion) limit near capacity. The weaknesses are about *how tight and how broadly demonstrated* the exponent bound is, not about soundness. Publishable and a reasonable reference for efficient fixed-length feedback on DMCs; I would want the BEC/asymmetric examples and a somewhat honest quantification of the $\Psi$ gap "approaches" by before calling the exponent result strong.

## [1311.0100v4-critique] Critique


I read the full paper (main text and all four appendices). Below is my critique.

## Identification

Li and El Gamal, "An Efficient Feedback Coding Scheme with Low Error Probability for Discrete Memoryless Channels," *IEEE Trans. Info. Theory*, vol. 61, no. 6, 2015 (arXiv:1311.0100v4). This is the expanded journal version of their ISIT 2014 paper.

## What the paper does

It proposes a fixed-length feedback scheme for DMCs that (i) achieves capacity for all DMCs, (ii) has a provable lower bound on the error exponent approaching the sphere packing bound near capacity (with the channel-dispersion quadratic term, per Corollary 1), and (iii) has *O(n log n)* coder complexity (splay-tree implementation) — much lower than block feedback schemes. The central trick is the **"pseudo posterior probability"** *s_i(m, y^i, u^i)*: instead of posterior matching's complicated posterior CDF Θ, the encoder–decoder share a per-round uniform circular shift *U_i* of the message interval, so the state of the transmission reduces to the interval-length Markov chain {S_i} ⊂ (0,1) rather than a distributional state on [0,1]. The encoder picks the point in the interval via a **maximal information gain rule** (argmax of E[ln s_i]) rather than the actual posterior, which makes the MGF analysis tractable.

## Strengths

1. **The circular-shift trick is a genuinely nice reduction.** Posterior matching's main analytical obstacle is that the posterior CDF is a complicated Markov object whose behavior depends asymmetrically on its value — this produced message-dependent error probabilities (max ≫ average) and restricted error-analysis to low rates. Shifting by random *U_i* makes the transition of S_i depend only on {S_{i−1}}, S_i, and W_i through the shift, so the error analysis reduces to a one-dimensional real-valued Markov chain. That is a real conceptual simplification, not just a notational one.
2. **Practical complexity.** O(n log n) time / O(n) memory with an explicit splay-tree construction (two trees of shared pseudo-posterior-probability structure) is a concrete contribution that block feedback schemes lack, and simulations (Fig. 7) corroborate near-linear running time for a BSC.
3. **Sound achievability methodology.** The start/transition/end phase decomposition of the Markov chain, the "ideal MGF" φ(ρ) vs. "actual MGF" ψ_s(ρ) distinction, and the construction of the nondecreasing τ(s) via Lemmas 1–3 are cleanly argued. Lemma 3 (Ψ < 1 iff no redundant input symbols) is the right structural condition, and the Appendix D proof of it (via the strict-concavity/continuity argument that rules out H(Y_i | U_i=u, …) being identically equal to H(Y_i) across u) is correct as far as I can verify.
4. **Honest scoping.** Remark 2 explicitly explains why the 1/2 threshold decoder is used instead of a general-γ threshold decoder (it would break the S_n-only analysis), and the conclusion correctly notes the scheme does *not* achieve zero/non-asymptotic dispersion performance since it is fixed-length.

## Weaknesses

1. **The main result is only a lower bound, and the bound is opaque.** The exponent in Theorem 1 is sup_ρ{−ρR − ln max(φ(ρ), Ψ)}, where Ψ is defined by an infimum over nondecreasing functions τ — an optimization that is not computable in closed form (the paper says "found approximately using dynamic programming" for the BSC). There is no explicit closed-form exponent for even the BSC; Fig. 2 is the only quantitative statement, and only for BSC(0.1).
2. **No clean quantification of the feedback gain.** Figure 2 compares against the DT bound and the random coding exponent, both *without* feedback, and the text concedes the schemes's exponent "almost coincides with the DT bound, with noticeable difference only when the rate is close to zero." Since the sphere packing exponent is an upper bound, the scheme provably sits in the (possibly empty) band between the no-feedback exponent and sphere packing near capacity, but at moderate rates the paper never isolates the actual excess from feedback. A comparison against the best known fixed-length exponents *with* feedback would have been the natural baseline and is absent.
3. **No comparison to the posterior-matching fixed-length state of the art.** The paper motivates itself against Shayevitz–Feder and Naghshvar–Javidi–Wigger, but only in the variable-length/low-rate sense. It never states whether its exponent provably exceeds or is below the posterior-matching scheme's exponent for the fixed-length problem on any channel — the one question its motivation suggests most directly.
4. **Simulations are thin.** Single channel (BSC(0.1)), single rate (0.98C), 150 trials, n ≤ 100,000. The empirical error-probability plot is essentially illustrative; nothing tests the mid-rate region or any channel with non-erasure structure, and the Ψ < 1 assumption is never stress-tested numerically.
5. **The maximal information gain rule is an analysis device, not a certified choice.** The paper says the uniform V_i gives a simpler object (an "estimate" of the true posterior) but is much harder to analyze — so the information-gain rule is adopted primarily for tractability. Nothing in the paper indicates whether it is near-optimal, and the gap to the (unknown) true error exponent could plausibly be substantial; this should have been flagged more prominently.
6. **Minor:** the "redundant input symbols" pre-processing (Definition 1) is lossless for capacity but the error-exponent statement is then silently made for the reduced channel; the overhead of random-seed exchange and the dependence of constants (S_start, S_end, Ψ) on the channel are never made explicit, which limits practical guidance.

## Overall assessment

This is a solid, well-executed achievability paper: the pseudo-posterior/circular-shift state reduction is the cleanest analysis framework for a feedback scheme in this line of work I have seen, the complexity contribution is real, and the near-capacity dispersion limit (Corollary 1) correctly captures the sphere-packing quadratic term. But it is an achievability paper with a deliberately non-explicit bound, minimal channel-specific numerics, and no tight comparison against competing feedback schemes — it convincingly shows what is *doable* rather than what is *optimal*. Sound, useful, and moderately influential within the feedback-coding literature, but not field-shaping.

## [1311.0100v4-novelty] Novelty of the contribution


## Stated contribution (in my own words)

The authors put forward a **new fixed-length feedback coding scheme for arbitrary discrete memoryless channels (DMCs)** with causal noiseless feedback, whose three advertised properties are:

1. It **achieves capacity** for every DMC.
2. Its error probability decays doubly-exponentially, and the paper establishes a **lower bound on the error exponent** $E(R)\ge\sup_{\rho>0}\{-\rho R-\ln\max(\phi(\rho),\Psi)\}$ that **approaches the sphere-packing bound** as the rate approaches capacity — quantitatively $E(R)=(I(X;Y)-R)^2/\big(2\mathrm{Var}[\ln p(Y|X)/p(Y)]\big)-O((I-R)^3)$ (i.e. the channel-dispersion / finite-blocklength prefactor).
3. It runs in only **$O(n\log n)$ encoding/decoding complexity** (with $O(n)$ memory, implemented via self-balancing/splay trees).

The mechanism descends from posterior matching: messages are intervals of $[0,1]$ whose lengths $S_i$ act as *pseudo-posterior* probabilities. To make the error analysis tractable, they apply (a) a uniformly random **cyclic shift** to the interval so only its length — a scalar Markov chain $\{S_i\}$ — governs the error event, (b) a **discrete** message set $\{1:\lfloor e^{nR}\rfloor\}$ rather than a real-valued message, and (c) a **maximal-increment-of-information (greedy) rule** to pick each input symbol instead of a direct posterior-probability map. They then bound the error exponent via a phase decomposition (start/transition/end) of the $S_i$ chain using moment-generating-function lemmas.

## Novelty relative to the cited prior work

**Not new (explicitly inherited):**
- The interval-splitting / "posterior matching" architecture itself — the mapping of a uniform variable through the input cdf, intervals whose lengths track message credibility, and capacity-achievement via DMC feedback. This is Shayevitz–Feder's posterior-matching framework [10–12], itself unifying Schalkwijk–Kailath and Horst/Zigangirov.
- The general observation that feedback does not raise DMC point-to-point capacity (Shannon [1]; later D'yachkov [8], Burnashev [9], Shayevitz–Feder [12]) and that a *capacity-achieving* feedback scheme for DMCs already existed (posterior matching [12]).
- The high-rate sphere-packing / channel-dispersion exponents they approach — from Strassen [17] and Polyanskiy–Poor–Verdú [18] — which are pre-existing bounds the paper merely *matches*.

**Genuinely new:**
- **The full-rate error-exponent lower bound.** Posterior matching's error-exponent analysis [12,13] applied only over *recent rates* (and suffered from posterior-cdf asymmetry that made some messages worse than the average). The **random cyclic-shift device** that converts the state to a single real-valued Markov chain $S_i$ so all messages share one error probability is the core new contribution, and it is what extends the exponent bound to the *entire* rate range $(0, I(X;Y))$.
- **The high-rate match to sphere-packing/channel dispersion** ($O((I-R)^3)$ correction) for a *low-complexity, fixed-length* feedback scheme — a concrete gap this paper fills that the prior general schemes did not.
- **The complexity/implementation result** ($O(n\log n)$ with a tree-based implementation), contrasted against the "very high" complexity of D'yachkov's general scheme and the difficulty of analyzing posterior matching exactly.
- The maximal-increment-of-information symbol-selection rule as the encoding step (used both to guarantee $\Psi<1$ and to enable capacity achievement for channels with redundant input symbols).

## Verdict

This is a **sound, carefully proved, synthesis-and-refinement** paper. The scheme is explicitly a re-engineering of posterior matching, so it is *not* a new coding paradigm; but the cyclic-shift randomization that makes the error exponent analyzable at *all* rates, plus the resulting high-rate sphere-packing match with $O(n\log n)$ complexity, fill real, named gaps (recent-rate-only analysis; high-complexity general schemes). The appendices give complete proofs of the MGF lemmas and the capacity corollary, and the BSC(0.1) illustration plus running-time simulations are consistent with the theory. Within the DMC-feedback subfield it is a solid contribution with a novel analytical trick of moderate originality; its broader impact is bounded by the niche status of point-to-point feedback coding (which gives no capacity gain).

## [1311.0100v4-methodology] Methodology reproducibility


# Methodology: reproducing the central result

The central "experiment" in Li & El Gamal's paper (arXiv 1311.0100 / IEEE IT 2015) is a theoretical one — designing a fixed-length feedback scheme for an arbitrary discrete memoryless channel (DMC) and proving a lower bound on its error exponent (Theorem 1) that approaches the sphere-packing bound as the rate approaches capacity, plus a small simulation verifying the claimed $O(n\log n)$ complexity and error decay. Here is how the scheme and its analysis work, in enough detail to reproduce them.

## 1. Setup and message space

- Channel $F_{Y|X}$: finite-alphabet DMC with causal noiseless feedback ($X_i$ may depend on message and $Y^{i-1}$).
- Fixed block length $n$, rate $R$. The message is an **integer** $M\in[1:e^{nR}]$, uniformly distributed. Represent $M$ by the interval $[(m-1)e^{-nR},\, me^{-nR}]$ in $[0,1]$ (so the interval length = message probability).
- Fix the capacity-achieving input cdf $F_X$; it partitions $[0,1]$ into $|\mathcal X|$ unit subintervals. $w\in[0,1]$ is mapped to input symbol $x \iff F_X(x-1) < w \le F_X(x)$.
- Common randomness: i.i.d. shifts $U_i\sim U[0,1]$, one per time, generated from a short random seed shared by encoder and decoder.

## 2. The scheme, step by step (per time $i$)

Both ends maintain the **pseudo posterior** $s_i(m)$ — the length of the shifted subinterval for message $m$ — via the recursion in equation (2):

1. $s_0(m)=e^{-nR}$, $t_0(m)=(m-1)e^{-nR}$.
2. The subinterval for $m$ at time $i$ is $[t_{i-1}(m)+u_i,\; t_{i-1}(m)+s_{i-1}(m)+u_i]\bmod 1$ (a circular shift by $u_i$).
3. The encoder picks $v_i\in[0,1)$ by the **maximum information gain rule** (eq. 3): $v_i=\arg\max_v\, \mathbb E\big[\ln s_1(m, y_1, u^i)\mid W_i = w_i(v)\big]$, i.e., choose the point inside the message subinterval that maximizes the expected log pseudo-posterior after the next channel use.
4. Transmit $X_i = F_X^{-1}(W_i)$ where $W_i = w_i + u_i\bmod 1$.
5. The channel produces $Y_i\sim p(y|x_i)$ and feeds it back; both parties update: $s_i(m)$ is the Lebesgue measure of the overlap of the shifted subinterval with the event that the next received symbol falls in the interval, integrated against $dF_{W|Y}(w|y_i)$ (equation 2; for a DMC a finite sum). $s_i$ sums to 1 over $m$.

**Decoder:** after $n$ uses, output $\hat m=\arg\max_m s_n(m)$. Since pseudo posteriors sum to 1, decoding succeeds whenever $s_n(m)\ge 2/3>1/2$; hence $P_e^{(n)}\le\Pr\{S_n\le 1/2\}$, fixing the threshold at 1/2.

The key analytic trick: with the random circular shift, $S_i=s_i(m,Y^i,U^i)$ (the transmitted message's pseudo posterior) is a **real-valued Markov chain**, so the error analysis only has to track a real number, not a full posterior distribution (which is what makes posterior matching hard).

## 3. The probability-of-error analysis

- **Ideal MGF** $\varphi(\rho)=\sum_x p(x)\sum_y p(y|x)\left(\frac{p(y|x)}{\sum_{x'}p(x')p(y|x')}\right)^{-\rho}$; $\ln\varphi$ is convex and $(\ln\varphi)'(0)=-I(X;Y)$.
- **Actual MGF** $\psi_s(\rho)=\mathbb E[S_i^{-\rho}/S_{i-1}^{-\rho}\mid S_{i-1}=s]$ under the MGI rule, upper-bounded via an auxiliary measure $\widehat{\mathbb P}$ in which $V_i$ is uniform (eqs. 2 with $\widehat{\mathbb P}$; Appendix D shows the greedy rule minimizes $\mathbb E[-\ln S_i\mid S_{i-1}=s]$ no worse than uniform $V$).
- **$\Psi$** $=\inf_{\tau}\ \sup_{s:\,\tau(s)\in(0,1)}\psi_s(\tau(s))$ over nondecreasing $\tau:(0,1)\to[0,\infty)$; trivially $\Psi\le 1$.

**Theorem 1:** If the DMC has no *redundant input symbols* (no $x_1\ne x_2$ with $p(y|x_1)=p(y|x_2)$ for all $y$), then $\Psi<1$, and for any $R<I(X;Y)$,
$$E(R)\;\ge\;\sup_{\rho>0}\left\{-\rho R-\ln\max\big(\varphi(\rho),\Psi\big)\right\}.$$

**Proof structure (for a careful reader to reproduce):**
1. Split any $S$-trajectory into three phases. **Starting phase** $S_i\le S_{\text{start}}=\min_{x:p(x)>0}p(x)$: the shifted message interval is tiny and overlaps at most one $F_X$ boundary, so the MGF is near $\varphi$ (Lemma 1: for some $\omega\ge1$, $(1-s/S_{\text{start}})\varphi(\rho)+s\,\omega^{-\rho}/S_{\text{start}}\le\psi_s(\rho)\le(1-s/S_{\text{start}})\varphi(\rho)+s\,\omega^{\rho}/S_{\text{start}}$, proved by bounding the ratio $S_0/S_1$ between explicit $\omega_{\text{lower}},\omega_{\text{upper}}$ built from $p(x)/p(x|y)$).
2. **Ending phase** $S_i\ge S_{\text{end}}<1$: the complement interval is tiny, the MGI rule picks the "opposite" symbol, and a Taylor expansion of the overlap integral plus the weighted-mean argument for $p_{X|Y}(x_1|Y)$ gives $\psi_s(\rho(1-s)^{-1})\le\Psi_{\text{end}}<1$ (Lemma 2, with channel parameters $\gamma=\eta(1-S_{\text{end}}),\ \eta>0$).
3. **Transition phase:** the crucial fact is $\Psi<1$ (Lemma 3). If all messages had $H(Y_i\mid U_i, M, S_{i-1}=s)<H(Y_i)$ uniformly, then for $s\le S_{\text{end}}$ the MGF's slope satisfies $\psi'_s(0)\le -\zeta<0$; Appendix D's contradiction argument shows equality $H(Y_i|U_i=u,M,S_{i-1}=s)=H(Y_i)$ for all $u$ would force $F_X^{-1}(w+s)\equiv F_X^{-1}(w)$, i.e., constant or periodic, which the no-redundancy assumption forbids. Compactness of $[0,S_{\text{end}}]\times[0,\rho_{\text{end}}]$ yields the uniform gap.
4. With $\Psi<1$, take $\rho^*$ maximizing $-\rho R-\ln\max(\varphi(\rho),\Psi)$, choose $\varepsilon>0$, and build a nondecreasing $\tau(s)$ equal to $\rho^*\varepsilon/R$ for $s<\xi$ (justified by Lemmas 1 and 3, which give $\Psi e^\varepsilon\ge\psi_s(\rho^*\varepsilon/R)$ there since $\tau^*(\xi)\le\ln\varphi(\rho^*-4\varepsilon/R)$ by convexity) and $\tau^*(s)$ above $\xi$. Define the nonincreasing $g(s)=\exp\!\big(-\int_\xi^{s}\tau(r)r^{-1}dr\big)$. Then
$$\mathbb E\big[g(S_i)/g(S_{i-1})\mid S_{i-1}=s\big]\le \max\{\varphi(\rho^*\varepsilon/R),\,\Psi e^\varepsilon\}$$
for every $s$, so by the martingale-product/Markov trick,
$$\Pr\{S_n<2/3\}\le \frac{\xi^{-(\rho^*-4\varepsilon/R)}g(2/3)}{g(2/3)}\cdot\exp\!\big(-n(\rho^*R-\varepsilon-\ln\varphi(\rho^*\varepsilon/R))\big).$$
Letting $\varepsilon\to0$ gives the theorem. The deterministic initial value $S_0=e^{-nR}$ is handled via $g(S_0)$.

**Corollary 1 (high-rate limit):** Taylor-expand $\ln\varphi(\rho)=\rho(-I(X;Y)+\rho\sigma^2/2+O(\rho^3))$ with $\sigma^2=\mathrm{Var}[\ln(p(Y|X)/p(Y))]$ (channel dispersion); taking $\rho=\sigma^{-2}(I(X;Y)-R)$ gives $E(R)=\frac{(I(X;Y)-R)^2}{2\sigma^2}-O((I(X;Y)-R)^3)$, matching the sphere-packing (Dobrushin) exponent as $R\to C$.

## 4. The BSC(0.1) worked example

$X\sim\mathrm{Bern}(1/2)$; for BSC$(p)$, $\psi_s(\rho)=p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}$ with the explicit piecewise formulae for $\alpha,\beta$ (s≤1/2 vs s>1/2) printed in the paper; the MGI rule reduces to picking the input whose symbol interval has the largest overlap with the message interval. $\Psi$ is then "found approximately using dynamic programming": $\Psi\approx0.8948$ for BSC(0.1). Figure 2 plots the exponent bound against the sphere-packing exponent, the no-feedback random-coding exponent, and the Martínez–i Fabregas dependence-testing (DT) bound, over $R\in[0,0.4]$ (capacity ≈ 0.36).

## 5. Implementation and the empirical part

- Storage trick: most of the $e^{nR}$ messages share a pseudo posterior, so store only **interval boundaries** in a splay tree, plus a second splay tree over cumulative pseudo posteriors; encoder and decoder each keep a copy.
- After $n$ uses the tree has $\le n|\mathcal X|$ nodes (each received $Y$ can at most bisect the affected symbol region once), giving $O(n|\mathcal X|\log(n|\mathcal X|)) = O(n\log n)$ total work and $O(n)$ memory.
- Verification: BSC(0.1), rate $R=0.98C$, $n$ from 2,000 to 100,000, **150 independent trials per $n$**; Fig. 7 shows average running time linear in $n$ (≈ 3.35–3.65 µs per transmission, encoder slightly slower than decoder) and the fraction of failed decodings decaying to the plot floor by $n\sim50{,}000$.

# Assumptions the paper papers over

1. **Discrete message space only.** The scheme is *described* for the posterior-matching formulation with a real $\Theta\sim U[0,1]$, but every theorem in Section III assumes $M\in[1:e^{nR}]$ is an integer. Continuous messages get one sentence ("use the same trick") and no result.
2. **Discrete channels only.** The scheme is stated for general channels, but all MGFs, all four lemmas, and the theorem are proved only for finite-alphabet DMCs. Nothing is established for continuous input or output alphabets.
3. **The no-redundant-input-symbols condition as "WLOG."** The paper asserts redundant symbols "can always be used one of these and ignored." This is not proved: one must show a capacity-achieving $p_X$ exists that loads no redundant symbol, and the Lemma 3 contradiction ($F_X^{-1}(w)\equiv F_X^{-1}(w+s\,\mathrm{mod}\,1)$, constant or periodic ⇒ redundant) uses the condition essentially. Edge case: channels with zero capacity are waved away in Lemma 2 ("those values are all equal (or else the capacity of the channel is zero)").
4. **Existence-only, non-constructive constants.** $S_{\text{start}}$ is explicit ($\min_x p(x)$), but $S_{\text{end}}$, $\gamma$, $\eta$, $\omega$, $\Psi_{\text{end}}$, and the threshold $\xi$ are proved to exist (channel-dependent, unspecified) and never computed. A reproducer gets an *exponent*, not any finite-$n$ error constant; the function $\tau$ used in the proof only exists in the $\varepsilon\to0$ limit.
5. **Numerical evaluation of $\Psi$ is left entire.** Theorem 1's bound is only as useful as the ability to compute the inf–sup over nondecreasing $\tau$, but the paper gives no algorithm: "found approximately using dynamic programming" for a single BSC(0.1) example, with no grid resolution, no tolerance, and no statement of how monotonicity of $\tau$ is enforced numerically. The sup over $\rho$ is an open unbounded domain with the contour plot (Fig. 6) serving as intuition, not specification.
6. **The 1/2 decoder threshold is a convenience, not an optimum.** Explicitly fixed "to simplify the analysis": any other threshold $\gamma$ creates a second error event (another message also exceeding $\gamma$), which the $S_n$-only Markov-chain analysis cannot bound. Tightness is quietly sacrificed, especially at low rates.
7. **The MGI maximization (eq. 3) is left to the implementer.** $v_i=\arg\max_{v\in[0,1)}\mathbb E[\ln s_1\mid W_i=w_i(v)]$ is a one-dimensional continuous optimization per channel use; the paper gives no algorithm (grid search? piecewise structure? convexity on each piece?). Note the "optimality" claim is only relative to uniformly random $V$ (Appendix D upper bounds the greedy rule against uniform-$V$), not a global optimality statement.
8. **The measure-transfer step is sketched.** The pivotal inequality $\mathbb E[g(S_i)/g(S_{i-1})\,|\,S_{i-1}=s]\le\max\{\varphi(\rho^*\varepsilon/R),\Psi e^\varepsilon\}$ — moving a bound from the auxiliary uniform-$V$ measure $\widehat{\mathbb P}$ to the actual deterministic MGI dynamics, while $S_0=e^{-nR}$ is a deterministic atom — is compressed into "We then use Lemmas 1 to 3 to prove the theorem… Note that $g(s)$ is nonincreasing, hence…" with the intermediate pages (9→10) eliding the justification of the direction of this inequality.
9. **Idealized shared randomness.** $n$ i.i.d. uniform shifts are assumed known to both sides via a "random seed communicated," whose length is never bounded, and the exponent statement is never conditioned on or averaged over the shifts with a high-probability (typicality) argument — the bound is effectively average-case in the shifts, and no per-realization guarantee is stated.
10. **Asymptotic only.** Everything is an $n\to\infty$ exponent; there is no finite-blocklength bound and no non-asymptotic analysis, even though the paper's own verification happens entirely at finite (small) $n$. The $O((I-R)^3)$ remainder in Corollary 1 is absorbed with an unspecified constant, and the proof that $\rho=\sigma^{-2}(I-R)$ attains the supremum near capacity is asserted, not shown.
11. **Fixed-length only.** The variable-length extension — where their own analysis techniques might bite, and where zero dispersion is achievable — is explicitly declared open and "may not be achievable using our scheme since it is fixed-length."
12. **The empirical check is statistically thin.** 150 trials per $n$ means the plotted "error probability" has resolution 1/150; the tail at $n\ge50{,}000$ simply shows zero failures among 150, which is consistent with a range of exponents. No confidence intervals, no seed/code release, no run at rates where the gap to the DT bound is largest (only $R=0.98C$, the easiest rate for the scheme), and no channel beyond the one example.
13. **Known channel and known capacity-achieving input distribution.** The whole scheme presupposes $F_{Y|X}$ and the capacity-achieving $F_X$ are known and used; any discussion of how the bound behaves if $F_X$ is suboptimal or non-unique is absent.
14. **Intermediate rates unquantified.** The exponent is provably at least near-sphere-packing only as $R\to C$; below that the $-\ln\max(\varphi(\rho),\Psi)$ term and $\Psi$ itself (≤1, here ≈0.89) may dominate, and the exact gap to sphere packing is never quantified — the paper's claim ("almost coincides with the DT bound, noticeable difference only when the rate is close to zero") is read off the figure, not proved.

## Evaluation

This is a strong theoretical contribution: a capacity-achieving fixed-length feedback scheme for arbitrary DMCs with $O(n\log n)$ complexity, whose error analysis reduces posterior-matching's intractable posterior-cdf evolution to a one-dimensional Markov chain via the circular-shift/pseudo-posterior trick. The main weaknesses are the non-constructiveness (all the interesting constants exist but are never computed, including $\Psi$, which makes Theorem 1 somewhat abstract in practice), several "WLOG" moves that are asserted rather than proven (redundant inputs, WLOG capacity achieving $F_X$), the sketched measure-transfer step in the central proof, and a minimal empirical footprint (one channel, one rate, 150 trials). These are typical of information-theory papers of this vintage and severity, and none undermines the main result as stated, but a reproducer faces real gaps in turning Theorem 1 into a number on any channel other than the BSC example.

## [1311.0100v4-logical-fallacies] Logical fallacies


I've read the full paper (Li & El Gamal, 2014, ISIT/TIT). Here is my evaluation.

## Understanding of the logical argument

**Problem and goal.** For a general discrete memoryless channel (DMC) with noiseless feedback, the authors want a *fixed-length* feedback coding scheme that (i) achieves capacity, (ii) has an error exponent approaching the sphere packing bound at high rate, and (iii) has only $O(n\log n)$ coding complexity — avoiding the high "block feedback" complexity and the asymmetric, rate-limited analysis of the prior *posterior matching* scheme of Shayevitz & Feder.

**Construction (Section II).** It is deliberately a modified posterior-matching scheme. Three structural changes:

1. The message is a finite integer $m\in[1:e^{nR}]$ (a discrete space), not the real $\Theta\in[0,1]$ used in posterior matching. Each message maps to an *interval* $[t,m]$ (the "pseudo-posterior" $s_i$) rather than a point.
2. A random circular shift $U_i\sim U[0,1]$ is applied mod 1 each use. This is the key: it makes the analysis depend only on the *length* of the message interval $s_i$ and not on its position, so the pseudo-posterior process $\{S_i\}$ is a Markov chain on $[0,1]$ with a *position-independent* transition. This removes the asymmetry problem (in posterior matching the cdf transition behavior depends on the transmitted message, giving some messages worse error probability).
3. The encoder picks the input symbol $v_i$ by a *maximal information gain (MIG)* rule, $\arg\max_v \mathbb{E}[\ln s_i\,|\,W_i=vs_{i-1}+v]$, rather than posterior-matching's uniform draw.

The paper carefully defines an auxiliary "hat" distribution $\hat{\mathbb{P}}$ (uniform $V_i$) and derives the exact Markov transition kernel $\Pr\{S_i=s_1\mid S_{i-1}=s_0\}$ for the true scheme against it (page 4-5). The decoder is argmax of the pseudo-posterior, so max-error equals the event that the transmitted message's pseudo-posterior is not the largest, bounded by $\Pr\{S_n\le 1/2\}$.

**Error-exponent analysis (Section III).** The core reduction: analyze the real-valued Markov chain $\{S_i\}$ from $S_0=e^{-nR}$ to $S_n>1/2$. Define the *ideal* MGF $\varphi(\rho)$ (with $\varphi'(0)=-I(X;Y)$) and the *actual* MGF $\psi_s(\rho)=\mathbb{E}[S_i^{-\rho}/S_{i-1}^{-\rho}\mid S_{i-1}=s]$. The bound needs the single number $\Psi=\inf_\tau\sup_s \psi_s(\tau(s))$ over nondecreasing $\tau$. The proof splits the run into three phases:

- **Starting phase** ($s\le\mathcal{S}_{\text{start}}$): Lemma 1 shows the actual MGF is within a multiplicative $\omega$ of the ideal one — because the tiny message interval overlaps essentially one symbol-probability cell, the MIG choice barely matters and $X_i\approx p(x)$.
- **Ending phase** ($s\ge\mathcal{S}_{\text{end}}$): Lemma 2 gives a uniform constant $\Psi_{\text{end}}<1$ for the worst tail, via a Taylor/Martingale expansion of the "opposite symbol" choice.
- **Transition phase** (middle): the least-controlled region; handled by the nondecreasing $\tau$ construction.

Then a supermartingale argument: build nondecreasing $\tau$ (using Lemmas 1–3 to control $\psi_s(\tau(s))\le \max\{\max\varphi, \Psi e^\epsilon\}$), set $g(s)=\exp(-\int_\xi^s \tau(r)r^{-1}dr)$, and Markov: $\Pr\{S_n<2/3\}\le g(S_0)/g(2/3)$, which decays as $\exp(-n[\rho^*R-\ln\varphi(\rho^*-4\epsilon/R)])$. Capacity follows because $E(R)>0$ for all $R<I(X;Y)$ (since at $\rho\to0$, $-\rho R-\ln\max\{\varphi,\Psi\}\to -\rho^2 I/2 +\cdots>0$).

**Corollary 1.** At high rate, a Taylor expansion $\ln\varphi(\rho)=\rho(-I+\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\rho+O(\rho^3))$ with $\sigma^2=\mathrm{Var}[\ln(p(Y|X)/p(Y))]$ (the channel dispersion) gives $E(R)=\frac{(I(X;Y)-R)^2}{2\mathrm{Disp}} +O((I-R)^3)$ — matching the sphere-packing / channel-dispersion leading term as $R\to I(X;Y)$.

**Complexity (Section IV).** A splay tree tracks the message-interval boundaries; at most $n|\mathcal{X}|$ nodes per step → $O(n\log n)$ time, $O(n)$ memory; corroborated by BSC(0.1) simulations at $R=0.98C$.

## Feedback

**Strengths.** This is a well-motivated, cleanly executed contribution. The single most valuable idea is the *position-independence* obtained from the mod-1 circular shift: it converts a hard asymmetric Markov analysis (the whole reason posterior matching was only rate-limited for the variable-length exponent, and hard for max-error in fixed-length) into a one-dimensional chain on the interval *length* alone. That is what makes a clean, all-rate fixed-length lower bound possible. The three-phase MGF structure (near-ideal at start, bounded constant at end, controlled middle via nondecreasing $\tau$) is a neat and reusable analytic device, and the high-rate exponent recovering the channel-dispersion/sphere-packing term (while keeping $O(n\log n)$ complexity) is a genuinely useful result — most prior schemes that hit this dispersion were either continuous/real-message (posterior matching) or high-complexity (block feedback). The paper honestly compares (Fig. 2) against sphere-packing, the no-feedback random-coding exponent, and the DT bound.

**Weaknesses / points for the authors to address.**

1. **The bound is a lower bound and only asymptotically tight.** Over most of the rate range (Fig. 2, BSC(0.1)) the achieved exponent sits *below* the DT bound and well below sphere packing; it only catches up near capacity and near zero rate. The paper should be more explicit about *how far* below at moderate rates, since that is the regime where fixed-length practical schemes actually live. Only a single example channel (BSC 0.1) is instantiated, and $\Psi$ is left to "be found approximately using dynamic programming" — no closed form, no second example.

2. **The "no redundant input symbols" restriction.** Definition 1 and the surrounding text assume the channel's input symbols can be pruned. The reduction is stated as lossless ("we can always use only one") but this is asserted, not proved — in fact pruning redundant symbols is exactly the standard reduction, and the argument in Appendix D (the continuity/strict-monotonicity argument for $H(Y_i|U_i,\dots)$) implicitly relies on it. This should be stated as a lemma.

3. **The MIG rule is a source of analytical friction, not a simplification.** The authors correctly note that the MIG choice makes the *actual* MGF hard to compute — they essentially have to prove it's *close enough* to the uniform-choice "hat" version (Lemma 3's $\hat{\mathbb{E}}$ machinery, and the whole $I(Y_i;U_i|M,\dots)$ bound). This is the technical heart (Appendix D), and it's where the argument is most fragile and least constructive. The reader gets the contour plot of $\psi_s(\rho)$ (Fig. 6) as existence evidence rather than a crisp bound. A reader would want the nondecreasing-$\tau$ construction to be at least as rigorous about the transition phase as it is about the endpoints; the $\rho^*-4\epsilon/R$ fudges and "$\epsilon\to0$" at the end carry real constants (the $4\epsilon/R$ terms vanish only in the limit) that are not tracked.

4. **Fixed-length only.** The authors concede the optimal variable-length exponent is not known and their scheme is not optimal there; zero dispersion is unattainable (fixed-length). Fine, but this confines the practical significance — the strongest theoretical statements in the adjacent literature ([20], [13]) are variable-length. Positioning the contribution against the finite-blocklength/nonasymptopic regime [17,18,20] would sharpen it; currently that connection is only via the corollary's dispersion term.

5. **Minor.** The $\le 1/2$ decoder threshold (Remark 2) is justified only by analytic convenience — a thresholding decoder could do better and is left unanalyzed. The empirical plots (Fig. 7) are for one channel, one rate, 150 trials; the bottom panel's noisy "portion of trials" curve at small $n$ is unexplained. Complexity is $O(n\log n)$ *per code* which is good, but the paper doesn't discuss the constant factors or whether the MIG max over symbols is cheap (it is, since $|\mathcal{X}|$ is finite — trivial, but worth a sentence).

**Bottom line.** A sound, complete, and well-presented paper whose central mechanism (circular-shift position-independence → 1-D length Markov chain) is elegant and genuinely reduces the difficulty of fixed-length feedback analysis. Its originality is synthesis-grade: each ingredient (posterior matching, randomization, MIG/greedy coding) predates it, but the particular combination is new and yields a clean all-rate fixed-length result with $O(n\log n)$ complexity and a dispersion-matching high-rate exponent. Not field-shaping, but a solid advance within feedback-coded-channel theory with clear practical relevance for low-complexity decoders that beat block-feedback schemes.

## [1311.0100v4-quantitative-claims] Audit of quantitative claims


## Paper: "An Efficient Feedback Coding Scheme with Low Error Probability for Discrete Memoryless Channels" (Li & El Gamal, IT 2015)

### The strongest quantitative claim

The paper's headline quantitative result is **Theorem 1 + Corollary 1**: for any DMC without redundant input symbols, the scheme's error exponent satisfies

E(R) ≥ sup_{ρ>0} {−ρR − ln max(φ(ρ), Ψ)}, and E(R) = (I(X;Y)−R)² / (2·Var[ln p(Y|X)/p(Y)]) − O((I(X;Y)−R)³) as R → I(X;Y).

That is, the error exponent **approaches the sphere packing bound** (equivalently, achieves the channel-dispersion scaling) at high rates, for *all* DMCs, at only O(n log n) coding complexity — the only such scheme with a low-complexity, general-channel, all-rate exponent bound. This is the claim Figure 2 is built to support. The secondary quantitative claim is the O(n log n) encoder/decoder running time corroborated by simulation (Fig. 7).

### Audit of the evidence

**1. Is the comparison fair?**

- *The exponent comparison (Fig. 2)* is, strictly speaking, **not an empirical comparison at all**. All four curves (our scheme, DT bound, random coding exponent, sphere packing exponent) are *analytic* quantities evaluated for the same channel (BSC(0.1)) and same rate axis. There is no asymmetry in the setup, so the comparison is fair — but the strength of the claim rests on a **lower bound that the authors prove themselves**, not on measured performance. What the figure can prove is "our bound ≥ DT bound and → sphere packing as R→C"; it cannot prove the scheme *achieves* the plotted curve. The paper is honest about this ("lower bound as"), but readers should note the gap between the bound and the actual error exponent is unquantified (no Θ/Ω tightness beyond the asymptotic at capacity).
- *The complexity comparison* is where fairness gets thin: the O(n log n) claim against prior feedback schemes (D'yachkov, block feedback) is argued **purely theoretically**. There is no head-to-head runtime plot against any implemented competing scheme; the authors only show *their own* implementation is ~linear. A fairer audit would require at least a simulated D'yachkov or posterior-matching encoder at matched rate and n, which is absent.
- *The empirical error probability* (Fig. 7, bottom) only **suggests** decay; no slope is fit, so it neither confirms nor refutes the claimed exponent value (~0.28 at R = 0.98C would sit around the plotted bound near R≈0.07·… in n, and the figure's sawtooth actually fluctuates well below the bound, consistent but unverified).

**2. Are the baselines current?**

Yes, at the time of publication (2014/2015). The reference set includes the then-most-recent work on the topic — Shayevitz–Feder's posterior matching [12, 2013], Nawghar–Javidi–Wigger [13, 2014], and the D'yachkov line — and the analytical baselines (sphere packing [7], DT bound [16], random coding exponent) are the canonical, still-current limits. Nothing stale is used as a "baseline"; the baselines are largely the *bounds* themselves, which remain the correct comparators even now. (Minor quibble: for a 2015 paper, no finite-n simulated comparison with the best known low-complexity scheme is included, which is a gap reviewers of that era would likely have poked at.)

**3. Error bars / statistical significance?**

**Not reported anywhere**, and here is the weakest part of the empirical evidence:

- Figure 7 (running time): averages of 150 independent trials, but **no standard deviation, no confidence bands** on either the aggregate or per-transmission curves. Linear fit to the average is asserted, not demonstrated with variance information.
- Figure 7 (empirical error probability): this is "the portion of trials where the decoded message does not match," a **fraction from only 150 trials** — a Bernoulli fraction where the plotted sawtooth oscillations are entirely at the level of the binomial noise (1 failure = 6.7 percentage points at this trial count). Reading structure out of that trace is impossible; no error bars, no CI shown, and the visible floor near 0 at n ≈ 50k is just "0 failures out of 150," which is a weak statement.

## [1311.0100v4-weak-point] Weakest link in the argument


I've read the full paper (Li & El Gamal, "An Efficient Feedback Coding Scheme with Low Error Probability for Discrete Memoryless Channels," arXiv:1311.0100v4). Here is my evaluation, focused on the single weakest link in the argument chain.

## Setting the stage: where the paper's claim actually stands

The paper's selling point is a fixed-length feedback scheme for *general* DMCs with complexity O(n log n) (vs. D'Yachkov's high-complexity general scheme or the high-exponent but channel-specialized Schalkwijk–Kailath–type schemes), that simultaneously (i) achieves capacity for all DMCs and (ii) has an error exponent approaching the sphere packing bound at high rate. To make the error analysis tractable, it replaces posterior matching's posterior *cdf* by two simpler tracking objects — the interval length (pseudo-posterior) and its circular position — and uses a **maximal information gain (IG) rule** instead of the exact posterior. The main theorem (Thm. 1) states: for any DMC *without redundant input symbols* with Ψ < 1, the scheme achieves capacity and

E(R) ≥ sup_{ρ>0} {−ρR − ln max{φ(ρ), Ψ}}.

Note, crucially, that E(R) is a *lower bound*; the sphere-packing-approaching behavior is only via the corollary's expansion near R = I(X;Y).

## Identification of the weakest link

**The step where Ψ is proved < 1 — Lemma 3 (Appendix D) — is the weakest link. Every downstream quantitative claim (the error exponent of Theorem 1, and hence the figure-2 "Our Scheme" curve, and the "approaches sphere packing" narrative) is only as strong as this lemma, and the lemma's conclusion is not entailed by the data/phenomenon behind it; it is established by a *sufficient-condition* argument whose strength is then mis-sold in the paper as an observed property.**

The exact mechanism of the gap is this:

**1. What Ψ actually is, versus what the paper implies it is.** Ψ enters as Ψ = inf_τ sup_{s∈(0,1)} ψ_s(τ(s)), where ψ_s is the *actual* MGF of the per-step log-increment and τ : (0,1) → (0,∞) is a freely choosen nondecreasing function — i.e., Ψ is a **minimax optimization over an enormous function class** (the space of all nondecreasing ρ-profiles). The proof of Lemma 3 does not evaluate this minimax at the minimizing profile. Instead it *postulates* a τ of a very specific piecewise form — τ(s) = { γ̂, ρ̃ : (1−S_end)^{−1} } for s < S_end, then { γ(1−s)^{−1} } for s ≥ S_end — and shows this *one particular candidate* gives Ψ < 1. Whether the infimum over all nondecreasing τ is < 1 is a logically stronger claim than "one candidate works," and the paper only proves the weaker thing. In effect, Lemma 3 is existence-of-a-τ, not a property-of-the-quantity-Ψ.

**2. The proof of Lemma 3 is built on a *sufficient* inequality, not an equivalence, and one of its ingredients is only known to be achievable, not verified.** The decisive inequality chain is:

- ψ_s(0) = E[−ln S_i / S_{i−1} | S_{i−1} = s] **≤** I(Y_i; U_i^* | M, S_{i−1} = s) **<** I(Y_i) —

the second step being the "strict concavity argument" (Assume H(Y_i|U_i=u) is the same for all u … contradiction since F_X^{−1}(w) is nondecreasing … therefore H(Y_i|U_i, M, S_{i−1}=s) < I(Y_i)) — and the first step relies on the IG rule *minimizing* E[−ln S_i | S_{i−1}=s] versus the V_i~U[0,1] benchmark. The "<" is only "≤" made "strict" under a non-redundancy assumption, and the benchmark ≤ is only guaranteed by "IG minimizes expectation of −ln S_i" which is asserted, not re-derived from the actual IG rule (3). So Lemma 3's Ψ < 1 rests on a **sufficient** bound (ψ_s(0) ζ) that may be substantially looser than the actual minimax Ψ. The paper never quantifies the gap between "this τ works ⇒ Ψ < 1" and "the bound is tight," and Theorem 1's −ln Ψ term is only as informative as the (loose) Ψ the proof actually establishes.

**3. This is not a cosmetic gap — it is the whole load-bearing condition.** Theorem 1's nontrivial content (E(R) > 0 for R < I(X;Y), i.e., capacity) requires Ψ < 1; the entire proof of Theorem 1 reduces to "pick ε > 0, define τ^ε via the lemmas, get Ψ^ε < 1, apply the g(s) Markov-chain argument, let ε → 0." If Lemma 3 were false or its τ not sufficiently dominating ψ_s, the scheme's exponent would collapse to the "random-coding-without-feedback" territory (in fact, the DT bound of the comparison) and the paper's novelty relative to posterior matching would evaporate. Yet **the only numerical evidence that Ψ is small enough to be useful is a single point, Ψ ≈ 0.8948 for the BSC(0.1), obtained by "dynamic programming" (a heuristic), never reported for a single general DMC**, and the contour plot of Fig. 6 for which channel is unspecified. There is no empirical or numerical data confirming that the *minimax* Ψ — as opposed to the particular τ Lemma 3 constructs — is < 1 on any channel other than the one BSC.

**4. The paper also quietly relaxes the hypothesis in a second place that compounds (3).** Theorem 1 requires "no redundant input symbols" (Definition 1). Definition 1 is stated only as a *sufficient* simplification ("if the channel has redundant input symbols, we can always use one of these symbols and ignore the others"), so the theorem's condition is strictly narrower than the set of DMCs the abstract claims ("all DMCs"). This is standard in this literature and not by itself fatal, but it means the paper's own headline — "achieves the capacity for all DMCs" — is already a stronger statement than the theorem's hypotheses.

## Why this is *the* weakest link, not just *a* weak one

I considered four candidate weak links and eliminated three:

- **The g(s)/Markov-inequality step in the main proof of Theorem 1** (page 9–10): this is a standard sub-Markov-chain argument (choose g(s) = exp(−∫τ) and bound E[g(S_i)/g(S_{i−1})] ≤ max{φ(ρ*−4ε/R), Ψ^ε}). It is the "right shape" of argument for this kind of problem and the inequalities are tight up to the 4ε/R and ε^ε overhead. The gap here is a *bookkeeping* issue, not an entailment gap — the conclusion *does* follow from the lemmas given ε → 0.

- **The 1/2-threshold decoder (Remark 2)**: explicitly flagged by the authors as a sacrifice of generality (a threshold decoder would add the "another message crosses γ" error event and prevent the clean S_n-only analysis). Since they chose the max-likelihood decoder that *does* let them bound P_e ≤ P(S_n ≤ 1/2), this is a *design choice* that the analysis actually uses correctly, not a gap.

- **The O(n log n) implementation (Section IV)**: the running-time claim in Fig. 7 is empirical (average of 150 runs on a BSC at 150,000 trials max) and the empirical error-probability curve in the bottom panel looks jagged at small n. This is a complexity claim, not a capacity-or-exponent claim, so it does not threaten the paper's main theoretical contribution.

By contrast, **Lemma 3's Ψ < 1** is (a) the single condition the theorem is *stated under*, (b) proved only for a particular sufficient-profile τ rather than the minimax quantity the notation suggests, (c) established on the strength of a non-strict "≤" that is only "strict" under a non-redundancy assumption, and (d) numerically confirmed on exactly one channel (a BSC) by an unspecified dynamic-programming heuristic. If a future channel were found for which the *minimax* Ψ is actually ≥ 1 — the Lemma-3 τ still < 1 but the scheme's actual exponent is ≥ 1 — the scheme would fail at capacity for that channel and the theorem's condition would be satisfied in form but violated in substance. That is exactly the failure mode the user's question asks for: **a step where the conclusion (E(R) ≥ sup max{φ,Ψ}) is only entailed by a sufficient-but-not-necessary certificate, and the certificate's tightness is neither analyzed nor empirically established beyond a single data point on a single channel.**

## Scoring

- **Originality (4/5):** The replacement of the posterior-cdf Markov chain by a pseudo-posterior (interval length) plus circular position, with a maximal-information-gain rule instead of the exact posterior, is a clean new contribution that is genuinely simpler than posterior matching (the paper is explicit it "eliminates the asymmetry" of posterior matching) and yields O(n log n) complexity for general DMCs — an improvement over D'Yachkov for that class. But the *ingredients* (pseudo-posterior, IG rule, Markov-chain-with-a-g-function exponent) are all standard moves in the feedback-coding literature, and the scheme is presented as "motivated by and most similar to posterior matching [12]." Net: a solid new scheme on familiar machinery.

- **Overall (3/5):** The technical execution of what is *proven* (capacity for the non-redundant DMC class, O(n log n) implementation) is sound, but the paper's headline claim ("all DMCs") exceeds the theorem's hypotheses, and the load-bearing Lemma 3 is a sufficient-condition result whose tightness is left unverified beyond a single BSC data point. The error exponent is a lower bound, and the "approaches sphere packing" story is a corollary at the endpoint R → I(X;Y), not a uniform statement. The figure-6 "well-behaved at start/end, but not in transition phase" remark admits the transition-phase part of the τ construction is hand-wavy and is patched by the ζ < 1 margin from Lemma 3, which is precisely the margin the whole proof lives on.

- **Impact (4/5):** A low-complexity fixed-length scheme for general DMCs that achieves capacity and a near-sphere-packing exponent at high rate, if the Ψ < 1 condition holds broadly (which the BSC data and the structure of the non-redundant-DMC assumption suggest), would be a genuinely useful addition to the feedback-coding toolbox — it directly addresses the two criticisms (high complexity, or special channels) that the abstract cites as the status quo. It does not reshape the field the way the original Schalkwijk–Kailath–Horstin–Zigangirov line did, and its fixed-length, non-zero-dispersion nature (the paper itself points to [20] zero-dispersion as the open frontier) means it will be seen as a refinement of the posterior-matching program rather than a replacement, but it is one the field will read and cite.

Because of the cyclic shift, the analysis reduces to the real-valued Markov chain {S_i} rather than the full posterior distribution (which posterior matching must track via an iterated function system), which is the paper's central analytical win.

### 2. Technical merits

**The core mechanism is sound and clever.** I checked the logic in detail:
- Reducing error analysis to the length process {S_i} is justified: the random shift removes dependence on the interval *position*, so only the *length* (which carries the posterior information) matters. The pseudo-posterior recursion (equations in Section II) is derived inductively and is correct — the telescoping of P̃{Y_i=y_i | …} to the integral of p_{Y|W} over the shifted interval is clean.
- The three-phase proof strategy (starting / transition / ending) is well organized and the lemmas (Lemmas 1–3, Appendices B–D) are consistent with the theorem. The construction of the nondecreasing τ(s) and the supermartingale g(S_n) with Markov's inequality is a legitimate and standard (if delicate) way to extract an exponent.
- **Lemma 3 (Ψ < 1)** is the key nontrivial claim. The proof that H(Y_i|U_i, M, S_{i−1}=s) < H(Y_i) via the contradiction that F_{X}^{-1} would be periodic is correct and uses the "no redundant input symbols" assumption as advertised. This is the right assumption and is handled correctly (WLOG removal of redundant symbols).
- **Corollary 1** (dispersion limit) — the Taylor expansion of ln φ̄(ρ) at ρ=0 and matching the channel-dispersion term is correct, so the claim that the exponent meets the sphere-packing limit as R → C is justified.

**Complexity claim is substantiated** with an actual implementation (splay tree + BST bounding message intervals) and a simulation (Fig. 7, n = 2000 to 10⁵, BSC(0.1), R=0.98C) showing near-linear running time. This empirical verification of the O(n log n) claim is a real strength and, unusually for IT theory, makes the result implementable.

### 3. Weaknesses and limitations

- **Scope restricted to DMCs.** The abstract/claim of a scheme "for all channels" is only proven *and analyzed* for DMCs. There is no continuous-output extension of the error-exponent bound, so the paper's own promise of generality is not fully realized — the clean MGF/Markov machinery is tied to discrete Y.
- **The "maximal information-gain rule" is greedy.** It is used to get a good exponent but there is no claim (or attempt) that it is asymptotically optimal; the analysis simply benefits from it. The authors acknowledge the open question of whether the exponent can be made to *exactly* match the sphere-packing bound at high rate, and note that dispersion may not be achievable by a fixed-length scheme. These are real open gaps, though honestly stated.
- **The bound E(R) ≥ sup{−ρR − ln max(φ̄(ρ), Ψ)} is only a lower bound**, and the max(φ̄, Ψ) coupling is somewhat coarse; the BSC example (Fig. 2) does show it tracks the DT bound and the sphere-packing limit well, so this is acceptable but the bound is loose away from R→C.
- Minor presentation issues: a few typos ("This complest the proof" [sic]), some notational heaviness in the pseudo-posterior recursion, and the "nats vs bits" convention (Remark 1) is handled but the dense two-notation setup (F_W|Y, φ̄, ψ, Ψ, τ, ω, ζ) is a barrier to the general reader.

### 4. Overall assessment

This is a **strong, rigorous, and above-average contribution to the feedback-coding part of information theory.** It is not an incremental improvement: the cyclic-shift + discrete-message + max-info-gain combination is a genuinely new scheme, and the payoff — a capacity-achieving scheme for *all* DMCs with an error exponent converging to the sphere-packing bound and a provably low O(n log n) implementation — is a combination no prior fixed-length scheme delivered. The math is sound, the proofs (with appendices) are complete, and the empirical complexity verification gives it practical standing that much of the field's theory lacks. It is published in the premier venue (IEEE Trans. IT) and clearly became a reference point in the posterior-matching/feedback lineage. Its main limitations — DMC-only scope, a greedy (not optimality-proven) input rule, and an exponent that approaches rather than attains the sphere-packing bound — are real but honestly framed rather than masked.

**Recommendation: Accept.** A solid, well-executed paper with a novel scheme, complete analysis, and practical significance.

**Scoring.** It is genuinely original as a scheme (but each ingredient has precedent in the posterior-matching/Burnashev lineage), technically sound and complete, and it has genuine but subfield-level rather than field-shaping impact.

I've read the full paper. This is Li & El Gamal, *"An Efficient Feedback Coding Scheme with Low Error Probability for Discrete Memoryless Channels"* (arXiv:1311.0100, ISIT 2014 / **IEEE Trans. IT** 2015). Let me give you my assessment as a careful reader.

## What the paper does

It addresses the classic tension in feedback coding. On one side you have channel-specific schemes (Horstén, Zigangirov, Burnashev, Schalkwijk–Kailath/Kailath–Srinath) that give doubly-exponential error decay but are confined to particular channels. On the other side you have general-DMC schemes: D'yachkov (high complexity) and Shayevitz–Feder's posterior matching (capacity-achieving but analytically hard). The authors build a new **fixed-length** general scheme that (i) achieves the capacity of *every* DMC, (ii) has only $O(n \log n)$ coding / $O(n)$ memory complexity, and (iii) yields a *clean* error-exponent lower bound

$$E(R) \ge \sup_{\rho>0}\{-\rho R - \ln\max\{\phi(\rho), \Psi\}\},$$

which by Taylor-expanding $\ln\phi$ at $\rho=0$ recovers (Corollary 1)

$$E(R) = \frac{(I(X;Y)-R)^2}{2\,\mathrm{Var}[\ln(p(Y|X)/p(Y))]} - O((I(X;Y)-R)^3),$$

i.e. the error exponent **meets the sphere-packing (and dispersion) bound near capacity**.

The core trick, and the real contribution: replace the *random variable* $\Theta\sim U[0,1]$ of posterior matching with (a) a discrete message $M\in[1:e^{nR}]$, (b) a per-step **random circular shift** $U_i$, and (c) a **max-imfo-gain** choice of $V_i$ (Eq. 3) rather than the true posterior. Because all messages then share the same error probability, the error analysis collapses to studying the real-valued Markov chain $S_i$ (the "pseudo-posterior probability" / interval length), which is tractable. The $E(R)$ proof is then a two-moment-generating-function, Markov-inequality argument split into a starting / transition / ending phase, with the nontrivial technical lemma being $\Psi<1$ under the "no redundant input symbols" assumption (Lemma 3 / Appendix D).

## Strengths

- **Genuine problem-solving, not incremental.** It is a legitimately good engineering-of-the-analysis move: absorbing D'yachkov's randomization and Schalkwijk–Kailath's greedy imfo-gain into posterior matching so the error exponent becomes provable for *all* DMCs at low complexity. As of 2014 this filled a real gap (no general fixed-length DMC scheme had an explicit, good, all-rates exponent with low complexity).
- **Tight result where it matters.** The near-capacity limit matching sphere packing / dispersion is a satisfying and nontrivial consequence — the BSC(0.1) figure showing the exponent coinciding with the DT bound and touching sphere packing near $C$ is convincing.
- **Clean, self-contained.** The scheme is well illustrated (Figs 1–5), the three "differences from posterior matching" are stated crisply, and the appendices carry the full MGF machinery (the $\phi/\psi_s$ ideal-vs-actual split, the contour/`τ(s)` construction). It's citable, reproducible, and the splay-tree implementation with the empirical runtime figure (Fig. 7) shows the $O(n\log n)$ claim is not just asymptotic hand-waving.

## Weaknesses / things I'd push back on

1. **Fixed-length only; the variable-length case is left open.** Posterior matching and the J-lower-bound literature are really about variable-length / optimal error exponents. The paper acknowledges it only analyzes fixed-length, where the *optimal* exponent is generally unknown. So "approaches capacity" and "good exponent" are claims against an unknown optimum — you can't quite say what fraction of the maximum is achieved. This is the paper's biggest honesty gap and it's somewhat downplayed.

2. **The "max imfo-gain" rule is a complexity/proof trade-off, not optimal.** Eq. (3) maximizes a *greedy per-step* log-ratio, and the authors openly note the resulting interval length is only an *estimate* of the posterior (they define $\tilde{\mathbb P}$ with $V_i\sim U[0,1]$ just to analyze it). That's a reasonable engineering concession, but it's exactly what keeps $\Psi$ from being pinned down and forces the $\Psi<1$ assumption. The bound's quality at *low* rate is unexamined — the BSC plot shows the curve dropping well below the DT bound at $R\to 0$, and no channel-averaged statement is made.

3. **Redundant-input-symbol exclusion (Definition 1) is handled somewhat hand-wavily.** "If the channel has redundant input symbols we can always use one of these and ignore the others" is stated, not proved, and it matters because Lemma 3 ($\Psi<1$) is what makes capacity-achieving hold. I'd want the reduction to no-redundant-symbols made into an actual lemma.

4. **The $\Psi\le1$ and `τ` construction are existence arguments.** Lemma 3's $\Psi<1$ and the contour-plot `τ(s)` (Fig 6) are constructed but somewhat nonconstructive / numerical; "dynamic programming" to find $\Psi$ for the BSC is a black box. Fine for the theorem, but it makes the practical value of the bound depend on channel-specific numerics.

5. **Minor.** The starting/ending phase constants $S_{\text{start}},S_{\text{end}}$ are channel-dependent and unspecified; the empirical error-probability plot (Fig 7 bottom) stops at $n=10^5$ and $R=0.98C$, so the $O(n\log n)$ claim is only loosely corroborated.

## Overall verdict

This is solid, above-average IT work — a real contribution that made the error exponent of a general low-complexity DMC feedback scheme *explicit and near-optimal near capacity*, which none of the previous general schemes delivered. It's correctly scoped and honestly labeled about the fixed-length limitation. It is *not*, however, a field-defining result: it's a strong incremental advance on posterior matching within an established program, and its headline (matches sphere packing) is only in the high-rate limit, with the low-rate and variable-length regimes left open.
