## [1805.08518v2-general-review] General Review


# Review: "Highly Irregular Functional Generalized Linear Regression with Electronic Health Records" (Petrovich, Reimherr, Daymont)

## Summary

The paper addresses a real and common data problem — functional covariates observed sparsely, irregularly, and with noise (e.g., infant head-circumference trajectories assembled from electronic health records) — and proposes MISFIT, a multiple-imputation scheme for scalar-on-function (generally GLM) regression. The core ideas are: (i) reframe the sparse-function problem as a missing-data problem and impute from the Gaussian conditional distribution of the curve given (possibly only) the response, so that the imputation model is *compatible* with the target GLM; (ii) propagate imputation uncertainty into pointwise inference via Rubin's rules; and (iii) characterize, via Cameron–Martin theory, precisely when a Gaussian imputation for $X|Y$ is compatible with a logistic model for $Y|X$ (equivalence vs. orthogonality of Gaussian measures, plus a "grey zone"). The paper presents an initial (linear-case) consistency theorem, extensive simulations against PACE and related imputation schemes, and applies the method to a macrocephaly data set.

## Strengths

1. **Framing and motivation are excellent.** The diagnosis of why naïve PACE-style mean imputation is dangerous in sparse settings (bias in the linear FPCA plug-in estimator, and — more importantly — understated standard errors leading to anti-conservative tests) is clearly argued, with the key bias equation displayed explicitly (Sec. 2.2). The EHR setting is a genuinely compelling application where curves routinely have a single observation.
2. **The theorems on equivalence of Gaussian measures (Theorems 1–2) are the paper's most original contribution.** Making explicit the trichotomy — degenerate, "grey zone" (parameterized by a *discontinuous* functional), and the Cameron–Martin case — is a clean and useful piece of folklore that is rarely stated this way for FDA practitioners. The connection to Rao–Varadarajan / Kuo and to the Delaigue–Hall / Dai–Müller–Yao near-perfect-classification condition is a nice synthesis.
3. **Simulation design is unusually thorough.** Four imputation variants (MuC, MuU, MeC, MeU), plus PACE, `fcr`, `pfr`, `funreg`; true vs. estimated imputation parameters; MISE, Type I error and power, and a multivariate-t robustness check. The honest reporting that the conditional methods win only when the signal is strong and $m$ small, and that estimated parameters materially degrade MuC (Table 4), adds credibility.
4. **The inference story is right.** Using Rubin's rules and demonstrating (via the bootstrap overlay in Figure 4) that mean imputation yields nearly invisible, too-precise confidence bands is an important practical message that many FDA users do not hear.

## Weaknesses (ranked)

1. **The MuC type-I-error inflation is a serious practical defect of the recommended method, and the paper underplays it.** Table 8 shows the conditional-imputation estimator's empirical rejection rate at nominal 5% is **0.318** ($m = 2$, null) and 0.30–0.60 at $m = 5$ — a 6–12× inflation. This is a well-known consequence of imputing a predictor *conditionally on the response* ("double dipping" / response leakage): the response partially determines the imputed covariate, so the fit regresses partly on transformed noise of $Y$ itself. The paper notices this (Sec. 3.1.4) but treats it as a footnote, offering the unsatisfying escape hatch — "only use the conditional approach if there is evidence *a priori* that the variables are related, or for larger $m$." The problem is that the paper's own motivating application is exactly a case where one believes the variables are related, and the application uses precisely the flagged MuC method. This point should be foregrounded, stated as a general caveat on response-conditional imputation for predictors, and its interaction with the consistency theory discussed (the theory of Sec. 5 is for $N \to \infty$ with fixed $m$, where the leakage does not vanish in $N$ — it should not, since it is not a vanishing bias but a design artifact; the paper never addresses this at the theory level).
2. **The asymptotic theory is narrow relative to the claims.** Theorem 3 requires: linear model only (no logistic — the paper's headline application), fixed and *identical* $m$ for all subjects, exact known form of $\beta(t)$ in the first $p$ eigenfunctions (no truncation error), distinct eigenvalues, and, critically, Assumption 5 — *consistent estimation of all imputation parameters*. The simulations' estimated-parameter regime (Tables 4–6) shows the last assumption fails operationally: MuC is no longer unbiased and its MISE *increases* with $J$. The consistency theorem therefore certifies a setting that is the *least* realistic one. The conclusion is honest about these gaps (no rates, no $N$–$m$ tradeoff), but the abstract's "has the potential to produce consistent estimates without such assumptions" oversells: consistency here is still conditional on a consistent imputation model.
3. **The missingness mechanism is assumed but never defined.** The paper "implicitly assumes MAR as with the prior literature," yet in the EHR setting the visit schedule is plausibly informative about pathology (children with abnormal growth may be seen more or less often) — i.e., missingness in $X_j$ may be MNAR conditional on $(Y, X)$. A sensitivity discussion (even a brief one, e.g., what happens if visit propensity is related to case status) is warranted for the application section, since the entire EHR motivation rests on irregular *clinical* visit regimes.
4. **Missing baselines in the application.** A scalar-on-function analysis of raw, un-imputed visits (e.g., a logistic GLMM with a shared smooth time effect and subject random effects, or the `refund`/`fda` machinery applied at the score level without curve imputation) is a natural, simpler benchmark that a clinician-statistician reader will immediately ask for. The application compares the four imputation variants *against each other*, not against a no-imputation alternative. Also, all inference is pointwise (95% pointwise bands); the interpretive claims about "negative-then-positive regions" of the coefficient function would be safer with simultaneous (sup-$t$) bands. Finally, with 85 cases the effective sample is small and the logistic model should be reported with some measure of fit/overfitting (e.g., cross-validated discrimination), not just a coefficient plot.
5. **The compatibility theorems' practical content is not fully cashed out.** In the "grey zone," $\beta$ is only a (discontinuous) functional on $L^2$; in practice one sidesteps this by working in the $J$-dimensional FPC basis — which is what the paper does, but never says explicitly. A short paragraph connecting Theorem 1's functional analytic conditions to the finite-basis computation in practice (and to when near-separation, cf. Delaigue–Hall, could make the logistic fit itself pathological) would strengthen Section 2.3.
6. **Minor issues.** P-values reported as "0.000000" (say $<10^{-6}$ instead); the "MISFIT" vs. "MuC" naming is inconsistent (MISFIT appears to be the umbrella for the four variants, with MuC the recommended one — the abstract and Sec. 4 conflate the method with the variant); the type-I test statistic's p-value computation via `imhof` on estimated $\hat C_\beta$ is fine but unverified against a permutation alternative; some figure captions are inconsistent in what red/black denote (Fig. 2 vs. Fig. 3/4).

## Assessment of the central argument

The paper's argument chain — (a) mean imputation in sparse FDA is biased and, more damagingly, yields over-confident inference; (b) a response-conditioned Gaussian imputation is compatible with logistic regression precisely when two Gaussian measures are equivalent; (c) Rubin's rules fix the inference; (d) simulations and an EHR application demonstrate the workflow — is coherent, well-supported, and in places (the bias analysis, the Gaussian-measure characterization, the type-I diagnostics) genuinely illuminating. However, the paper's two main selling points partially collide in practice: the method that wins in sparse, strong-signal settings (MuC) is the one with badly inflated type-I error under the null and high sensitivity to the imputation-model estimator, while the honest theory covers a more-observed, simpler setting in which the unconditional methods suffice. A sharper framing — "response-conditional imputation buys point-estimate efficiency at the price of test validity; here is the tradeoff quantified" — would serve the community better than the current "MuC is best in the sparse regime" framing.

## Recommendations

- Foreground the type-I-error tradeoff of response-conditional imputation, with general advice (e.g., reserve MuC for estimation when $H_0: \beta = 0$ is already strongly supported, or provide a two-stage rule).
- Add a no-imputation GLMM baseline to the macrocephaly application, simultaneous bands, and a short MNAR sensitivity discussion.
- State explicitly how the finite-basis implementation circumvents the grey/orthogonal cases of Theorems 1–2, and (if possible) add theory or simulation for the estimated-parameter and varying-$m$ regimes, since that is where the method lives in practice.

**Bottom line:** a solid, well-crafted methodological paper on a practically important problem, with one of the clearest treatments of the PACE/imputation-bias literature available, but whose consistency theory is narrower than its claims and whose recommended variant carries a substantial, under-weighted validity cost.

## Review: *Highly Irregular Functional Generalized Linear Regression with Electronic Health Records* (Petrovich, Reimherr, Daymont)

### Summary of the contribution

The paper presents **MISFIT**, a method for fitting scalar‑on‑function generalized linear models when the functional covariate is observed only at a sparse, irregular set of time points. The central move is to re‑cast the sparse‑functional‑data problem as a *missing data problem*: build a Gaussian imputation model for $X_i(t)\mid (Y_i, \text{observed points})$, draw $K$ full‑curve imputations, fit the regression model for each, and pool with Rubin's rules. The authors deliver three pieces: (i) a clean imputation framework for linear (§2.2), logistic (§2.3) and, more briefly, general‑exponential‑family (§2.4) response models; (ii) a theoretical characterization (Theorem 1, and its continuous‑outcome cousin Theorem 2) of when a Gaussian $X\mid Y$ condition is *compatible* with a logistic $Y\mid X$ model, phrased through Cameron–Martin space / orthogonality of Gaussian measures; and (iii) a consistency result (Theorem 3) for the FPCA linear case. Simulations benchmark MISFIT ("MuC") against PACE/"MeU", a mean‑conditional variant "MeC", and a multiple‑unconditional "MuU", and the method is applied to a macrocephaly head‑circumference data set from EHR.

This is a well‑motivated and well‑executed methodological paper, and I'd like to flag the places where I think it stands up and the places where I'm genuinely uncertain.

### What I think works well

1. **The problem is real and important.** Sparse and irregular functional data is one of the most practically difficult corners of FDA, and the EHR/clinical‑visit timing motivation is genuinely concrete, not contrived. The head‑circumference application (Fig 1–2, with the striking "many children have exactly one measurement; almost none have $>10$" histogram) makes the sampling problem viscerally clear.

2. **The diagnosis of existing methods is sharp and correct.** The statement that PACE/mean‑imputation approaches (a) produce biased $\hat\beta(t)$ *and* (b) silently understate standard errors / p‑values, with both problems only partially repairable by ad‑hoc confidence‑band work, is the right critique. The explicit demonstration (Eq. 2 onward) that plugging PACE BLUPs into the FPCA estimator does not target the right moment unless $\bar m \to \infty$ is exactly the kind of argument a reader needs before being sold on MI.

3. **The MI re‑framing is clean and principled.** Drawing from the *conditional* distribution (conditioning on $Y_i$) is the correct choice, and doing it in the score basis with the corrected covariance (Eq. 3) is the right technical detail. The treatment of both point estimation (MISE) and statistical inference (type‑I error, Table 8) is one of the paper's genuine strengths — the inference comparison, where mean‑imputation methods visibly inflate the type‑I error at small $m$, is arguably the more important scientific message than the MISE comparison.

4. **The Cameron–Martin / Gaussian‑measure orthogonality angle is a legitimate fresh observation.** Theorem 1 — that a non‑degenerate logistic $Y\mid X$ exists with a Gaussian $X\mid Y$ model precisely when $\|C_X^{-1}\Delta\|<\infty$, with a degenerate (perfectly‑classifiable) regime in between — is a nice, non‑obvious connection between the FDA and measure‑theoretic probability literatures (Delaigle–Hall, Kuo, Bogačev, Mirshani). The supplementary material (App. A, B, C) is honest and correct about the role of the full‑rank / strictly‑positive‑eigenvalue condition.

5. **Clear writing and an honest authorship of caveats.** The conclusions candidly flag that accuracy depends heavily on how the imputation parameters are estimated, and that the theory is "by no means complete." That honesty is refreshing and I trust the authors are stating the real state of the results rather than overselling.

### My substantive concerns

1. **The consistency theory is thin relative to the framing of the paper.** The headline claim in the abstract is that MISFIT is *consistent without* the "$\bar m$ grows fast enough" assumption. But Theorem 3 delivers this only for the **linear** model, with **FPCA**, with **fixed** $m_i\equiv m$ (Assumption 7), **distinct eigenvalues** (Assumption 6), **no truncation error** (Assumption 4), and — crucially — **consistently estimated imputation parameters** (Assumption 5). The logistic and continuous‑outcome results have *no* asymptotic theory at all. So the consistency claim covers strictly less of the method than the method itself. And the authors themselves note (in §6) that the interesting open line is precisely the rate‑vs‑$m$ interplay that their theory sidesteps. I'd want the abstract to be a little more circumspect: the result is "consistency in a restricted linear/FPCA/fixed‑$m$ regime under a strong estimation assumption," not the broad consistency the framing implies.

2. **Assumption 5 is the load‑bearing pillar, and the simulations show it is the weak point.** The consistency result *assumes* one can consistently estimate $\mu_X, C_X, \sigma_\varepsilon^2, C_{XY}, \mu_Y, \sigma_Y^2$. But Section 3.1.2 (Tables 4–6) shows that once the imputation parameters are estimated instead of known, MuC's clean advantage largely disappears in the small‑$m$/small‑$N$ regime, and MuC is **no longer unbiased for any $J$**. So the consistency story is somewhat circular with respect to the practical setting: the regime where the problem is hardest is exactly the regime where the method's advantage erodes. This is acknowledged, but I think it deserves to be foregrounded as *the* key limitation rather than buried in a subsection, because it bounds how much one should trust the theoretical claim in the applications.

3. **The application is under‑powered for the methodological point the paper wants to make.** With 85 cases, all four methods reject $H_0$ (Table 13), and the clinical conclusion (association with both mean head‑to‑height ratio and the growth‑rate contrast, Fig 5) is the same under mean and multiple imputation. The "propagate the imputation uncertainty" benefit shows up in the *width* of the confidence bands (Fig 4; the re‑bootstrap diagnostic) much more clearly than it changes any substantive conclusion. That's fine, but I'd have liked the authors to say more bluntly: *for this data set, the MI vs. mean‑imputation distinction does not change the scientific answer — it only makes the uncertainty statement honest.* I also want a little more justification of modeling with the head‑circumference‑to‑*height* ratio rather than a growth‑curve / LMS or z‑score parameterization, since the sign‑change decomposition of $\hat\beta$ is quite interpretation‑sensitive to that choice. The reported p‑values "0.000000" are essentially uninformative — the actual magnitudes would help.

4. **The method is confined to a single functional covariate with a scalar response.** The authors flag multiple functional covariates and GAMLs as future work, but for EHR work this is not a minor extension — realistic pediatric growth has weight, length, head circumference and a battery of scalar covariates simultaneously. As it stands, the method's direct applicability to the motivating application is narrower than the motivation suggests (they in fact analyzed only head circumference).

5. **The fixed‑$m$ theory contradicts the motivation.** The whole premise is that sampling is irregular and $m$ varies wildly (Fig. 1: modal $m=1$, max $23$). The one consistency theorem assumes *equal and fixed* $m_i$. I'd at least like a remark or a remark‑level theorem discussing what breaks, or a stronger explicit statement that varying $m$ is handled empirically but not theoretically.

### Smaller issues

- The benchmarking of "PACE" goes through `fcurve`/`face`/`fcr` reimplementations and, I read it, compares against a *mean‑unconditional* implementation (MeU) with estimated parameters, whereas classical PACE (Yao–Müller–Wang 2005) achieves consistency by directly smoothing the cross‑covariance. The paper correctly reproduces the bias argument, but I'd want a sentence making clear that it is benchmarking an implementation, not the full PACE machinery, so the "PACE is inconsistent" claim isn't over‑read.
- The $w=0$ columns in the main MISE tables are trivial (β=0 ⇒ many methods hit exactly 0.00) and clutter the figures; I think they'd be cleaner folded into text.
- The software stack (`fcr`, `face`, `fcurve`) is relatively obscure; a public release of MISFIT itself (with a worked macrocephaly script) would materially raise the paper's utility.
- Theorem 1's "grey area" (Case 2: $\|C_X^{-1/2}\Delta\|<\infty$ but $\|C_X^{-1}\Delta\|=\infty$) is handled well in the discussion, but the practical implication — that a near‑perfectly‑separable design makes the logistic model exist but with a non‑continuous functional — is a real hazard in real sparse data and could use a simulation check.
- Minor: the re‑bootstrap in §4 ("basically a one sample bootstrap") should be written out more precisely (resample 1 of 800, re‑duplicate imputation + estimation, 100 times) so it is reproducible; the red‑band coverage statement in Fig. 4 relies on it.

### Bottom line

This is a strong, clearly written, well‑motivated methodological paper that does something genuinely useful: it gives sparse/irregular functional regression a principled multiple‑imputation backbone that yields (a) unbiased $\hat\beta$ in the sparse strong‑signal regime and (b), more importantly, honest uncertainty quantification that mean‑imputation/PACE‑type methods do not provide, plus a nice measure‑theoretic compatibility result. I'd recommend it for publication at a good statistics journal. My reservations are concentrated in one place — the *gap between the theory the paper claims and the theory it proves*: the consistency result is restricted (linear/FPCA/fixed‑$m$), leans on a strong "imputation parameters are consistently estimated" assumption that the simulations show to be the binding constraint, and the method is single‑covariate. Tightening the abstract's consistency claim to match Theorem 3, foregrounding the imputation‑parameter‑estimation sensitivity, and broadening the method or at least the theory to varying $m$ would move this from "good" to "very good" in my eyes.

## Summary

**Petrovich, Reimherr, Daymont (arXiv 1805.08518v2, 2019)** introduce **MISFIT** (Multiple Imputation of Sparsely-sampled Functions at Irregular Times) for scalar-on-function linear and GLM regression when the functional covariate is sparsely/irregularly observed. The core idea: instead of PACE-style *mean* imputation of the curve/scores (which the authors show is biased unless the number of points per curve m → ∞), draw the functional covariate from its **conditional distribution given the outcome Y and the observed points**, pool with Rubin's rules, and propagate imputation uncertainty. They develop imputation models compatible with logistic (Sec 2.3) and canonical-link GLM (Sec 2.4), connect these to the equivalence/orthogonality of Gaussian measures (Theorems 1–2), run extensive simulations (Sec 3), apply it to EHR head-circumference trajectories vs. macrocephaly pathology (Sec 4), and give a preliminary consistency proof (Theorem 3).

## Strengths

- **Genuinely useful, honest motivation.** The PACE "plug the BLUP in" bias decomposition (Sec 2.2, showing E[Yi E[ξij|x]] ≠ E[Yi ξij]) is a clean, correct diagnostic of why naive sparse-FDA pipelines give biased, under-dispersed inference. Borrowing from Y and doing it as *proper multiple* imputation rather than mean imputation is a sensible and well-argued fix.
- **Excellent 2×2 conceptual scaffolding.** Decomposing methods into {mean, multiple} × {conditional, unconditional} (MeU/MeC/MuU/MuC) and simulating all four is pedagogically strong and isolates *what* drives each property (MISE vs. type-1 error vs. power). This is the paper's clearest contribution.
- **Broad, competent simulation study** (true vs. estimated parameters; varying N, m, J; heavy-tailed robustness check; logistic; benchmarking against fdapace/PACE, refund pfr, funreg), with a real tool behind it (R, `fcr`/`face`).
- **Honest about limitations** — the authors themselves surface the type-1 problem and the thinness of the theory.

## Weaknesses

**1. The flagship method has a serious, self-documented validity flaw in exactly the regime it is needed for.** Table 8 is damning and under-weighted in the main text. Under the null (w = 0) the *conditional* imputers MuC and MeC have rejection rates of 0.318/0.118 (MuC at m = 2, 5) and up to 0.786 vs. nominal 0.05. Conditioning on Y to impute X injects spurious X–Y dependence, so the association you then test is contaminated — a "double-use of Y" / reverse-causation problem that Rubin's rules do *not* fix (Rubin's rules account for curve-reconstruction variance, not for the structural bias introduced by conditioning on the response). The authors' remedy — "only use conditional imputation if there is a priori evidence of an association, or for larger m" — effectively tells readers not to use their headline method in the sparse (m = 2–5) setting that motivated the whole paper. Meanwhile the valid (well-calibrated) MuU has lower power. This tension is never resolved, and it directly undermines the abstract's claim that MISFIT enables "a more accurate assessment of the uncertainty" as its marquee benefit.

**2. The application's inference is infected by the same flaw.** The macrocephaly result reports p = 0.000000 from MuC, but the simulations just established that at these sparsity levels MuC's nominal type-1 error can run 0.12–0.79. The "significant" coefficient is only safe here because the true signal happens to be large; the p-value is not trustworthy as written. The one-sample bootstrap checks the *width* of the bands against resampled curves, but that does not validate the null-calibration, and resampling subjects (800, ~6.5 obs each) does not re-sample the missingness mechanism. The "average + contrast = velocity" reading of a sign-changing coefficient function is a plausible but somewhat post-hoc interpretation.

**3. The theory assumes away the hard part and is otherwise a repackaging.** (a) Theorem 3 (consistency) holds only under Assumption 5 (the imputation parameters — µX, CX, σδ², CXY, σY² — are consistently estimable) plus Assumption 4 (β in the span of the first p eigenfunctions). But Sec 3.1.2 shows that with *estimated* parameters MuC is biased and does **not** become unbiased by increasing J precisely because the imputation parameters are poorly estimated at small m, N. So the theorem's premise is exactly the ingredient the simulations show is the practical failure mode; the result is close to tautological ("if the imputation model is right, then this estimator is consistent") and says nothing about the rates or the estimability of the imputation model from sparse data — the genuinely open question, which they defer to future work. Fixed mi ≡ m in the theorem also does not match the highly variable (1–23 obs, many single-observed) designs of the real data. (b) Theorems 1–2 are a faithful but largely classical restatement: the authors themselves note the key quantity ‖C⁻¹ᐟ²∆‖ was known "at least as early as the 70s" (Kuo 1975; Bogachev; Rao–Varadarajan 1963). The contribution is the mapping to the logistic/GLM-compatibility question, which is reasonable packaging rather than new mathematics. Note the "grey area" (‖C⁻¹ᐟ²∆‖ finite but ‖C⁻¹∆‖ = ∞) — where β is only a *discontinuous* linear functional and the model does not actually hold in L² — is the natural regime for smooth covariance functions and gets a paragraph, not a resolution. The model is often *not* even well-specified, and this is under-explored.

**4. Strong and possibly misspecified data assumptions for EHR.** A single *shared* Gaussian process with *common* covariance plus homoscedastic Gaussian measurement error. Yet ~14% of subjects have **one** measurement (their "imputed curve" is then essentially the population prior, not a subject trajectory — unaddressed), and the authors elsewhere stress EHR measurements carry errors "of varying type and degree." The robustness check only perturbs tail weight (t₄); there is no stress-test of heteroscedasticity, non-Gaussian functional shape, or subject-specific mean functions — all plausible in this data.

**5. Comparison and methodology details.** The "MISFIT vs PACE" comparison (Table 7) is muddied because their MeU and the fdapace PACE use different imputation-parameter estimators, so the difference is not cleanly attributable to imputation strategy. The most relevant competitors — *Bayesian* joint-imputation models (Thompson & Rosen 2008; Kowal et al. 2019), which the intro cites as the "notable exception" — are discussed but never benchmarked numerically, leaving the strongest alternative untested. FPC count J is chosen by eye per setting (the optimal J differs by method in the logistic tables); there is no data-driven truncation/selection procedure.

## Bottom line

A competent, well-motivated, and useful methodology paper whose clean 2×2 framing gives real conceptual value and whose tool is practically deployable. But its central "inference" claim is undercut by its own simulations (conditional imputation badly breaks null calibration in the important sparse regime), its consistency theory sidesteps the actual difficulty, and its "theoretical novelty" is a re-clothing of classical Gaussian-measure results. Sound within narrowed claims; the validity wrinkle keeps it from stronger claims. A solid Q2-tier FDA/EHR contribution, not a field-moving one.

# Critique of *"Highly Irregular Functional Generalized Linear Regression with Electronic Health Records"* (Petrovich, Reimherr, Daymont; arXiv:1805.08518v2)

## Stated contribution, in my own words

The paper offers **MISFIT** (Multiple Imputation of Sparsely-sampled Functions at Irregular Times): a framework for *scalar‑on‑function (GLM/logistic) regression* when the functional *covariates* are sparsely and irregularly observed (as in EHRs). The central move is to stop treating the problem as a functional‑data "impute‑then‑model" task and instead recast the unobserved curve values as a **missing‑data problem solved by multiple imputation**. Concretely, for each subject the latent curve $X_i(t)$ is drawn repeatedly (K=10) from its conditional distribution given the *observed points and the outcome* $Y_i$ — a Gaussian‑process/concurrent‑regression model — the regression is fitted on each completed data set, and the parameter estimates plus their within‑**and**‑between‑imputation covariance are pooled via **Rubin's rules**. Because the imputation is done *conditionally on the response* and repeatedly, the resulting estimator is consistent and, crucially, the standard errors / confidence bands / p‑values correctly reflect the uncertainty the imputation introduces — something the dominant mean‑imputation methods (PACE/Yao et al. 2005; spline analogues in *refund*; Di et al.; Goldsmith et al.) do not, and which the paper's simulations and a Type‑I‑error study show badly misstate (mean imputation → biased coefficients and artificially small SEs / inflated rejection under H0). It also proves a pair of **Cameron–Martin "equivalence of Gaussian measures" theorems** (Thms. 1–2) characterizing exactly when *separately* imputing the two outcome groups stays compatible with a logistic (or canonical‑link) model ($\|C_X^{-1}\Delta\|<\infty$), tying the FDA to the classical orthonogality‑of‑Gaussian‑measures / perfect‑classification literature (Rao & Varadarajan; Kuo; Delage & Hall; Dai et al.). Finally it applies the method to ~7,400 children's EHR head‑circumference trajectories, predicting macrocephaly‑related pathology, showing elevated risk is carried by both mean head circumference and the velocity of head growth.

## What is genuinely new

- **The reframing + the conditional‑on‑outcome multiple‑imputation variant (MuC/"MISFIT") for the functional *covariate*.** The cited prior work falls into two camps: (a) mean‑imputation / score‑imputation (PACE, splines, *refund*, Di et al.) which ignores the outcome and yields no downstream uncertainty; and (b) a few missing‑data‑flavoured pieces — He et al. 2011 (functional mixed model, MIM, response‑side), PreDA (NIPALS, a two‑state Markov missingness mechanism), Crambes & Henchiri 2017 and Ferraty et al. 2013 — which all impute the **response**, not the functional predictor, or are restricted to the linear model. Doing multiple imputation of the *functional covariate*, *conditioned on the outcome*, and then carrying the resulting uncertainty through a GLM via Rubin's rules is a synthesis none of the cited papers actually does; this is the paper's real core novelty. The accompanying simulation showing the naive PACE plug‑in bias and the Type‑I error inflation of mean imputation is convincing and, as a diagnostic, novel.
- **The Cameron–Martin compatibility theorem** (Thm. 1 logistic / Thm. 2 continuous), giving the precise boundary at which group‑conditional imputation remains consistent with a logistic model and the degenerate "perfect‑classification" edge. Useful, correctly stated, and a good application‑relevant bridge from classical probability into FDA, though its ingredients are classical.
- **A concrete EHR application** (macrocephaly from head‑circumference curves, using *fcr* concurrent regression for the imputation model) — interesting and a new empirical result.

## What is not new / incremental, and where the novelty is thin

- **The multiple‑imputation machinery (Rubin's rules, within/between covariance pooling) is textbook** (Rubin, Schafer, Royston) and merely repurposed; nothing new is done here.
- **The conditioning distribution** in §2.2 is a direct consequence of multivariate‑normal/Gaussian‑process conditioning — standard; only its application to the functional‑score case and its pairing with regression are new.
- **The imputation‑model estimation reuses existing concurrent‑regression machinery** (*fcr*/Leroux; the spline smoothing is the same as *fadapce*/Dai et al. 2019) rather than introducing a new estimator.
- **The asymptotic theory is preliminary, and — importantly — it does not actually cover the motivating data.** The consistency Theorem 3 requires *fixed* points per curve (Assumption 7), *no* truncation error (Assumption 4: $β(t)$ in the span of the first $p$ eigenvectors), distinct eigenvalues, and — critically — *assumes* consistent estimates of the imputation‑model parameters (Assumption 5). The authors themselves concede the theory is "by no means complete," obtain *no* minimax rates, and leave the key quantity (estimation of the imputation model) out of scope. Since their entire motivation is that $m_i$ varies wildly (1–23 in the real data), the consistency result sits in a different regime from the data on which the method is sold.
- **The theorems on Gaussian‑measure equivalence largely repackage classical RKHS/Cameron–Martin facts** for an FDA readership (the supplement even defers the detail to Bogachev).

## A significant practical caveat the authors flag

The recommended MuC ("MISFIT") method **inflates Type‑I error under the null in the sparsest regime it targets** (Table 8: at $m=2$, $w=0$ the rejection rate is 0.318 vs. nominal 0.05; the *unconditional* mean‑imputation MeU at 0.050 is actually the most calibrated there). The authors candidly note this and attribute it to residual leakage of the response signal into the covariate imputation. That is a real weakness of the "headline" method precisely where the method is most needed — a good‑faith self‑criticism, but one that tempers the consistency/validity story.

## Bottom line

A competent, well‑motivated, well‑executed methods paper. Its genuine contribution is a **clever, non‑trivial reframing** — treating sparse functional‑covariate imputation as a missing‑data problem solved by *response‑conditional multiple imputation* with Rubin's‑rules inference, plus a nice compatibility theorem connecting it to Gaussian‑measure equivalence — and a clean EHR application. Nothing, however, is a new tool from first principles: every component (MI, Rubin, GP conditioning, concurrent regression) pre‑exists, the theory is preliminary and doesn't cover the varying‑$m$ real data, the recommended variant is null‑miscalibrated in its core regime, and the estimation‑model quality (whose consistency is assumed) is the load‑bearing, unexamined step. It is a solid, credible, moderately novel synthesis rather than a landmark contribution.

## Methodology: enough detail to reproduce the central experiment

### Model

Scalar-on-function linear regression with one functional covariate:

$$Y_i = \alpha + \int \beta(t) X_i(t)\,dt + \varepsilon_i,\quad \varepsilon_i \sim N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2)\ \text{iid}.$$

$X_i(t) \in L^2[0,1]$ is assumed iid Gaussian process with mean $\mu_X$ and covariance $C_X$. Observations: $x_{ij} = X_i(t_{ij}) + \delta_{ij}$, $\delta_{ij} \sim N(0,\sigma_\delta^2)$ iid, with $m_i$ observed time points $t_{ij}$. Goal: estimate $\beta(t)$.

### Imputation (core of MIsFIT)

The conditional $(X_i(t), X_i(s)) \mid Y_i, \{x_{ij}\}$ is Gaussian (since everything is jointly Gaussian). The authors draw $K$ full curve (or score) replications from this conditional. For the *curve-level* variant, the conditional has mean $\mu_X(t)+\mathbf{a}_i(t)^\top \mathbf{B}_i^{-1}\mathbf{d}_i$ and covariance $C_X(t,s) - \mathbf{a}_i(t)^\top \mathbf{B}_i^{-1}\mathbf{a}_i(s)$ where $\mathbf{d}_i = (Y_i - \mu_Y, x_{i1}-\mu_X(t_{i1}), \ldots)$, $\mathbf{a}_i(t) = (C_{XY}(t), C_X(t,t_{i1}), \ldots)$ and $\mathbf{B}_i^{-1}$ is a bordered matrix with diagonal $C_X(t_{ij},t_{ik}) + \mathbf{1}_{(j=k)}\sigma_\delta^2$.

For the *score-level* variant (the one used in all simulations), the conditional of the *centered* scores $\hat\xi_{ij} = \langle\mu_y, v_j\rangle + \xi_{ij}$ is drawn with a different $\mathbf A_i$ matrix and with the score covariance $\text{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_p)$ replacing $C_X(t,s)$.

**Four estimators are compared:**

- **MuC** (Multiple-Conditional = MIsFIT): K imputations from the conditional of scores given $Y_i$.
- **MeC** (Mean-Conditional): single BLUP-type imputation (the mean of the above) — this is essentially PACE with the $Y$-conditioning.
- **MuU** (Multiple-Unconditional): K imputations from the marginal of scores (no conditioning on $Y$).
- **MeU** (Mean-Unconditional): the plain PACE / mean-imputation estimator.

For each of the $K$ imputed datasets, fit a truncated FPCA regression keeping the first $J$ FPCs: $\hat\beta^{(k)}(t) = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \sum_{i=1}^N \hat\xi_{ij}^{(k)}Y_i/(\hat\lambda_j N)\, v_j(t)$. Combine via Rubin's rules:

$$\hat{\mathsf W}(t,s)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \widehat{\text{Cov}}(\hat\beta^{(k)}(t),\hat\beta^{(k)}(s)),\quad \hat{\mathsf B}(t,s)=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\hat\beta^{(k)}(t)-\bar\beta(t))(\hat\beta^{(k)}(s)-\bar\beta(s)),$$
$$\hat{\mathsf C}_\beta(t,s) = \hat{\mathsf W}(t,s) + (1+1/K)\hat{\mathsf B}(t,s), \quad \hat\beta(t) = \bar\beta(t).$$

### Estimating the imputation parameters

The paper does not propose new estimators for $\{C_X, C_{XY}\}$; it relies on existing tools:

- For *estimated-parameter* simulations and the application, fit a **functional concurrent regression** via the R package `fcr` (Leroux et al. 2017/2018) to the model

$$X_i(t) = f_0(t) + f_1(t)Y_i + b_i(t),\qquad b_i \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(0, C_b),\quad X_i(t_{ij}) = f_0(t_{ij}) + f_1(t_{ij})Y_i + b_i(t_{ij}) + \delta_{ij}.$$

From this, $\hat\mu_X(t) = f_0(t)+f_1(t)\hat\mu_Y$, $\hat C_X(t,s) = f_1(t)f_1(s)\sigma_Y^2 + C_b(t,s)$ (curve level) or $C_b$ (score level), $\hat C_{XY}(t) = f_1(t)\sigma_Y^2$, and $\hat\sigma_\delta^2 = \hat\sigma_{\delta,b}^2$. The score-level parameters $\{\hat\lambda_j,\hat v_j\}$ come from a spectral decomposition of $\hat C_X$; in the linear case $\hat\lambda_j = \hat f_1(t_j)\sigma_{Y,\delta}^2\lambda_j + \lambda_{b,j}$, in the logistic case $\hat C_X = C_b$ (so the two groups give the same covariance — no $C_{XY}, \mu_Y, \sigma_Y^2$ needed). The unconditional estimators instead use `face.sparse` (`face` package, Xiao et al. 2019) to estimate $C_X$ and $\mu_X$ without $Y$.

### Central linear simulation (Section 3.1.2 — the one that drives Tables 4–7)

- **Covariance**: Matérn $C_X(t,s) = \sigma^2/\Gamma(\nu)^{2\nu-1}\,(\sqrt{2\nu}|t-s|/\rho)^{\nu}K_\nu(\sqrt{2\nu}|t-s|/\rho)$ with $\nu=5/2$, $\rho=0.5$, $\sigma^2=1$.
- **True curves** on a grid $M=100$ equally spaced points in $[0,1]$, plus per-point noise $\sigma_\delta^2 = 0.5$.
- **Signal**: $\beta(t) = w\sin(2\pi t)$ with $w \in \{0, 5, 10\}$.
- **Outcome**: model (1) with $\alpha=0$, $\sigma_\varepsilon^2=1$.
- **Sampling**: for each $i$ draw $m$ random indices from the $M=100$ grid; $m \in \{2,5,10,20\}$.
- **Sample size**: $N \in \{100,200,400,800\}$.
- **Truncation**: $J \in \{1,\ldots,6\}$.
- **K** = 10 imputations, score-level.
- **Replications**: 100 (estimated-parameter) or 1000 (true-parameter) per cell; report **median** ISE over replications (not mean, because small $m$ gives occasional outliers).
- One parameter varies per table, defaults $N=200$, $m=2$, $J=4$ (true-parameter) or $J=2$ (estimated-parameter).

**Type I error / power (3.1.4):** 500 sims per cell, $N=200$, $J=2$ fixed, $m \in \{2,5,10,20\}$, $w \in \{0,1,2\}$ ($w=0$ = null, $w=1,2$ = power). Test statistic $T = \| \hat\beta \|^2 \sim N\,\sum \hat\lambda_j^* \chi^2(1)$, i.e. the eigen-decomposition of $\hat C_\beta$ used to form a chi-square-like statistic; p-values via `imhof()` in `CompQuadForm`. Nominal $\alpha = 0.05$.

**Multivariate-t robustness (3.2):** replace the iid Gaussian score sample with iid multivariate $t_4$ with same mean and covariance; everything else as in 3.1.2.

**Logistic (3.3):** $Y_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.5)$, $X_i \mid Y_i = y \sim N(\mu_y, C_X(t,s))$ with $\mu_0 \equiv 0$ and $\mu_1 = v_1(t) + v_2(t)$ (which satisfies $\|C_X^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_0)\|_{L^2} < \infty$, hence $\beta^* = C_X^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_0) = v_1/\lambda_1 + v_2/\lambda_2$ is in $L^2$). Same Matérn $C_X$, $\sigma_\delta^2 = 0.5$, $M = 100$, $m$ random grid samples per curve, $K=10$ imputations. Default $N=400, m=2, J=2$; vary $N \in \{100,\ldots,800\}$, $m \in \{2,5,10,20\}$, $J \in \{1,\ldots,6\}$, 100 replications. Imputation done *separately within* $Y=0$ and $Y=1$ (no common parameters), as in (5): conditionals with $\hat\mu_y$, $\hat C_b$ from the group-specific fits.

**Benchmarks (3.1.3):** compare to **PACE** as implemented in the R package `fdapace` (Dai et al. 2019) and to `pfr` / `funreg` from `refund` (Goldsmith et al. 2018; Dziak & Shiyko 2016), all using *estimated* imputation parameters, 100 sims.

### EHR application (Section 4)

- Data: 74,428 children aged 3 days to 3 y from a large primary-care EHR; 85 with macrocephaly-related pathology, the rest controls.
- Subsample $N=800$: all 85 cases + **715 controls selected by stratified sampling** to roughly match the case sampling-frequency distribution (a 1:7.2 ratio, mean 6.5 measurements per subject).
- Response: binary pathology indicator $Y_i$. Covariate: **head-circumference / height ratio** (to adjust for height).
- Imputation: score-level multiple-imputation MIsFIT with **$K=10$**; imputed parameters obtained from `fcr`.
- Per-imputation fit: logistic scalar-on-function regression, truncated FPCA.
- Uncertainty: Rubin's rules for the coefficient function; **95% pointwise CIs** shown; and a **one-sample bootstrap** (resampling subjects; refitting the whole imputation + regression pipeline) used to sanity-check the width of the bands.
- Inference on the global effect: p-values in Table 13 (all four approaches give $p = 0$ for McC, MeU, MuC; 0.123 for Mu).

Everything the paper needs to reproduce is in the body; the R code itself is not reproduced in the paper (the supplementary only contains technical notes (A) on Cameron–Martin theory and proofs (B–D)).

## Assumptions the methodology papers over

I read "papers over" as: explicitly or implicitly assumed in the analysis but not flagged as a potential weakness, or assumed without ever being tested.

1. **Missingness is MAR (or MCAR) w.r.t. $Y$ and the unobserved $X$.** Section 1.2 explicitly says this is "implicitly assumed ... [but] we make the same implicit assumption, without formally defining the missing data mechanism in our procedure." A child with a single head measurement at age ~2 y may be missing precisely because the child is healthy; this is MNAR in the data-generating sense. MIsFIT inherits the bias any MAR-multiple-imputation scheme has under MNAR, with no sensitivity study.

2. **Gaussian measurement error $\delta_{ij}$, IID and homoskedastic in $(i,j)$.** Real EHR growth measurements are skewed and the variance changes with age (neonates vs. 2 y olds). The multivariate-$t_4$ robustness check (3.2) stress-tests *heavy tails in the curve* but not in the *measurement noise*.

3. **IID Gaussian process for $X_i$ (or, in the application, the head-circumference/height ratio) with a *single* shared covariance $C_X$.** No between-subject heterogeneity in $C_X$ (no FPC-level random effect on the covariance, no mixture). This is a strong assumption for EHR data spanning 3 days to 3 y.

4. **The $fcr$ imputation model (7) — $X_i(t) = f_0(t) + f_1(t)Y_i + b_i(t)$ — is the *right* shape.** In particular: (i) the $Y$-effect is *multiplicative in $t$* via $f_1(t)$, (ii) the interaction is *additive in $Y_i$* (not saturating/sigmoidal, despite $Y$ being binary), (iii) no other covariates (height, age, weight) enter the imputation. If $f_1$ is misspecified, the conditional (5) is misspecified and K imputations from a wrong conditional does not fix bias (they only fix the variance of the estimator).

5. **Canonical link is required (not optional).** Theorem 1 (and the companion for exponentials in Theorem 2) shows the *necessary and sufficient* condition for $Y|X$ to be a logistic (or, more generally, canonical-form exponential-family) model is a Cameron–Martin condition involving $C_X^{-1}\Delta$. The authors point out the converse ("swap the conditioning") in §2.4 but then say "this appears to be a serious open mathematical question" — meaning that under a non-canonical link (e.g. probit) the Gaussian imputation is *generally* incompatible with the GLM and the procedure is formally unjustified, yet no non-canonical-link simulation is reported.

6. **Score-level imputation with $p$ FPCs and the *centered* scores.** The paper notes (in §2.3, last paragraph) that the *raw* scores do not separate the group-mean effect from the residual; it recommends centering first. But: (i) centering introduces a *parameter to estimate* ($\mu_y$) inside the imputation model, (ii) the choice of $J$ (number of FPCs) is left to the practitioner, and the simulation shows the method's accuracy is *extremely* sensitive to $J$ when $m$ is small (compare Tables 1 vs. 4 at $J=4, w=5$: MuC drops from MISE 0.10 (true params) to 5098 (estimated params)). There is no data-driven rule for $J$.

7. **Number of imputations $K = 10$ is fixed without justification.** The asymptotic theory (Section 5) assumes the *conditional* distribution is known; the *finite-K* bias of Rubin's rules (and the known under-estimation of between-imputation variance at moderate $K$) is not quantified anywhere. The simulation only ever uses $K=10$.

8. **The truncation of the FPCA basis at $J$ in the *final regression* is treated as if it were a fixed design choice, not a parameter to be estimated/regularized.** Assumption 1.4 in Section 5 makes this explicit ("$\beta(t)$ lies in the span of the first $p$ eigenfunctions of $C_X$") and the authors then say "clearly not true in general and at best an approximation," with no way to check it on real data.

9. **The "consistency" of Theorem 3 is conditional on Assumption 1.5 — consistent estimation of *every* imputation parameter ($\mu_X, C_X, C_{XY}, \mu_Y, \sigma_Y^2, \sigma_\delta^2$) in $o_P(1)$ norm.** The paper *delegates* this to "any number of methods (i.e. splines, local smoothing, etc.)" and then uses `fcr` (spline-based) in the simulations. There's no rate analysis; a slow rate on the spline estimates (say $N^{-1/10}$) is consistent but still dominates the regression's $N^{-1/2}$ rate, which is exactly what Tables 4–7 show empirically (MuC is no longer best when parameters are estimated and $w=0$).

10. **IID subjects with a common $m_i \equiv m$ (Assumption 1.7).** Theory in Section 5 is for a *fixed* number of points per curve; the simulation only varies $m$ across all subjects identically. The application has wildly varying $m$ (1 to >20), and the bootstrap CI sanity-check is the *only* coverage evidence for real data. There is no simulation with heterogeneous $m_i$.

11. **Distinct first $p+1$ eigenvalues (Assumption 1.6).** The authors acknowledge this "can be relaxed (Petrovich and Reimherr 2017)" but the consistency theorem as stated requires it. Real growth curves with a Matérn covariance can have close eigenvalues, and no eigenvalue-gap diagnostic is reported.

12. **The test statistic $T=\|\hat\beta\|^2 \sim N\sum\hat\lambda_j^*\chi^2(1)$ in §3.1.4** is the asymptotic chi-square mixture; finite-sample calibration at $J=2$ is asserted to be "close to" 0.05 (Table 8 confirms for MeU at moderate $m$), but the *type-1 error of MuC inflates above nominal* at small $m$, and the authors flag this only as "an additional not-so-obvious result" rather than deriving when it happens. The p-values in the EHR application (Table 13: $p=0$ for three of four methods) therefore rest on this same finite-sample behaviour, which is not formally controlled.

13. **The "stratified sampling to match case and control sampling frequency" in the application (Section 4) is not a probability-calibrated procedure.** It matches empirical visit-count distributions but not, in any stated sense, the conditional $X|Y$ or the missingness mechanism. The 1:7.2 case-control ratio and the resulting 6.5 average measurements per subject mean the logistic model is fit on a *re-weighted* subsample of the original 74,428 children; this introduces the standard case-control bias in the intercept and any marginal (non-conditional) claims about the effect. MIsFIT estimates *conditional* parameters so this is arguably OK for $\beta(t)$, but the $fcr$ imputation is fit on the *same re-weighted subsample*, meaning the imputation model is also fit on a biased sample. No sensitivity analysis to the choice of 715 controls vs. the full control pool.

14. **No cross-validation or external validation.** Every reported p-value and CI comes from the same 800-subject subsample. The bootstrap is one-sample (resampling subjects with replacement within the 800), which measures sampling variability but not methodological misspecification.

15. **The application's effect interpretation** ("velocity of head growth drives the distinction between the groups", Section 4) rests on the *joint* reading of the sign change in $\hat\beta(t)$ plus the contrast decomposition in Figure 5 (right). This decomposition is post-hoc, and the authors do not formalise the contrast as a pre-specified secondary outcome; the $p$-values in Table 13 are for the global test only.

16. **The "fcr spline" estimator is one specific instantiation.** The Conclusions candidly say there are "multiple justifiable approaches to estimate these parameters ... our results are based on the use of `fcr`, which employs a spline-based approach. However, we did see some preliminary evidence in favor of the local polynomial smoothing approach used by `fdapace`. These differences were not explored in depth." So the central experiment is *not* the MIsFIT procedure but (MIsFIT + `fcr`), and the general MIsFIT claim is conditional on the quality of the imputation-parameter estimator, which is not characterized.

---

**Bottom line.** For a reader wanting to reproduce: the linear-model pipeline is fully specified (Matérn parameters, $w$ values, $N, m, J, K$, `fcr` + `face` for imputation params, FPCA truncation, Rubin's rules with $K=10$, median ISE). The *central claim* that MIsFIT (MuC) beats PACE (MeC/MeU) and that it gives honest CIs is well-supported in the simulation but is heavily conditional on (a) the `fcr` imputation model being the right form, (b) Gaussian curves, (c) MAR missingness, (d) the user picking a $J$ in a narrow sweet spot, and (e) $K=10$ being enough. The application is a single-subsample one-shot with a post-hoc effect decomposition, no external validation, and imputation-model misspecification that is not stress-tested.

# Evaluation of MISFIT: Highly Irregular Functional Generalized Linear Regression

## My understanding of the paper's logical argument

The paper (Petrovich, Reimherr, Daymont, 2019) tackles functional scalar-on-function regression when the *functional* covariates are sparsely and irregularly sampled — the regime of electronic health records, where a child's head circumference might be measured 1–23 times across 3 years (mode = 1).

**The motivating diagnosis of the incumbent (PACE).** PACE and its relatives (refund, fdapace) impute the missing curve points via *unconditional* mean imputation — pooled smoothing over curves with no conditioning on the outcome Y. The authors argue (Sec. 2.2, eq. 2–3) that plugging PACE BLUPs into a downstream FPCA estimator is *biased* unless either β(t)≡0 or the average number of points per curve grows to 0 as n→∞ — i.e. consistency is not guaranteed. Worse, because imputation uncertainty is not propagated, mean imputation yields understated standard errors, inflated p‑values, and (as their simulations show) inflated Type I error.

**The core reframe (the contribution).** Sparse functional data is reinterpreted as a *missing data problem*. Under MAR, impute the missing curve points from the *conditional* distribution X_i(t) | Y_i, {x_ik}, a procedure they call MISFIT (Multipl**e** Imputation of Sparsel**y**-sampled Functions at Irregular Times). Two claims rest on it:
1. **Consistency**: as a multiple-imputation procedure (Rubin's rules for within + between covariance), it can yield a consistent β̂ and, crucially, a *reliable* estimate of the imputation uncertainty (wide CIs) — something mean imputation cannot do.
2. **Compatibility (the theoretical center piece, Thms 1–2)**: a *Gaussian* imputation model for X|Y is a *compatible* model (i.e. yields the correct predictive distribution for Y|X) for a logistic/GLM if and only if a Cameron–Martin/orthogonality condition on the covariance operator holds. They translate the Kuo(1975)/Bogachev(1998) equivalence-of-Gaussian-measures question into a clean condition on ‖C_X^{−1/2}Δ‖ and ‖C_X^{−1}Δ‖ over L2[0,1], producing three regimes (degenerate / non-degenerate-but-Gaussian-imputation-incompatible / compatible). Canonical link (the Gaussian case for Y too) makes the Gaussian imputation "boxed." This is the genuinely interesting, self-contained piece.

**Evidence.** A large simulation suite (linear + logistic; varying N, m, J, signal strength; true vs. estimated imputation parameters; t-distribution robustness; Type I / power) compares four methods — Mean-Conditional (MeC), Multiple-Conditional (MuC), Mean-Unconditional (MeU), Multiple-Unconditional (MuU) — plus benchmarks PACE, pfr, funreg. Findings: MuC (MISFIT's core) has the best MISE in sparse, high-signal settings and converges as N grows; mean imputation gives artificially small CIs and inflated Type I error. The application to macrocephaly (800 subjects, 85 cases) finds an elevated pathology risk associated with both overall head-circumference level and head-growth velocity.

**Theory closeout (Sec. 5, Thm 3).** A *preliminary* consistency result for the FPCA-based estimate sup_t|β̂(t)−β(t)|=o_P(1) as N→∞, but under notably restrictive Assumption 1: β in the span of the first p eigenfunctions, a full set of imputation parameters assumed consistent, and a *fixed, equal* number m of points per curve.

## Feedback and critique

**Strengths.**
- The reframe — "treat sparse functional prediction as MAR imputation and let Rubin's rules do the uncertainty bookkeeping" — is clean and the practical payoff is real: mean imputation produces pathologically narrow CIs and the simulations convincingly show inflated Type I error (Table 8) and, in the application, near-invisible bands for the mean-imputation methods (Fig. 4).
- The Cameron–Martin compatibility theorem (Thm 1/2 and the Section A exposition) is the paper's most original and self-contained contribution. Reducing "is a Gaussian imputation model compatible with this regression?" to two computable operator norms is a nice, general statement that stands on its own and connects FDA (Delagle & Hall perfect classification; Mirshahi privacy) to missing-data theory. This elevates the paper beyond "we applied multiple imputation to FDA."
- The simulation battery is genuinely comprehensive and honest about where the advantages are real (sparse + high signal) and where the methods collapse together (dense).

**Weaknesses.**
1. **The theory does not match the motivating data.** The consistency theorem (Thm 3) assumes a *fixed* and *identical* m for every subject and only treats the FPCA linear case. The macrocephaly data — the whole reason the paper exists — has m from 1 to 23 with a mode of 1 and heavy irregularity. So the one rigorous result the paper offers does not formally cover the application; the application is supported purely by simulation analogy and the heuristic "more imputed scores → lower variance." The abstract's claim of "consistent estimation" is therefore doing more work than the theory supports for their own data.
2. **The conditional-imputation Type I error penalty is real and only partially addressed.** Table 8 shows MuC rejecting at 0.32 under the null (w=0) when m=2, even overshooting 0.14–0.56 at m=5–10 before settling to nominal at m=20. The paper correctly explains this (conditioning on Y leaks the group difference into the imputed covariate; the detected "signal" is residuum). But their fix — "only use conditional imputation if there's a priori evidence the variables are related" — is circular for an exploratory application, and the honest recommendation is that the *unconditional* multiple-imputation (MuU) is the safer default under the null. This is a tension with the paper's own headline method (MuC) and deserves more prominence than a one-paragraph caveat; it actually complicates the "MISFIT is the method" story.
3. **Imputation-model dependence is under-explored.** The authors note (Sec. 3.1.2, Conclusions) that results are quite sensitive to whether imputation parameters come from fcR vs. fdapace-style local polynomial smoothing, and to the choice of J, yet the paper essentially commits to fcR and a fixed J without a principled selection rule (no CV-based guidance for J). For a method whose stated advantage is robust uncertainty quantification, leaving J-selection ad-hoc is a meaningful gap.
4. **The Gaussian-imputation requirement is a quiet restriction.** For a GLM with a *non-*canonical link the Gaussian imputation model is explicitly *incompatible* (Sec. 2.4 calls this an "interesting" but open question, noting Y|X gets "boxed" into a canonical link). So the "generalized linear regression in the title" is in fact only the canonical-link / conjugate case, and the title slightly overpromises.
5. **Application concerns.** Near-perfect separation of the imputed case/control curves (Fig. 3) and a rare outcome (85/800 ≈ 10.6%) means the estimated coefficient functions are large and the logistic likelihood is fragile; the p-values reported for the mean-imputation methods (0.0) are rightly dismissed, but the MuU p-value (0.123) vs. the MuC/MeC/MeU 0.0000 in Table 13 is presented somewhat selectively — a reader can't tell how stable that 0.123 is without a sensitivity analysis. The one-sample-bootstrap CI check is a good idea but a thin substitute for a full model-diagnostic or cross-validation on the held-out cases.
6. Minor: a typo in the definition of β̌(t) (sec. 2.2: the last sum is written as a sum over i in both numerator terms, i.e. it should be an average); Assumption 1 item 6 (distinct eigenvalues) is later said to be relaxable but is left in place.

**Net assessment.** A coherent, well-motivated methods paper whose best asset (the Cameron–Martin compatibility theorem) is truly novel and general, and whose central procedure (MISFIT/MuC) is shown empirically to fix mean imputation's two real pathologies (bias and understated uncertainty). But the formal guarantee is narrow (fixed equal m, linear FPCA only) and does not cover the motivating EHR setting; the conditional-imputation Type I error inflation meaningfully muddies the "use MISFIT" recommendation; and the "GLM" framing is effectively canonical-link-only. Solid, publication-quality work with a genuine but bounded contribution.

### The strongest quantitative claim

### Audit

**1. Fairness of the comparison**
- The MISFIT-vs-PACE contrast is *methodologically thinner than presented.* Both use the same FPCA-scores regression; they differ only in *how* the imputation parameters are estimated — MISFIT uses `fcr` (Leroux et al. 2018), `fdapace` uses local-polynomial smoothing (Xiao et al. 2018). - Different default hyperparameters are silently used in the two simulation settings (J = 4 with true parameters vs. J = 2 with estimated parameters), and J = 4 was chosen *post hoc* from Table 1 — a mild form of data snooping on the model-dimension hyperparameter.
- The m = 20 column of Table 7 (where funreg = 8.72, competitive with everyone), and the w = 0 columns of Tables 4–6 (where MuU or MeU dominates), are downplayed in the narrative.

**2. Are the baselines current?**
For a 2018–2019 paper, the baselines (fdapace 2019, refund 2018, funreg 2016) are appropriately up-to-date for the field *as of 2018*. The 2019 dates are a minor anachronism for the v1 (May 2018) submission but not for the v2 (Oct 2019) under review. No major alternative (e.g., Bayesian functional GLMs of Kowal et al., which the paper does cite) is included as a benchmark — a notable omission given the paper's own critique of two-step methods points precisely at the Bayesians.

This is a defensible but self-serving choice, and it is reported with no interquartile range, no bootstrap CI on the MISE itself, and no paired significance test (e.g., a Wilcoxon signed-rank or permutation test) across the four methods. The MISE gap of ~1–2 units in the estimated-parameter setting, with 100 replications and heavy right-skew, is not statistically justified.

A competent, well-scoped statistical-methods paper addressing a real gap (uncertainty propagation for sparse functional GLMs). The headline quantitative comparison is real but **under-supported: medians over 100 reps, no CIs or paired tests, a straw-man funreg baseline, and a "MISFIT vs PACE" distinction that is an estimator-detail difference rather than a substantive methodological one.** The consistency promise is theorem-backed only under stronger assumptions than the data at hand satisfy. Not field-shaping; a solid incremental contribution.

## [1805.08518v2-weak-point] Weakest link in the argument


I read the full 34-page paper (Petrovich, Reimherr, Daymont — "Highly Irregular Functional Generalized Linear Regression with Electronic Health Records," arXiv:1805.08518v2). I traced the argument chain and isolated the step where the evidence stops carrying the conclusion.

## The argument chain (in one breath)
1. **Problem:** sparse/irregular functional covariates break existing mean-imputation methods (PACE, `refund`), both for bias and for *under-stated* standard errors; they also need points-per-curve to grow with N for consistency.
2. **Method (MISFIT):** reframe as missing data; do *multiple imputation* of the functional covariate by drawing from the Gaussian conditional $X_i(t)\mid Y_i,\{x_{ik}\}$, with a Camel–Martin / orthogonality condition (Theorems 1–2) guaranteeing that the GLM is compatible with a Gaussian imputation model.
3. **Theory (Theorem 3):** consistency of the FPCA plug-in under *fixed* $m$, so the "no growing-m" promise holds (though under strong, hedged assumptions).
4. **Evidence:** extensive MISE simulations (Tables 1–12), a type-1-error study (Table 8), and a real logistic application to macrocephaly (Tables 13, Figs 3–5).
5. **Conclusion:** MISFIT (MuC) propagates imputation uncertainty, giving "more accurate assessment of the uncertainty of parameter estimates that most methods currently cannot accomplish," and in the real data "clearly demonstrates" that macrocephalic pathology is associated with overall head circumference and head-growth velocity.

## The single weakest link
The step that converts *accuracy + theory* into **"MuC yields valid, correctly-calibrated statistical inference (hence trustworthy SEs / p-values) in the sparse regime — and therefore the application's significance is real."**

This is the one step the paper's own data run *against*.

### Why the data don't entail it
The validity claim rests on the paper's own type-1-error simulation (Table 8, nominal 0.05, N=200, J=2, estimated imputation parameters, 500 runs). Under the global null $H_0:\beta\equiv0$:

| m | MeC | **MuC** | MeU | MuU |
|---|-----|---------|-----|-----|
| 2 | 0.704 | **0.318** | 0.050 | 0.000 |
| 5 | 0.302 | **0.118** | 0.062 | 0.000 |
| 10 | 0.144 | 0.056 | 0.042 | 0.012 |
| 20 | 0.082 | 0.056 | 0.050 | 0.036 |

The headline method **MuC is strongly anti-conservative at exactly the sparsity it was built for** — at m=2 its "5 %" test rejects 31.8 % of the time (≈6× nominal); at m=5 it rejects 11.8 % (≈2.4×). The *well-calibrated* method, in fact, is **MeU** (the mean-imputation, no-Y method) at every m. The mechanism (which the paper concedes) is that conditioning the covariate imputation on $Y$ injects the finite-sample, spuriously-estimated $C_{XY}$ back into $X$; when $m$ is tiny, the imputed $X$ is dominated by the $Y$-slope, so the regression $Y\sim X_{\text{imputed}}$ looks associated even under the null.

The application then applies exactly this miscalibrated configuration. The macrocephaly subsample has a **mean of 6.5 observations/curve** — i.e. right in the m≈5 zone where MuC's size is 11.8 %, not the calibrated regime (m≥10). Yet Table 13 reports the MuC test as "0.000000" and the abstract calls the association "clearly" demonstrated.

The Table 13 row makes the fragility even more direct, because it splits the two *proper* (uncertainty-propagating) multiple-imputation methods:

| | MeC (mean, cond.) | MeU (mean, uncond.) | MuC (multi, cond.) | MuU (multi, uncond.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| p | 0.000000 | 0.000000 | 0.000000 | **0.122862** |

The significance survives only in the configurations that use $Y$ to inform the $X$ imputation (MuC/MeC) or that understate imputation error (MeU). The one multiple-imputation setup that does **not** leak the outcome into the covariate — MuU, which the type-1 study supports as the better-behaved one — finds **no significant effect.** So the flagship conclusion ("pathology is clearly associated with overall head size and growth velocity") hinges on the single modeling choice that the paper's own finite-sample calibration shows is anti-conservative at the data's own sparsity, and that choice is doing real inferential work rather than the data speaking for itself.

### What the paper concedes, and why it still doesn't save the conclusion
Section 3.1.4 flags the inflation and offers a heuristic: "only use the conditional imputation if there is evidence a priori (i.e. before imputation) that the variables are related, or for larger values of m." But the application does not heed its own caveat: it is *not* a large-$m$ setting (6.5 pts/curve, 98 % of the full cohort at ≤10), there is no cited pre-imputation evidence of association, and the p-value is read off as though the MuC SEs were nominal-calibrated when the simulations say they are ~2× too small there. That gap — between the caution the simulations forced and the confidence the application reports — is precisely the link that breaks.

### Weaker-link candidates I considered and ranked lower
- **The MISE artifact** (under *true* parameters, MuC's MISE slightly *increases* with m, Tables 3 vs 6) — acknowledged and explained as a multiple-imputation "more correct scores" artifact; not the weakest.
- **The hedged consistency theory** — Assumption 7 holds $m$ fixed, so it actually *meets* the "no growing-m" promise (the strong assumptions — $\beta$ in the span of the first $p$ eigenfunctions, distinct eigenvalues, consistent imputation parameters — are standard for a first MI result and explicitly labeled preliminary).
- **Sensitivity to the `fcr` spline-based imputation-estimation** (Tables 4–5 vs 1–3) — real but addressed by the discussion; the consistency leg is empirically supported (MuC MISE → 0 in N, m).

All of these are honest and contained. The **inference-validity leg** is where the data and the conclusion actually diverge, and it is where the two headline claims — "valid inference" and "clear pathology association" — both sit.

The fix would be to (a) report the macrocephaly result with a confidence band/sparsity-matched calibration, (b) test the association with the no-outcome-leakage MUU as the primary inference, and (c) present MuC's significance as conditional on a power/falsifiable-prior trade-off their own Table 8 documents.

## Scores
A technically sound, well-organized, and honest paper with a clean Cameron–Martin compatibility result, thorough simulations, and a real EHR application — but whose central "valid inference" claim is undercut by its own type-1-error study in the very sparsity regime it targets, and whose flagship association rests on the one configuration the paper shows is anti-conservative there. The multiple-imputation-for-functional-covariate idea is a useful but not field-shaping advance (Bayesian functional MI precedent exists; the real novelty is the GLM-compatibility framing and the MuC/MuU/MeC/MeU taxonomy).

## [1805.08518v2-writing-framing] Writing and framing critique


I read the full paper (Petrovich, Reimherr, Daymont, "Highly Irregular Functional Generalized Linear Regression with Electronic Health Records," arXiv 1805.08518v2). Below is a writing-and-framing critique.

# Framing and overclaiming

The paper's core idea — recast sparse/irregular functional covariate regression as a missing-data problem and use response-conditioned multiple imputation (MISFIT) — is coherent, the methods writeup is generally clear, and there are several honest caveats in the body (median vs. mean ISE; the Type I error inflation and its explanation; sensitivity to the `fcr` choice). The problem is that the **abstract, introduction, and conclusion systematically omit those caveats**, so the front matter overpromises relative to what the evidence supports.

## Specific overclaims

1. **Abstract:** *"Current methods do not allow for consistent estimation unless one assumes that the number of observed points per curve grows sufficiently quickly with the sample size."* This is a sweeping, uncited characterization. The paper's own treatment of Yao et al. (2005) — a moment-based method developed precisely for sparse designs — complicates this clean dichotomy, and the sentence is stated as a settled fact of the literature rather than an argument.

2. **Abstract:** *"MISFIT is based on a multiple imputation framework, which has the potential to produce consistent estimates without such an assumption."* The only consistency result in the paper (Theorem 3) covers **only** the linear model, only the FPCA estimator, and only under a *fixed* number of points per curve ($m_i \equiv m$), $\beta$ in the span of finitely many eigenfunctions, and distinct eigenvalues. Nothing of the sort is established for the logistic model that is actually used in the motivating application, or for the irregular designs the title promises. The word "potential" hedges it, but the abstract presents consistency as the headline property.

3. **Abstract:** *"it propagates the uncertainty of not having completely observed curves, allowing for a more accurate assessment of the uncertainty of parameter estimates, something that most methods currently cannot accomplish."* Two problems. (a) He et al. (2011) — cited by the authors — addresses imputation uncertainty via multiple MCMC chains, so "most methods" is an overclaim the paper's own bibliography undercuts (their real objection is that the approach targets the functional mixed model). (b) "More accurate assessment of the uncertainty" is directly contradicted by the paper's own Table 8: for the *recommended* conditional multiple-imputation method, the Type I error at the nominal 5% level is **0.318 at m = 2, 0.118 at m = 5, and 0.056 at m = 10** — i.e., 6× nominal precisely in the sparse regime that motivates the entire paper (the real data's modal m is 1). The abstract never discloses this.

4. **Abstract/Conclusion:** *"Using MISFIT we are able to clearly demonstrate that the development of pathologic conditions related to macrocephaly is associated with both the overall head circumference of the children as well as the velocity of their head growth."* "Clearly demonstrate" is too strong for: a single model on a single subsample (n = 800, 85 cases) selected by a stratified random scheme, p-values all rounded to 0.000000 (Table 13), a coarse one-sample bootstrap as the only uncertainty check on the confidence bands, and — for the "velocity of head growth" claim specifically — no formal test of the contrast component at all, only a visual decomposition in Figure 5 (right panel) with pointwise bands.

5. **Introduction (p. 4):** *"We will show that such an approach can lead to biased and even inconsistent estimators."* The "show" is a two-line heuristic in §2.2 (plugging BLUP scores into the FPCA estimator and taking expectations). No theorem establishes either bias or, certainly, **inconsistency**; "inconsistent" is a strong regularity-claim word applied where there is only a heuristic argument plus a reasonable but unstated assumption about the plug-in behavior of the smoothing.

6. **p. 17:** *"It is again evident that MISFIT outperforms PACE in nearly every setting and remains competitive where it does not."* The very same section concedes the PACE comparison is partially an artifact of implementation — PACE results use `fdapace`'s local-polynomial estimator of the *imputation* parameters while MISFIT uses `fcr`'s splines, and the authors note "these differences are purely due to differences in estimating the imputation parameters." A conclusion framed as "evident" superiority over a benchmark that was run with a different (worse here) parameter-estimation pipeline is confounded. The honest statement is: the *conditional-vs-unconditional* imputation model comparison is confounded, and this is not flagged where the claim is made.

7. **Conclusion:** *"This approach yields consistent estimates and captures the variation due to imputing the functional covariates, thus enabling more reliable statistical inference."* Restated here as fact what the theory only gives conditionally ("as long as the imputation model can be consistently estimated") in the linear case; and "more reliable statistical inference" is undercut by the 6× Type I inflation at sparse designs and by the paper's own admission that accuracy is "quite sensitive to the estimates of the imputation parameters."

8. **Introduction (p. 5):** *"none of these methods [NiPALS, PACE-variants] are immediately applicable"* because "our motivating example requires a logistic regression set-up" — a rhetorical move that inflates MISFIT's distinctiveness; adaptation to logistic is exactly what the paper itself does for parts of these frameworks.

What an adversarial reviewer would say in their first paragraph

> "The proposed recasting of scalar-on-function regression on sparsely observed curves as a multiple-imputation problem is sensible, and the writing is generally clear. However, the paper's central empirical claim — that conditioning the imputation on the response yields 'a more accurate assessment of the uncertainty' — is contradicted by its own tables. At the sparsest designs that motivate the work (m = 2 and m = 5 points per curve; the real data's modal m is 1), the recommended conditional multiple-imputation procedure rejects a true H₀ at 32% and 12% of the nominal 5% (Table 8), miscalibration the abstract and conclusion never mention. The headline demonstration that 'MISFIT outperforms PACE in nearly every setting' is confounded by the authors' own admission that the gap comes in part from using different estimators of the imputation *parameters*. The only consistency theorem applies to the linear FPCA model with a fixed number of observations per curve, while the application uses a logistic model on irregular designs for which nothing is proved. And the clinical conclusion — that they 'clearly demonstrate' an association with head-size *and* the velocity of head growth — rests on a single subsample (800 subjects, 85 cases), p-values rounded to zero, one-sample bootstrap bands, and a visually decomposed coefficient function. In short, the abstract, introduction, and conclusion claim more than any of the evidence in the paper supports."

# Overall assessment

The methods and simulation study are substantive (four-way MeU/MuC/MeC/MuU comparison, Type I error and power, robustness to a multivariate-t functional covariate, and a genuinely interesting Cameron–Martin characterization of when the logistic model can exist at all — Theorems 1–2 are the paper's most original theoretical contribution). The failures are chiefly ones of framing: the summary sections present best-case results as the method's character while the worst-case calibration properties of the recommended estimator at sparse designs remain buried. A revision that (i) softens "clearly demonstrate," "consistent," "most methods cannot accomplish," and "we will show"; (ii) discloses the sparse-design Type I inflation in the abstract; and (iii) separates the imputation-model comparison from the parameter-estimation implementation in the PACE benchmark, would make the paper honest to its own evidence.

## Review of "Highly Irregular Functional Generalized Linear Regression with Electronic Health Records" (MISFIT)

ArXiv:1805.08518v2 — Petrovich, Reimherr, Daymont

### Summary and strengths

The paper tackles a well-motivated, practically important problem: scalar-on-function regression when the functional covariates are observed sparsely and irregularly — precisely the situation in EHR growth-trajectory data. The authors correctly identify the two key flaws of the standard PACE-style pipeline: (i) plugging imputed BLUPs/scores into dense estimation is generally biased (the paper derives the bias explicitly, Eqs. 1–2 and the surrounding discussion); and (ii) mean imputation discards imputation uncertainty, producing undercoverage. Their fix — MISFIT — reframes the problem as a missing-data problem and draws imputations *conditionally* on (Y, observed curve) from the joint-Gaussian conditional, K times, with Rubin's rules for combination, then handles logistic regression via a genuinely interesting theoretical bridge: Theorems 1–2 use Cameron–Martin/Radon–Nikodym orthogonality of Gaussian measures (the functional analogue of Efron 1975 / Rao–Varadarajan 1963) to show exactly when a Gaussian functional imputation model is compatible with a canonical-link logistic model. This is a nice, independently interesting theoretical by-product of the paper.

The supporting work is extensive: linear and logistic simulations with true and estimated imputation parameters, sweeps over N, m, J, and signal strength, a type-I-error/powe

r study, a non-Gaussian (multivariate-t) robustness check, and benchmarks against PACE, pfr, and funreg — with an honest treatment that PACE-favorable settings exist (dense designs, dense designs in favor of funreg). The macro

### Weaknesses (why this is not a clean accept)

1. **The headline benefit — honest uncertainty — is not demonstrated where it matters.** The recommended MuC (conditional-draw) procedure is badly miscalibrated in exactly the sparse regime that motivates the paper: Table 8 shows nominal-5% rejection rates of 0.318 (m=2) and 0.118 (m=5) under the null, and the authors' own explanation (conditioning on Y leaks spurious "signal") applies with full force to the application, where the subsample has only ≈6.5 measurements/curve. 2. **The theory is too idealized relative to the motivating data.** Theorem 3 assumes m_i ≡ m across all subjects — the real EHR data famously do not satisfy this (modal m = 1, max 23) — assumes β(t) lies exactly in the span of the first p eigenfunctions (zero truncation error), assumes distinct eigenvalues, and only establishes consistency for the linear model. No rate, no CLT, and nothing on the validity/coverage of the Rubin's-rules-combined estimator (which the simulations show is needed, since that procedure is the one that miscalibrates). **Borderline (leaning accept contingent on revisions); score 6/10.** The problem is real, the method is a clean and useful synthesis of the multiple-imputation and sparse-FDA literatures, the Gaussian-measure compatibility results are nice and reusable, and the simulations are more honest than most (including admitting the MuC type-I inflation and the PACE-friendly regimes). But the paper's central promise — reliable uncertainty quantification for highly irregular data — is undercut in its own sparse-data setting and muddied by the contradictory p-value statement in the application, and the theory is substantially further from the motivating data than the abstract suggests.

### Three changes that would most improve the paper

1. **Fix inference honesty in the sparse regime.** Derive or empirically calibrate the coverage of the Rubin's-rules-combined test for MuC at small/variable m (the simulation setup of Section 3.1.4 already does this — lean on it). Also correct the text/table inconsistency about which MI p-value is "convincingly low" (Table 13 shows MuU = 0.123 is the largest).
2. **Bring the theory closer to the data and to the logistic model.** Rel

     ax the fixed-m assumption to allow m_i to vary (the defining feature of the EHR data); account for or empirically bound the FPCA truncation error; extend the consistency/coverage results to the logistic case that the application actually uses; and provide at least a rate or a CLT for the combined MI estimator.
3. **De-risk the imputation pipeline.** Add a misspecification study for the fcr-based imputation model (alternative bases/splines, different estimators — the Conclusions already note fcr vs. fdapace differences but the paper does not explore them) and propose a principled, data-driven choice of J (e.g., cross-validation of the imputation model). Given the J-sensitivity and logistic instability, stability of the main conclusions across imputation models and J values is currently just asserted, not shown.

# Peer Review: "Highly Irregular Functional Generalized Linear Regression with Electronic Health Records" (Petrovich, Reimherr, Daymont)

## 1. Summary

The paper proposes MISFIT, a multiple imputation framework for fitting scalar-on-function linear and generalized linear (GLM) regression models when the functional covariates are sparsely and irregularly observed. The core idea is to treat PACE (Yao et al., 2005) as mean imputation, and to replace it with draws from the conditional distribution of the curve given both the observed points *and the response* (a "Multiple, Conditional" strategy). A Gaussian imputation model is shown to be compatible with logistic (canonical-link) GLMs, yielding a theoretical by-product (Theorem 1) connecting the existence of a functional logistic model to the equivalence/orthogonality of Gaussian measures (Cameron–Martin/Kuo). The method is evaluated in extensive simulations (linear, logistic, true vs. estimated imputation parameters, Gaussian vs. t-distributed curves) and applied to 800 children's head-circumference trajectories from an electronic health record to distinguish pathologies associated with macrocephaly.

## 2. Major Concerns

**(a) The only asymptotic result barely covers the application it motivates.** Theorem 3 (consistency) is established for the *linear* model only, under: Gaussian process covariates, β(t) exactly in the span of the first p eigenfunctions, **fixed and identical m across subjects (Assumption 7)**, and — most critically — Assumption 5, which stipulates that the imputation parameters (µX, CX, CXY, σ²Y, …) are already consistently estimable by unspecified means. That is: the hard part (consistent estimation of the imputation model from sparse irregular designs) is assumed away, so the theorem is close to tautological (MI from a correctly specified conditional distribution plus a correctly specified analysis model). Meanwhile the motivating data have m ranging from 1 to 23, so the very assumption that makes the proof tractable is violated by the application. The authors acknowledge this ("by no means complete"), but a paper pitching consistency as its main theoretical selling point should at least (i) derive rates, (ii) extend consistency to the logistic case, which is where the application lives, and (iii) state clearly how fcr-based estimation of the imputation parameters satisfies Assumption 5. Notably, the Conclusion reports that "MISFIT's accuracy is quite sensitive to the estimates of the imputation parameters" — an important caveat that is left purely anecdotal (fcr vs fdapace "could warrant further investigation").

**(b) No non-functional baseline in the application.** For a paper aimed at clinicians and EHR analysts, the natural comparison is a mixed-effects logistic model (random intercept/slope) or even simpler growth-velocity summaries fit to the observed data. The four-way MI comparison is a methodological self-reference; a reader cannot tell how much value the functional machinery adds over a well-specified classical longitudinal model, especially with only 85 events in 800 subjects. Given the extreme class imbalance, the reported p-values of "0.000000" carry little information without effect sizes, odds ratios at clinically meaningful ages, or cross-validated classification performance.

**(c) The Gaussian-process and Gaussian-imputation assumptions are stressed but not examined in the application.** The t-distribution simulation (Section 3.2) is reassuring for estimation, but real head-circumference data include measurement errors of "varying type and degree" (the authors' own words, citing Daymont et al. 2017), which are non-Gaussian and possibly non-iid. A single imputation-parameter bootstrap ("basically a one sample bootstrap") is the only calibration of the confidence bands and is not a substitute for coverage checks in a design resembling the real data.

**(d) The logistic-model compatibility theory has an under-discussed edge case.** Theorem 1 shows that a logit model exists with β = CX⁻¹∆ only when ‖CX⁻¹∆‖ < ∞, and exists at all only when ‖CX⁻¹ᐟ²∆‖ < ∞. For realistic smooth covariance operators (eigenvalues decaying polynomially), ‖CX⁻¹∆‖ = ∞ is the *typical* case — then by part 2, the true conditional model is not a logistic one with any square-integrable β, and the Gaussian imputation model is a misspecified approximation whose consequence for the fitted β̂(t) is not analyzed. The "grey area" discussion is intriguing but the practical implication — that in the regime where the data are smooth, the compatibility condition the method relies on generically fails — deserves an explicit statement and perhaps a remark on what the fitted coefficients then estimate.

## 3. Minor Concerns

- K = 10 imputations is modest under Rubin's rules, particularly for variance components; a sensitivity check (K = 20/50) would strengthen the claims about uncertainty quantification, which is one of the paper's headline contributions.
- The paper says MAR/MCAR are "implicitly assumed... without formally defining the missing data mechanism." In a method whose selling point is rigor about missingness, the missingness mechanism (e.g., conditioning on age at visits, which correlates with both visit density and the response) should at least be stated and argued.
- Typographical: "nonparameteric" (Intro §1), inconsistent use of "MISFIT is based on a multiple imputation framework, which has the *potential* to produce consistent estimates" — given the authors derive the PACE bias formally, the abstract undersells what is proved.
- The imputation of ξ̃ij (adding group means back into centered scores) for the logistic case is a clever fix but deserves a sentence on why this is equivalent to curve-level imputation for the fitted contrast; currently it is just asserted.
- Figure 2 is nearly unreadable as rendered (overlaid spaghetti plot); a summary (e.g., quantile bands) would communicate the irregularity far better.
- Software reproducibility: the R implementation should be named and archived (no package is provided for MISFIT itself; fcr only covers imputation parameters).

## 4. Strengths

- The 2×2 design (Multiple/Mean × Conditional/Unconditional) is an excellent way to disentangle the two ingredients — conditioning on Y and variance propagation — and the simulations make the contributions of each separable. This framing alone is a useful pedagogical contribution to the sparse-FDA literature.
- The bias argument for PACE plug-in estimation in §2.2 (E[ξ̂ijYi] ≠ E[ξijYi] unless m → ∞) is cleanly derived and correct; it justifies the paper's focus precisely.
- Theorem 1 and the Cameron–Martin discussion are a genuinely interesting by-product, correctly situating results of Delaigle & Hall (2012) and Dai et al. (2017) in a measure-theoretic framework that pre-dates them.
- The application's decomposition of the coefficient function into average effect and contrast effect (Fig. 5, right) yields a clinically sensible interpretation — elevated overall head size *and* atypical growth velocity — and the confidence-band comparison against the bootstrap convincingly illustrates the uncertainty-propagation benefit over mean imputation.
- The simulation suite is broad (N, m, J, signal, true vs. estimated parameters, non-Gaussian curves) and the paper is honest about where performance degrades.

## 5. Assessment of Novelty, Soundness, and Impact

**Novelty.** The central contribution is a synthesis: taking a familiar missing-data principle (impute from the conditional distribution and combine via Rubin's rules) and applying it to sparse functional data with a GLM-compatible Gaussian imputation model. The missing-data literature had already flagged the bias of mean imputation in exactly this type of problem (and Crambes & Henchiri's regression imputation work is adjacent), so the idea is principled and well-motivated rather than new-in-kind. The novelty resides in the execution: the functional GLM compatibility theorems, the 2×2 evaluation design, and the EHR application. I rate this a solid, well-executed contribution of moderate originality — the kind of paper that advances practitioners more than it advances theory.

**Soundness.** The mathematics presented is correct to the extent it is presented; no errors of substance were found. The weaknesses are in scope: the consistency theorem is conditional on strong, application-irrelevant assumptions and does not cover the logistic case; the compatibility condition for logistic models generically fails for smooth operators and this is under-discussed; and the application lacks an external baseline. None of these is fatal, but they bound the paper's claims.

**Impact.** The problem (very sparse, irregular functional covariates in observational clinical data) is exactly the regime in which EHR science lives, and many existing FDA methods fail there with silently wrong standard errors. A method that makes imputation uncertainty explicit, is implemented in R, and comes with a worked clinical application has clear, broad practical utility; the measure-equivalence side results are also citable in their own right. Impact is modestly above that of a routine methods paper, though tempered by the fact that Bayesian frameworks (also cited by the authors) achieve similar uncertainty propagation and by the thin theory.

## 6. Recommendation

**Minor-to-Major Revision with a favorable bias.** The paper should: (1) extend at least a consistency sketch to the logistic model and to heterogeneous mi; (2) add a classical longitudinal-model baseline to the application with effect-size reporting; (3) discuss the generic failure of ‖CX⁻¹∆‖ < ∞ and what the fitted coefficient then targets; (4) report MI-sensitivity for K and for the choice of imputation-parameter estimator. With these, this is a publication-worthy contribution to the functional data / electronic-health-records literature.

## Overall verdict

This is a **strong, well-motivated methods paper** that you should be proud of. It does a real job: it takes a well-known but flawed default (PACE-style mean imputation, then plug-and-fit) and replaces it with something principled — frame the sparse/irregular functional covariate as a **missing data problem** and do **multiple imputation conditioned on the response**, then combine with Rubin's rules. The payoff is two-fold and it's honestly the most valuable part of the paper: you get (a) consistency without the "points-per-curve → ∞" assumption that plagues PACE, and (b) a **proper uncertainty budget** for the imputation, which mean-imputation methods silently throw away. The macrocephaly EHR application is a credible, domain-relevant use case, and the factorial simulation design is clean.

My reservations are (i) a gap between your theory and your practice, (ii) a real methodological tension in the conditional-imputation branch that you flag but don't fully resolve, and (iii) the "Generalized Linear Regression" in the title quietly requires a canonical link. None of these are fatal, but I want you to think hard about all three before submission.

## What works well

1. **The conceptual reframe is the paper's real contribution.** Pivoting from "FDA with missing data" to "a missing-data / imputation problem" is not just cosmetic. The two-axes factorial — *conditioned on Y vs unconditional* × *multiple vs mean* (MuC/MeC/MuU/MeU) — is a beautiful, minimal design that lets you isolate exactly what each ingredient buys. That this decomposition produces a crisp story (conditioning wins in power, multiple wins in calibration) is much more convincing than a "we beat PACE" comparison would have been.

2. **The bias diagnosis (Section 2.2, around eq. 2) is the conceptual core and it's correct.** Showing that plugging PACE BLUPs into the FPCA regression is biased unless $\hat \xi_{ij}\hat Y_i$ reproduces $\mathrm{E}[\xi_{ij}]$ — and that this essentially only holds with $\beta\equiv0$ or in a dense limit — is the right thing to have established, and it cleanly motivates why you need a method that *conditions on* the response.

3. **The by-product theorem is genuinely interesting in its own right.** Theorem 1/2 — that the Cameron–Martin norm $\|C_X^{-1}\Delta\|_2$ governs (i) when $X|Y$ is Gaussian-compatible, (ii) when the logistic model exists and is nondegenerate, and (iii) the existence of a *continuous* separating functional, versus the "grey area" where the logistic model exists, is finite a.s. on sample paths, but the GLM form cannot hold for any $\beta\in L^2$ — is a subtle and nice result. The careful distinction between the topological dual and the algebraic dual, and the orthogonality-of-Radon–Nikodým-densities argument (app. B/C), are exactly the sort of thing that justifies the title's "results of independent interest." 5. **Rubin's rules actually deliver.** Table 8 is the single most important empirical table: mean-imputation methods reject at 0.30–0.97 vs nominal 0.05 in the sparse, small-signal regime — i.e. they are catastrophically anti-conservative.
