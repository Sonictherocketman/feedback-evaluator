## Review: "Solar irradiance variability" (Solanki & Unruh, arXiv:1210.5911, *Astronomische Nachrichten*, 2018)

### The paper at a glance
This is a short, seven-page review (two authors, one figure-heavy discussion) on how the Sun's total and spectral irradiance vary, why, and how the Sun compares to other cool dwarfs. It is organized in a clean timescale cascade: seconds-to-minutes (granulation, p-modes) → hours-to-days (rotational modulation by sunspots/faculae) → the solar cycle (active-region evolution) → centuries (secular change, Maunder minimum) → stellar context (rotation and long-period activity cycles on Sun-like stars). The two threads tied to the larger climate question — the magnitude of the Maunder-minimum irradiance deficit, and the long-term evolution of open magnetic flux — are handled honestly, as genuinely open problems.

### Strengths
1. **Honest calibration of uncertainty.** The paper repeatedly refuses to overclaim. It presents the Maunder-minimum irradiance range (0.7 to 6 mW m⁻²) as an *extreme-value* bracket, notes that reconstructions "differ from each other in various aspects, but most strongly in the amplitude," and flags the UARS/SUSIM vs. SORCE discrepancy in the visible as "one of the major open questions" rather than brushing it aside. This is the right posture for a review in a contested field.
2. **Coherent physical narrative.** The causal chain — surface magnetic fields → modification of convection and radiative leakage → net brightness change — is laid out clearly, and the paper correctly identifies the "activity-bright vs. activity-dark" dichotomy (Basri & Lockwood) as organizing principle, with the interesting conclusion that the Sun is *activity bright* in total flux yet sits *near the boundary* with respect to its stellar peers. That near-boundary placement is arguably the most scientifically interesting claim in the paper.
3. **Good structure for the intended audience.** For readers who want a single reference that puts TSI in context for both climate and astrophysical stellar variability, it delivers, with appropriately up-to-date (for 2018) references (Basri et al. 2011 Kepler results, Lockwood et al. 2007/2009, Vieira & Solanki 2010).

### Weaknesses and critique

1. **No new synthesis — pure consolidation.** The paper makes no new measurements, proposes no new method, and (as a review) is largely derivative of the 2003 Solanki/Krivova/Unruh reviews and of Fligge et al. (2009). For a 2018 publication in *Astronomische Nachrichten*, a journal that is not the field's leading venue, the incremental value is modest. The field's center of gravity — TSI/SSI review — runs through Space Science Reviews and Solar Physics; this shorter note doesn't displace or meaningfully extend them, which is a fair question to ask about its utility.

2. **The "activity dark vs. activity bright" argument is underdeveloped.** Section 5 (Comparison with other stars) is where the paper is most interesting, yet it is the thinnest. The authors conclude the Sun is "close to the activity dark/bright boundary" but the quantitative basis is a single extrapolation (Fig. 3, black curve) of solar facular/spot filling factors to higher S-index values, using *disk-integrated* fluxes that "neglect the location of the active-region contrast." For a star at a random inclination, projected filling factor — not S-index — is what sets the observed light curve, so comparing solar disk-integrated to stellar (random-inclination) quantities on a single S-index axis is not quite apples-to-apples. The authors gesture at this ("the Sun from a somewhat special 'equator-on' position") but don't quantify it, and that gap matters for how strongly one can claim the Sun is near the boundary.

3. **The Maunder-minimum discussion is underpowered by the paper's own framing.** The paper presents the 0.7–6 mW m⁻² span as if reconstructions are the main uncertainty, but the deeper issue is that (a) *cosmogenic proxies* (¹⁰Be, ¹⁴C, sunspot number) are noisy and model-dependent, and (b) the *irradiance model* applied to a reconstructed proxy (SATO/ViSAT/SATIRE-type flux conservation) carries its own systematic uncertainties that are not addressed. A more honest framing would separate the two sources of error rather than present the 0.7–6 mW m⁻² as a single "reconstruction" range. As written, the reader might mistakenly think most of the spread is in the proxy data.

4. **Missing quantitative treatment of the UARS/SUSIM–SORCE discrepancy.** The paper mentions the visible-band opposite-phase divergence in one sentence (Fig. 4 caption, §3). This is arguably the single most important measurement-level controversy in the modern irradiance record, and it has material climate-relevant implications (it changes the inferred solar cycle–to–cycle evolution of TSI). A review claiming to cover "irradiance at all timescales up to decades" should at least give the two dominant physical explanations (SUSIM stray-light/cosmic-ray background; SORCE/SIM gain drift) and indicate how much literature supports each. As written, it's a passing comment.

5. **Fig. 1's p-mode interpretation is loose.** The paper says the 5-mHz peak "gives the peak at around 5 min (3000 μHz)" from p-modes. At 3000 μHz = 3 mHz, the dominant p-mode period is ~5 min, which is correct — but the power spectrum in Fig. 1 is dominated by granulation at high frequency and the p-mode contribution is barely visible in a 1-min-sampled 2002 spectrum. Calling out "p-mode oscillations also contribute" with a single sentence in a power-spectrum figure that doesn't actually show a resolvable p-mode peak is a small overstatement. (At 1-min cadence, p-modes alias; the 3 mHz peak in TSI is actually often attributed to a granulation/convective-timescale combination.)

6. **Stellar sample selection bias is not discussed.** When citing Basri et al. (2011) and Lockwood et al. (2007) for the activity bright/dark dichotomy, the paper does not note that *ground-based* stellar samples are biased toward large-amplitude rotators (small stars with long periods) and that Kepler's exoplanet target selection is biased toward small, old, quiet stars. For a *Sun-like* sample, the boundary location could shift. A 2–3 sentence caveat would strengthen the argument significantly.

7. **Minor issues.**
   - Header still says "Received XXXX, accepted XXXX, Published online XXXX" — this is a submission draft, not the final published version.
   - Fig. 5: open-flux reconstruction from the geomagnetic aa-index is treated as roughly equivalent to the Lockwood 2009a,b reconstruction, but the aa-index is a proxy of a *different quantity* (geomagnetic response) than the interplanetary magnetic field measurement used by Lockwood. The agreement is notable, but it deserves one sentence noting this distinction rather than reading as independent confirmation.
   - The §4 discussion of the *10⁵-yr thermal timescale of the convection zone* and solar dynamo secular evolution is in a single sentence with no citation — the claim is reasonable but should be referenced.

### Overall assessment
This is a competent, well-organized, and appropriately cautious short review. It is *correct* as far as it goes, and its framing of the two key open problems (Maunder-minimum amplitude, open-flux evolution) is fair. Its limitations are (i) low novelty — it consolidates rather than advances; (ii) the two places where it would most naturally make a contribution — the stellar-comparison section and the UARS/SUSIM–SORCE discrepancy — are both underdeveloped; and (iii) a few small interpretive leaps (p-mode in Fig. 1, open-flux agreement) that go beyond what the cited data rigorously show. For the intended audience of a solar-physics or astro-climate reader needing a one-stop orientation, it does a reasonable job; for a researcher working in the field, it adds little.

# Peer review: "Solar irradiance variability" (Solanki & Unruh, *Astron. Nachr.*, 2018)

## Summary
This is a review paper covering solar irradiance variability: its magnetic causes, behaviour from minutes to millennia, and a comparison with other cool dwarfs. It is written by a group (Satire/Solanki school) that has produced much of the underlying work, and it reads cleanly as a pedagogical synthesis.

## Strengths

1. **Clear physical framing.** The explanation of why some flux tubes are bright and others dark (radiation from walls vs. reduced convective flux; the ~250–400 km critical radius) is one of the better compact treatments I have seen. The Spruit (1982) redistribution argument, tying the ~10⁵ yr convection-zone relaxation timescale to the slow recycling of blocked spot energy, is set out accurately.
2. **Honest treatment of the open problems.** The authors are candid that (a) the TSI change since the Maunder minimum is uncertain by an order of magnitude (0.7–6 W m⁻²) and (b) the SSI cycle evolution is unresolved, with SIM measurements and model reconstructions even showing opposite phases in the visible. Stating both as "major open questions" rather than papering over them is a virtue.
3. **Useful stellar-context framing.** Placing the Sun near the activity-bright/dark boundary and showing how a hypothetical higher-S Sun (S≈0.32) flips to activity-dark is a nice illustration for interpreting Kepler/CoRoT microvariability.
4. **Well structured** and readable; the multi-timescale overview (p-modes → granulation → rotation → cycle → Gleissberg → convection-zone relaxation → solar evolution) is a good map for a non-specialist.

## Weaknesses

1. **No new results and strong in-group bias.** This is entirely a summary of existing work, and much of it is the first authors' own. The "most reconstructions ... roughly between 1 and 2 W m⁻²" framing (p. 4) is presented matter-of-factly, but the spread of published estimates is genuinely wider and the paper under-weights competing reconstructions (e.g. the lower estimates of Shapiro et al. and the higher side). As a review, it should weigh alternatives more symmetrically and quantify each estimate's uncertainty, not just its central value.

2. **Dated content for a 2018 publication.** The arXiv version is from 2012 and the discussion has largely stopped around then: no TSI composites developed after 2012, no mention of post-2012 measurements (e.g. the DIAR/TSI trend work, the debated near-zero cycle-to-cycle trend, or updated SSI from SORCE successor instruments), and no treatment of the ongoing community debate about the *slope* of the TSI trend — arguably as important as the minimum-to-minimum question. This materially limits its value as a current overview.

3. **The stellar extrapolation is more fragile than presented.** The activity-dark/bright boundary is inferred by assuming facular area scales linearly and spot area quadratically with the S-index, extrapolated well beyond the observed Sun. The paper mentions "we do not yet have long-term solar spectral measurements equivalent to the b and y filters" but does not quantify how much the inferred boundary depends on those extrapolation assumptions or on inclination-angle effects (Knaack et al. 2001 is cited in passing only). The conclusion that "regardless of spectral shape the integrated change is ~1000 ppm for S=0.18 and 0.22" slightly oversells the robustness of the 3-component model.

4. **The SSI discrepancy is identified but not advanced.** The SIM-vs-models disagreement in the visible cycle phase is flagged as a major open question, yet the paper offers no diagnostic for which side is wrong (e.g. discussion of possible SIM degradation-correction issues, or specific spectral diagnostics). As the authors' own flagship model (SATIRE) is on one side, a critical self-examination would strengthen this.

5. **Scope limitation stated but arguably under-acknowledged.** The whole review focuses on irradiance. Since the paper's motivating framing is "how strongly the Sun affects our climate," the exclusion of non-irradiance channels (cosmic rays / cloud cover) is a defensible scope choice, but given the repeated climate framing it deserves an explicit sentence saying it is deliberately out of scope.

6. **Editorial/copyediting problems — these should be fixed before publication:**
   - "Figure kindly provided R. Knaack" (Fig. 3 caption) — missing "by"; inconsistent with "Figure kindly provided by W. Ball" in Fig. 2.
   - A stray "Fig. 2" label appears at the top of a two-column figure on p. 3 that is actually the filling-factor/SSI figure (Fig. 3), a copy-paste caption mix-up.
   - "mandatory for maintaining *higher* life on Earth" (p. 1) — almost certainly should be "all life" or "higher" is a typo.
   - The abstract says "how much darker the Sun was during the Maunder minimum," but the reconstructions actually debated whether it was darker *in TSI at all*; the phrase presumes the answer.
   - Figure/caption references drift (the SSI standard-deviation plot is called "Fig. 4" in text but the two-panel layout in the layout mixes Figs. 3–4).
   - The "Received XXXX / Accepted XXXX / Published online XXXX / Copyright line will be provided" placeholders are, of course, expected for an accepted-manuscript arXiv version.

## Minor
- The statement that TSI averaged over a rotation is "roughly proportional to solar luminosity" is fine, but the caveat about polar emission is a single underdeveloped clause; a sentence on how anisotropy could bias the TSI–luminosity inference would help.
- Reference list: "Beer ... 1990, Nature 347, 164" appears under a 1992 citation (the text cites Beer et al. 1992; the reference is 1990). Check the year.

## Overall assessment
A competent, clearly written pedagogical review from the group that built most of the underlying semi-empirical modelling. Its main limits are (i) no new content and an in-group framing that should be more balanced on the competing reconstructions, (ii) a content snapshot that stopped ~2012 and is therefore stale for 2018, and (iii) a number of fixable copyediting errors. It is a useful onboarding document for a reader new to solar irradiance and the Sun–stars–climate connection, but it will not change anyone's understanding of the field and should be updated (and copyedited) before it is relied on as a current reference.

**Recommendation:** minor revision — balance the competing reconstructions, update to post-2012 data/trends, and fix the caption/typo errors.

# Editorial Evaluation: "Solar irradiance variability" (Solanki & Unruh)

## Summary

This is a review article surveying solar irradiance variability from minutes to millennia, connecting it to the broader physics of magnetic surface features, secular changes, and comparisons with Sun-like stars. The logical architecture is sound: the authors move from the short-timescale power spectrum to magnetic-feature physics, to cycle-scale variability, to secular reconstructions, and finally to the stellar context before concluding. The argument flows well enough that a reader unfamiliar with solar physics can follow the narrative thread, and the two "major open questions" framed in the conclusions (spectral evolution and the Maunder minimum brightness) are clearly signposted throughout.

## Prose and Style

The prose is generally clear and competently written, though it carries a few tells of a draft that has not had a thorough editorial pass:

- **Typos/archaisms:** "speacially over longer times" (p. 1) should read "especially over long times." "Evolutional effects" (p. 2) is nonstandard; "evolutionary" or simply "evolution of active regions" would be idiomatic.
- **Subject–verb agreement:** "Note that the active-region contrast used here *are* derived from disk-integrated fluxes and thus *neglect* the location of the active regions" (p. 5). "Contrast" is singular; both verbs need to be singular. This is the most conspicuous grammatical error in the paper.
- **Register shifts:** "For us denizens of the Earth" (p. 1) is a somewhat archaic and informal register that sits uneasily with the otherwise measured scientific tone. Similarly, "the Sun provides basically all the energy needed to keep the Earth at its present temperature and hence is mandatory for maintaining *higher life* on Earth" uses "higher life" in a near-tautological way (higher than what? plants are also life) and the phrase "mandatory" is slightly off-key for a physics review.
- **Mild redundancy:** "The irradiance is a spectral quantity and is often called the spectral irradiance" states the same fact twice in one sentence.
- **Long, clause-heavy sentences:** The paper occasionally strings together three or four subordinate clauses (e.g., the sentence defining the normalised standard deviation on p. 3, the concluding paragraph's final sentence). None is impenetrable, but a copy-editor would tighten several.

These issues are minor and do not impede comprehension. For a review in *Astronomische Nachrichten*—a journal whose audience is broad—they are within tolerance, but a careful second pass would eliminate them.

One structural weakness: Section 4 (secular change) and the discussion of the open magnetic flux in Fig. 5 are somewhat compressed. The jump from "open flux roughly doubled since 1900" to "0.7–6 W m⁻² estimates" is a large inferential step that would benefit from a sentence explicitly connecting flux to irradiance amplitude. The paper also gives relatively brief treatment to the *anti-cyclic* visible/NIR irradiance changes suggested by SORCE/SIM (p. 4), flagging the model–data tension but not spending enough time on why the discrepancy matters for the broader climate question.

This is a solid, well-organised review that does what a review should do: it lays out the state of the field, identifies the key open questions, and places the Sun in the broader context of cool-dwarf stellar variability. The prose is competent with scattered minor errors, and the argument is clear throughout. ## What it is
A timescale-organized survey of solar brightness variability: the physical drivers (magnetic features — sunspots, faculae, network — with granulation and p-modes at the short end and evolution at the long end), the TSI composites vs. semi-empirical models on rotation-to-cycle scales, the contested secular amplitude since the Maunder minimum, and a comparison with other cool dwarfs. ## What works
- The timescale-to-driver mapping in §2 is the paper's clearest contribution: p-modes/granulation (minutes–hours), spot/faculae rotation and evolution (hours–days), active-region lifecycle (daily–cycle), dynamo secular changes (centuries–millennia), convection-zone thermal relaxation (10⁵ yr), and solar evolution (10⁶+ yr). That is a useful mental scaffold.
- The physical explanation of why small flux tubes are bright and large ones dark — cooling from suppressed convection vs. radiative heating limited by the ~100 km horizontal photon mean free path, giving the ~250 km / ~400 km threshold — is a nice concrete insight and correctly attributed (Spruit; Grossmann-Doerth).
- The honest positioning of the Sun as "activity-bright but not far from the activity dark/bright boundary" (§5) captures a real tension in the literature better than more dogmatic treatments.
- Figures 1–5 (power spectrum; PMOD vs. SATIRE-S; filling factors and the "hypothetical Suns"; SSI normalized σ; open magnetic flux) are the right ones, and the SSI figure correctly makes the point that the UV varies ~10× more than the TSI.

## Major weaknesses

**1. It contains no original science.** There is no new data, no new measurement, no new model, no new analysis. Every quantitative result is quoted from someone else, and the two figures the authors most lean on are "kindly provided by W. Ball" and "R. Knaack." The "hypothetical Suns" in Fig. 3 bottom are drawn from the Wenzler et al. 2006 / SATIRE-S filling-factor fits, not derived here. As a research contribution the paper is purely aggregative.

**2. Its central open question is treated superficially.** The Maunder-minimum amplitude is *the* disputed quantity the abstract and §4 build toward, yet it is disposed of in a single paragraph with a list of extremes (0.7 vs. 6 W m⁻²; "most estimates 1–2 W m⁻²"). A review at this juncture ought to unpack *why* the reconstructions differ by an order of magnitude — proxy-to-flux calibration, the spot-faculae balance, the UV/visible ratio, the treatment of the network — rather than just reporting the spread. **3. No uncertainty quantification anywhere.** There are no error bars, no uncertainty budgets across the TSI composites, no comparison of the stability of the constituent instruments. The authors note composites "agree well … on timescales of days to years" and that instruments are "not yet good enough" to detect inter-minimum drifts — but this is asserted, never characterized. **4. **6. It is now dated on its own flagship example.** The SIM-vs-models spectral discrepancy is highlighted in the Conclusions as "one of the major open questions," but the discussion relies on cycle-23 declining-phase SIM data and Lean & DeLand 2012. The SIM degradation problem and the SORCE composite have evolved substantially since, so the specific framing here is stale even within the paper's own stated topic.

**7. Dangling quantitative claims.** "More than 90% of the TSI changes … can be explained" is a good, precise-sounding point, but the residual ~10% is never discussed — and that residual is arguably where the interesting (secular? measurement-instrument?) signal would be. Likewise the Spruit 1982 "energy gets re-emitted over ~100,000 yr" assertion is stated as fact and not examined.

- Caption grammar is inconsistent ("Figure kindly provided by W. Ball" vs. "Figure kindly provided R. Knaack" — missing "by").
- Fig. 3 (top) refers to "the top two plots" in the §5 text, which is a slightly confusing self-reference to a two-panel figure.

## Bottom line
A fair, well-organized, and accurate review of a well-trodden field, whose clear contribution is the timescale-organized synthesis and the honest catalogue of open questions. It is not wrong anywhere I can find, and it would serve as a decent introduction. But it makes no original scientific claim, treats its single most important open question (the secular amplitude) with only the surface detail a review of a contentious topic should avoid, provides no quantification of the measurement/model uncertainties it is implicitly built on, and promises a climate connection the body never delivers. It is a solid-competence review rather than a defining one, and it is moderately dated against its own 2018 publication date.

# Evaluation: "Solar irradiance variability" (Solanki & Unruh, 2012; Astronomische Nachrichten)

## Stated contribution in my own words

The authors present a **review** of what is known (as of ~2012) about how variable the Sun's radiative output — total solar irradiance (TSI) and spectral irradiance (SSI) — is across timescales from minutes to millennia, and how this compares with Sun-like stars. The paper is explicitly organized around three *open* questions it does not resolve: (i) how much darker the Sun was during the Maunder minimum (citing the extreme range 0.7–6 W m⁻², most estimates 1–2 W m⁻²), (ii) how the spectrum changes over the cycle (noting the strong disagreement between SORCE/SIM measurements and semi-empirical models like SATIRE-S), and (iii) how solar variability compares with other cool dwarfs. Its conclusions section is essentially a catalog of these unresolved issues.

## Novelty judgment against the cited prior work

This is a **synthesis/review article, not a research paper**, so "novelty" is judged on whether it introduces new methods, data, or conclusions versus what it cites.

**What is genuinely new in the paper:**
- Very little in the way of new data or new models. All the key results are attributed to others: SATIRE-S (Ball et al. 2011, 2012), the PMOD composite (Frölich 2000), the open-flux reconstructions (Lockwood et al. 2009; Vieira & Solanki 2010; Vieira et al. 2011), the spectral-variability characterization (Unruh et al. 2008), the semi-empirical modelling framework (Krivova et al. 2003; Wenzler et al. 2006).
- The one element with a modicum of originality is the **"hypothetical Suns" extrapolation in Fig. 3 (bottom)** — extending the measured facular/spot filling-factor–vs–S-index relation (from Wenzler et al. 2006) to higher S-index (0.22, 0.32) with a simple 3-component model to predict where Sun-like stars flip from "activity-bright" to "activity-dark." However, even this is framed as consistent with, and derived in agreement with, the already-cited detailed SATIRE-S model (Ball et al. 2011), so it is an illustrative re-derivation rather than a new result.

**What is not new (essentially everything else):**
- The attribution of cycle/rotational TSI variability to sunspot darkening vs. facular brightening (Willson 1982; Fligge et al. 2000).
- The bright/small-flux-tube vs. dark/large-flux-tube physics (Spruit 1976, 1982; Grossmann-Doerth et al. 1994) — the ~250/400 km size boundary for bright vs. dark features, and the Kelvin–Helmholtz "leak" timescale.
- The stellar context: Mt Wilson Ca II H&K (Wilson 1978; Baliunas et al. 1995), the b/y photometric program and the activity-dark/activity-bright split (Lockwood et al. 1997, 2007; Radick et al. 1998), Kepler variability (Basri et al. 2011).
- Holocene reconstructions via cosmogenic isotopes and the ~1–2 W m⁻² Maunder-minimum amplitude range (Steinhilber et al. 2009; Usoskin et al. 2007; Krivova, Balmaceda & Solanki 2007; Schrijver et al. 2011 vs. Shapiro et al. 2011).
- The two "major open questions" (SSI model-vs-measurement divergence; Maunder-minimum amplitude) are explicitly stated as *existing* unresolved debates, not solved here.

## Verdict

The paper's stated contribution is a **state-of-the-field review** for Astronomische Nachrichten, and it fulfills that role reasonably well — it is a coherent, readable synthesis that correctly identifies the three central open problems and frames the Sun for the new Kepler/CoRoT stellar-variability context. But by its own design it advances no method, no dataset, and no conclusion that a reader of the cited literature wouldn't already have. The only candidate for a fresh result — the hypothetical-Sun activity-dark/bright crossover extrapolation (Fig. 3) — is itself acknowledged to be in agreement with, and derivative of, the pre-existing SATIRE-S work. There are also internal inconsistencies that weaken it as a document (e.g., "received XXXX" placeholders, "Astronomische Nachrichten, 17 October 2018" header on an arXiv 1210.5911 / 2012 paper) — signs it was assembled as a review manuscript rather than a polished contribution. For a review, that is expected; for a novelty claim, it is the ceiling on this paper.

## The methodology, in enough detail to reproduce the central claim

The central, best‑established claim of the paper is: **magnetic surface features (spots, faculae, network) account for essentially all TSI variability on timescales of hours to the solar cycle.** Reproducing it end‑to‑end looks like this.

Reproducible numbers the paper cites: UV variability exceeds the TSI by ~10× below 240 nm and peaks in Mg II 280 nm; spots dominate variability in the visible/NIR, faculae in the UV below ~290 nm, mixed between 290–400 nm.
- The paper also shows the simpler **3‑component model** (faculae + sunspots + quiet Sun) that a reader can actually build from scratch: take the empirical fits of facular and spot filling factor vs. the Ca II S‑index (Wenzler et al. 2006), where **facular area ∝ S‑index (linear)** and **spot area ∝ S‑index²**; assume a fixed spot/facula contrast derived from disk‑integrated fluxes; integrate over the disk. That is the reproducible core of Fig. 3 (bottom).

**3. Long‑term / Holocene reconstruction (the "centuries" question).**
- Reconstruct solar activity over the last centuries–millennia from **cosmogenic isotopes** — ¹⁰Be (Be et al.) in Greenland/Antarctic ice cores and ¹⁴C in tree rings, plus (cited) nitrate in Antarctic ice (Traversi et al. 2012) — giving sunspot numbers / open magnetic flux (Usoskin et al. 2004; Steinhilber, Beer & Fröhlich 2009).

## Every assumption the paper leans on / papers over

1. **TSI ≈ luminosity equivalence.** TSI (disk‑integrated, one vantage point) is taken as "roughly proportional to solar luminosity" — valid only if the polar radiative output is constant or co‑varies in phase with the equatorial flux. Never tested.
2. **Instrument stability / long‑term calibration.** The whole secular‑trend argument rests on composite TSI and SSI being stable across generations of instruments. The paper itself concedes instrument degradation is uncontrolled enough that "changes (or the absence of changes) between subsequent cycle minima" are **not unambiguously detectable** — yet cycle‑to‑cycle comparison is exactly what Fig. 2 and the grand‑minimum argument need.
3. **Degradation corrections ≈ modelled physics.** Because "the uncertainty of the instrumental degradation corrections is of a similar order of magnitude as the expected long‑term change" (especially >300 nm), the long‑term SSI trend is effectively *assumed* by the correction, not measured.
4. **Complete feature inventory for the models.** SATIRE‑S‑type models assume magnetograms + model atmospheres capture all brightness‑changing surface physics; convection, supergranulation (hours–days) and small‑scale network are approximate, and the 90 % figure ("more than 90 % of TSI changes on weeks–years") is a model claim, not an independent bound.
5. **Disk‑integrated contrast assumption.** The 3‑component model uses spot/facula contrasts "derived from disk‑integrated fluxes and thus neglect the location of the active regions" — projecting limb and center‑to‑limb contrast effects is left out, which biases the wavelength‑integrated and especially the visible‑band result.
6. **Extrapolation beyond the measured range (hypothetical Suns).** The star‑comparison method assumes facular ∝ S and spot ∝ S² scaling that holds *only* over the observed S range; extending it to S=0.32 (and to other stars) is asserted, not verified — the activity‑bright/dark sign flip hinges on this.
7. **Open magnetic flux ↔ irradiance calibration.** The paper states plainly "it is not clear to what extent and how strongly such variations [in open flux] convert into variations of the irradiance." The amplitude of the Holocene TSI therefore rests on a free calibration, and different reconstructions diverge by an order of magnitude (0.7 vs 6 W m⁻²).
8. **Separability of amplitude from proxy timing.** The methodology assumes the *shape* of TSI over centuries comes cleanly from the proxy while the *amplitude* comes from the modern record — an assumption valid only if the proxy‑to‑flux transfer function is stationary across grand minima/maxima and climate‑regime changes.
9. **Cosmogenic‑isotope reliability.** ¹⁰Be/¹⁴C/nitrate require a stable, invertible coupling between cosmic‑ray flux, magnetic shielding, atmospheric transport, and the ice/tree archive. Different reconstructions (Usoskin vs. Steinhilber) disagree on timing and amplitude, and this sensitivity is folded into the final "ups and downs."
10. **The 1900 open‑flux doubling is real.** Fig. 5's "roughly doubled, then dropped back" is taken at face value from the aa‑index reconstruction; the paper does not flag that this is exactly the period for which the geomagnetic‑record/observatory corrections are least certain.
11. **Schnerr & Spruit under‑estimate.** The paper accepts "the upper limit set by Schnerr & Spruit 2011" as a conservative bound *because* they modelled only visible continuum brightness of magnetic features, thereby "underestimate[ing] the total brightness excess due to magnetic fields in the quiet Sun" — i.e., the quiet‑Sun facular/network contribution is assumed rather than measured.
12. **250 km / 400 km flux‑tube contrast thresholds.** The bright‑small / dark‑large split (Spruit 1976; Grossmann‑Doerth et al. 1994) is treated as a robust, size‑thresholded physical boundary and used to justify the spot/facula partition; its sensitivity to tube geometry is not discussed.
13. **Kepler/CoRoT precision generalises.** The claim that "roughly half of all Sun‑like stars" are rotationally variable (Basri et al. 2011) is attributed partly to Kepler's higher precision; the fraction as a physical property is conflated with the instrument effect.
14. **Viewing‑geometry equivalence.** Comparing a Sun observed "from a somewhat special equator‑on position" to a stellar sample with a mix of inclination angles is acknowledged but **not corrected for** — the spot/facula balance (and hence the activity‑bright/dark classification) is inclination‑dependent, and the comparison is taken at face value.
15. **The SIM vs. model divergence is left as an open question, not resolved.** The paper's own headline discrepancy — SORCE/SIM measurements showing the visible SSI evolving **opposite in phase** to SATIRE‑S/SUSIM/UARS (Lean & DeLand; Preminger et al. 2011) — is presented as "one of the major open questions." This means the spectral half of the central claim (beyond the 290–400 nm band and the NIR sign flip) is, by the authors' own account, **not settled**, and the "models + measurements agree" statement in the abstract/§1 is overstated relative to §3.
16. **No predictive capability is claimed but implicitly needed.** The paper ends by admitting the "main missing ingredient from the solar side" is the inability to predict future irradiance/its activity — so any climate‑attribution use of the reconstructed trend presumes the dynamo's behaviour over the Holocene (Gleissberg cycle, grand minima recurrence) is stationary, which the paper does not justify.
17. **Correlation ≠ mechanism for climate.** The framing ("correlation studies between solar variability and climate indicators" suggest non‑negligible influence) is repeated, but the paper offers no causal estimate; the "how strongly the Sun influences climate" question remains as strong as the 0.7–6 W m⁻² amplitude uncertainty.

# Understanding of the paper's logical argument

This is a review article by Solanki & Unruh (2018), published in *Astronomische Nachrichten*, on **solar irradiance variability** (arXiv:1210.5911). Its central thesis is stated in the abstract and pursued through five sections:

**Thesis.** The Sun is not truly constant. The total solar irradiance (TSI) varies on many timescales, and the dominant question is how strongly the Sun varies on *century-scale* (secular) timescales, because that is the regime relevant to climate change and to whether solar forcing matters for global warming.

**Logical structure:**

1. **Introduction (§1)** — Establishes why it matters: the Sun is the main energy source for Earth, so its variability has climate implications. Defines TSI (wavelength-integrated, robust) vs. spectral irradiance (SSI, harder to measure because spectrometers degrade). Sets up the framing of the "non-negligible influence" of solar variability on climate.

2. **Overview of variability and causes (§2)** — Uses the VIRGO power spectrum (Fig. 1) to show power is dominated by longer periods and drops at short ones. Then the core mechanistic claim, organized by timescale:
   - Minutes–hours: granulation, p-modes.
   - Days–rotation: rotational modulation of sunspots (dark) and faculae/plages (bright).
   - Rotation–solar cycle: active-region evolution.
   - Centuries–millennia: slow secular evolution of the magnetic field / dynamo (≈70–100 yr Gieseberg cycle).
   - It then develops the *physics* of why magnetic features are bright or dark: strong magnetic pressure suppresses convective flux (cooling) but the vertical radiative flux is too small to fully compensate — net darkening (sunspots, pores); in smaller features, evacuated, radiatively-heated flux tubes (the bright interfaces of the "spruit mechanism") make them net bright. The "250 µm" and "40 km" thresholds separate bright from dark features. This gives the physical basis for the Sun being "activity-bright" on longer scales (faculae outlive sunspots).

3. **Variations up to the solar cycle (§3)** — Shows measured TSI since 1978 (Fig. 2) with PMOD/SATIRE composites; activity-bright behavior on cyclic timescales (Fig. 3, S-index vs. facular/spot filling factors). Discusses empirical and semi-empirical irradiance models. Argues for the wavelength-dependence of SSI variability (Fig. 4, normalized standard deviation per wavelength): strong at short UV (Mg II, Ca II), dropping steeply above ~400 nm, but with a UV/SSI discrepancy — the measured UV decline over ~200–400 nm in cycle 23 *exceeds* the TSI decline, in apparent tension with some models, and in the opposite sense to the "activity-bright" picture in the far-UV.

4. **Secular change (§4)** — The most contentious part. Two direct proxies for solar activity over long records: sunspot area (since 1874) and sunspot number (since 1610). Reconstructs open magnetic flux (Fig. 5): roughly doubled 1900–mid-20th c., then dropped back — a "large and rapid change" whose connection to irradiances is *unclear*. Quantifies the Mauderer-minimum uncertainty: reconstructed brightness from 1400–1900 ranges from ~0.7 to 2 W m⁻² (i.e., roughly between 1.9 and 2 W m⁻²). Concludes the magnitude of secular TSI variations is the **first major unknown**.

5. **Comparison with other stars (§5)** — Uses the Kepler/CoRoT context and Mt Wilson Ca II H&K program: activity cycles are ubiquitous on Sun-like stars (2.5–20 yr periods); ~half are rotationally variable; most are "activity-dark" (Ca II H&K anti-correlated with photometry, spots dominating), while the Sun is "activity-bright" on the solar-cycle timescale — though not far from the activity-dark/bright boundary. This places the Sun in stellar context and is used to bound how much its behavior could shift.

6. **Conclusions (§6)** — Restates the two major open questions: (1) amplitude of secular TSI variability (especially Mauderer minimum vs. present), and (2) inability to predict the *source* of irradiance variations (sunspots, magnetic features, or solar activity in general). Says the uncertainty in these two quantities constitutes the key open problems.

**The logical spine**, compressed: TSI varies on many timescales → the variations are ultimately tied to surface magnetic features → those features are physically understood to produce "activity-brightness" on cycle scales (faculae dominate) → yet there is a genuine tension in the far-UV (measured decline exceeds model/TSI expectations) → and the long-timescale amplitude is poorly constrained by proxy-based reconstructions → and the Sun sits near the activity-dark/bright boundary among similar stars → so we cannot reliably say how strongly the Sun has varied over centuries, which is exactly the regime that matters for climate attribution.

# Feedback

**Strengths**

- **Clear, honest epistemology.** The paper repeatedly flags what is known with confidence (timescale causes, cycle behavior, magnetic mechanism) versus what is genuinely open (secular amplitude, UV decline, dynamo). This is the right framing for a review: it resists the temptation to present proxy reconstructions as settled.
- **Timescale hierarchy is a strong organizational device.** The Fig. 1 power-spectrum framing and the minute→millennia ordering make a complex topic navigable and ties each timescale to a physical driver.
- **Physical grounding of "activity-bright."** The flux-tube/convective-suppression argument (with the 250 µm / 40 km thresholds) gives readers the *reason* the Sun is activity-bright on long timescales rather than just asserting it. The honest note that the Sun is "clearly activity bright in the UV, bright in visible, reverses sign in the NIR" is a sophisticated and accurate nuance many reviews miss.
- **Stellar-context section is timely and useful** (Kepler/CoRoT era, ~2015–17), and the "activity-dark/bright boundary" framing gives a concrete handle on the open question.

**Weaknesses / things to push back on**

1. **The Mauderer-minimum amplitude is presented wider than the evidence warrants.** The paper cites a "1–2 W m⁻²" range derived from two reconstruction families, but these share common systematic biases (they all use the same sunspot proxies and, to differing degrees, the same magnetic-flux-to-radiation assumption). The paper should be more explicit that the spread is *between methods that share assumptions*, not a true 95% interval — otherwise a non-specialist reader will read "0.7 vs 2 W m⁻²" as a bracket on nature when it is largely a bracket on modeling choices. That is the single most consequential number in the paper and it drives the climate-relevance claim, so its provenance deserves a paragraph of caution rather than a range in a sentence.

2. **The UV-decline tension is under-developed.** The paper notes (p.4) that measured UV (200–400 nm) decline during cycle 23's decay *exceeds* the TSI decline and is opposite in sign to models, and then moves on quickly. This is arguably the most interesting internal inconsistency in the whole topic. The review would be stronger if it said plainly whether this is (a) an instrument/calibration residual, (b) a genuine physical limit of the faculae-dominance picture, or (c) unresolved — rather than listing citations (Ball 2011, 2012; Lean & DeLand 2012) and deferring the judgment to "for further discussion" (Errmoli et al. 2012). A reviewer of *this review* would want the authors' synthesized position.

3. **"Activity-bright" is asserted more often than shown at the timescales that matter for climate.** The cycle-scale evidence for activity-brightness (faculae outliving spots) is fair, but the *secular* claim — that a grand maximum means a substantially brighter Sun — inherits the same magnetic-flux-to-brightness inference. The review should distinguish cycle-scale activity-brightness (well established) from secular-scale activity-brightness (inferred, not directly observed), because the climate argument rests on the latter.

4. **Proxy-based reconstruction limitations are stated but not stress-tested.** The paper acknowledges reconstructions differ "over the Holocene" and "diverge by an order of magnitude," but does not quantify how sensitive each is to the calibration of sunspot-area → magnetic-flux → irradiance. For a review whose headline claim is that we don't know the secular amplitude, one paragraph on *why* the reconstructions diverge (different proxy weighting, calibration anchors, model physics) would substantially strengthen the argument rather than just note the divergence exists.

5. **Minor structural issue.** The abstract promises that the paper addresses "how solar irradiance variability compares with that of other cool dwarfs, particularly now that observations from space are available also for stars" — but the stellar section (§5) is comparatively short and the comparison is somewhat qualitative (cycles are ubiquitous, ~half rotationally variable, most activity-dark). Given the promise and the era (Kepler/CoRoT data), this could have been a more substantial quantitative subsection.

**Scoring rationale**

- **Originality (3/5):** This is a competent *review*, not an original contribution. The value is in synthesis and clear exposition of the timescale/physical-mechanism/holocene-uncertainty structure, but the ideas, data, and citations are all from the existing literature (2000–2017). The "activity-dark/bright boundary" framing and the honest two-unknowns closing are mildly fresh as a framing device, but the underlying content is well-established by 2018. Not incremental (it does synthesize and reframe), but not novel.
- **Overall (4/5):** Sound, well-organized, internally consistent, and appropriately honest about open questions. The logic holds end to end without major gaps. It loses a point for under-weighting the shared-uncertainty in the secular reconstructions and for deferring rather than synthesizing its position on the UV decline. The paper is reliable and correctly concludes the field's two real open questions.
- **Impact (3/5):** Solar irradiance is a well-studied, important-adjacent field. The review clarifies where the field genuinely stands in the solar-forced-climate debate at an important moment (post-Kepler, post-SIM). It is useful reference material for solar/astrophysics readers but is a review of a niche subfield and does not reshape methodology or open a new direction; its impact is consolidating, not field-shaping.

## Evaluation: Solanki & Unruh (2018), "Solar irradiance variability"

This is a coherent, carefully-scoped review of total solar irradiance (TSI) and spectral irradiance (SSI) variations across timescales, culminating in a comparison of the Sun to other Sun-like stars. It is honest about the two genuinely open problems (secular change magnitude; the long-timescale TSI trend confounded by instrument degradation). The argument chain runs: (1) the Sun varies; (2) the variation is magnetic in origin (spots/faculae/network); (3) the Sun is "activity-bright" on solar-cycle timescales; (4) placing the Sun in a stellar context requires translating observed filling factors into total-irradiance change — and here the chain breaks.

### The single weakest link — the extrapolation in Fig. 3 / Section 5

The pivotal step is where observed facular and spot filling factors (top panel of Fig. 3) are fit with a linear curve (facula) and a quadratic curve (spots) and then **extrapolated to S-index values the data never reach**, from which the authors derive the headline comparison result: that a hypothetical Sun with a raised S-index "becomes clearly activity dark with a total irradiance change of ~1.2%," and that the Sun sits near the activity-bright/dark boundary.

Why the data do not entail this:

1. **The fits are anchored on a vanishingly small dynamic range.** The underlying S-index measurements span roughly 0.167 (cycle minimum) to ~0.20 (solar maximum) — a swing of only ~0.03 in a proxy whose natural range is an order of magnitude larger. A linear or quadratic least-squares relationship learned inside one band cannot be extrapolated to S ≈ 0.32 (more than double the observed maximum) or to lower values and be trusted. The "-1.2% / activity-dark" number is a property of the assumed functional form, not of the data. The data only show the Sun is activity-bright *within the measured range*; the flip to activity-dark is imposed by the extrapolation model.

2. **The chain multiplies model degrees of freedom.** Going S-index → filling factors → total irradiance assumes a three-component (facula/spot/quiet-Sun) picture, a specific wavelength-integration, and a 3-component model for the active region that "neglects the location of the active region" (as the authors concede). Each link is itself a model choice; compounded, they make the -1.2% figure a model output that the raw observations cannot validate. The authors do note the SATIRE-S active-region model is "in good agreement," but that is an internal consistency check within the same modeling framework, not an independent datum constraining the extrapolation.

3. **A proxy is being compared across regimes.** S-index (chromospheric emission, Ca II H&K) is a proxy for magnetic filling factor, and the linear/quadratic response is calibrated in a single regime. Extrapolating a proxy's response law to an unobserved regime, then converting through further models to get a total-irradiance sign and magnitude, is precisely the kind of step where a review's "conclusion" outruns what the measurements can support.

A competing weak spot is the secular/Maunder-minimum reconstruction (Fig. 5): the irradiance amplitude "must be set independently" of the open-flux/sunspot-number proxy, and reconstructions disagree by ~an order of magnitude (0.7 vs 6 W m⁻²). But the authors correctly frame that as *uncertain* rather than *conclusive*, so it is a flagged limitation, not an overclaim. The Fig. 3 extrapolation, by contrast, is presented as a definite quantitative result (the Sun "becomes clearly activity dark"; specific percentages) and is the one place a specific number is asserted from data that does not cover the regime in question. That is where the conclusion stops being entailed by the data.

### Scoring
