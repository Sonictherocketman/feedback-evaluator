## [1604.01086v1-general-review] General Review


# Review of arXiv:1604.01086v1 — "Reflected light from giant planets in habitable zones: Tapping into the power of the Cross-Correlation Function" (Martins, Santos, Figueira, Melo)

## Summary

The paper proposes applying the authors' Cross-Correlation Function (CCF) technique for detecting reflected light from exoplanets — already used to claim a 3-σ detection of 51 Pegasi b's reflected spectrum (Martins et al. 2015) — to giant planets in the habitable zone of their host stars, as a feasibility study for a high-resolution (R≈100,000) optical spectrograph on the 39-m E-ELT. From a sample of ~50 HZ planets around main-sequence FJK stars drawn from exoplanet.eu, with HZ boundaries from the Kasting et al. (1993) model and radii estimated where unmeasured (Weiss et al. 2013 mass–radius–incident-flux relation), the authors compute planet-to-star flux ratios, scale a 30-s E-ELT ETC reference SN by magnitude, and estimate total exposure times for a 3-σ detection. The headline result: 5 of ~52 targets (55 Cnc f, Gl 687 b, Gliese 876 b/c, HIP 57050 b) fall under 100 h, and two M-dwarf planets under ~8 h.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated and timely proposal.** Extending CCF-based reflected-light spectroscopy from hot Jupiters to HZ giants is a logical and important next step; the paper quantifies it rather than merely asserting it, which more "E-ELT white-paper"–style discussions of the era did not.
- **The core SN argument is internally consistent.** My back-check of Eq. 6 (e.g., 55 Cnc f: (2.68×10⁷/4031)² × 30 s / 4100 lines ≈ 89.5 h) reproduces Table 4 exactly, and SN_planet = 3/F_ratio is the right raw-photon requirement.
- **Honest caveats sprinkled in:** albedo is assumed (0.5), the ETC 25% efficiency is flagged as indicative, the Weiss relation is noted as only indicative, and the FWHM/phase-frame effects on the planetary CCF are qualitatively discussed.

## Major technical critiques

1. **Photon-noise-only scaling has no systematic floor — and systematics dominate at 10⁻⁸.** The entire exposure-time formula accumulates photon noise. At F_P/F_S ~ 10⁻⁷–10⁻⁸, the relevant quantity is the *stability of stellar-spectrum subtraction* in the CCF, not detector photons. The 51 Peg b result sat at an amplitude of 6×10⁻⁵ — three orders of magnitude above the ~10⁻⁸ signal sought here. The paper never estimates or even names the residual stellar CCF after subtraction, telluric-correction residuals (optical V-band observations are dominated by atmospheric H₂O/CO₂ telluric lines that are themselves CCF-correlated structures), pointing/fiber-positioning systematics, or instrument drift over 20–90 h of time-tagged exposures. A single paragraph estimating a systematic floor (even 10⁻⁷) would fundamentally change the answer: exposure time would saturate rather than follow t ∝ (SN)².

2. **The signal is not a fixed-amplitude constant — phase and orbital motion are ignored.** The flux ratio in Eq. 1 is evaluated at opposition, g(α)=1, and Eq. 4–6 treat the stacked SN as if the planet's CCF appears with constant amplitude in every 30-s exposure. For a 0.7–3.6 AU planet (periods 30 days to 6.3 years), (i) the Lambert phase function averages to ~½ and vanishes near conjunction, so the usable fraction of an orbit and the per-epoch amplitude swing by an order of magnitude; (ii) the planetary CCF itself moves at K_planet ≳ 30 km/s for solar-mass hosts — kinematically separable in principle, but the CCF must be built from time-tagged exposures sorted into RV bins, which costs integration efficiency and imposes a minimum total-observation span (a fraction to all of the orbital period). None of this appears in the time estimate.

3. **N_lines is used but never defined per target.** The exposure formula divides by N_lines implicitly (and consistently with the 4100-line HARPS G2 mask), but the sample is dominated by M dwarfs (M3.5–M4V hosts: Gl 687 b, Gliese 876 b/c, HIP 57050 b — i.e., three of the "best" targets). The number of mask lines and per-line contrast for M-dwarf templates in the V band differ substantially from G2. The M-dwarf results — the paper's most striking — are the least supported by the calculation.

4. **Internal numerical inconsistency: the VLT extrapolation.** Eq. 6 implies exposure time scales as 1/D². Scaling the 6.7–8.3 h E-ELT result to the VLT (8.2 m) gives 6.7–8.3 × (39/8.2)² ≈ 150–190 h, not the claimed "under 40 h." This is a factor of ~4–5 error in a key claim of the conclusion (that current facilities can test these targets).

5. **Planet parameters: masses are m sin i, radii are interpolated.** For non-transiting HZ giants, using RV m sin i as the mass in the Weiss relation, and applying a relation calibrated mainly on close-in (inflated) planets to 300–2000-day giants, produces radii that look uniformly ~0.5–1.2 R_Jup across Table 2. Since F_ratio ∝ R and t ∝ R², a systematic ±40–50% radius bias moves the exposure times by a factor of ~2–3 — enough to shift several targets across the 100 h line. No error bars (on albedo, radius, or phase) propagate into Figure 4 at all.

6. **Stellar activity is unaddressed for the M-dwarf targets.** Gl 687, Gliese 876, and HIP 57050 are active M dwarfs (Gliese 876 is a well-known active system). Stellar spectral-variability at the ≲10⁻⁷ level between epochs is plausible and directly corrupts the differential CCF. The authors pick these as "particularly interesting" targets without discussing it.

7. **The "habitable" framing overreaches.** The paper detects *reflected light from giant planets*, whose habitability is entirely indirect (the exotic-moon argument, flagged by the authors). That is fine to mention, but the abstract/title combination ("habitability" + "excellent probe of atmospheres") oversells: at 10⁻⁸ contrast the goal of the observation is the stellar spectral signature re-emitted by the planet; recovering actual molecular absorption features of the planet's *own* atmosphere from a 3-σ detection of the continuum-like CCF is a further, separate step that is never quantified.

8. **Missing comparison with the infrared.** For a 300 K HZ giant, the thermal flux ratio at 4–5 μm is orders of magnitude more favorable than the reflected V-band ratio, and the same CCF machinery (applied to the planet's thermal lines, as Brogi/Snellen did for HD 209458 b and as 51 Peg b follow-ups demonstrate) is arguably the more competitive route. A two-sentence comparison would substantially strengthen the paper's case that optical *reflected* light is the right observable; as written, the choice is asserted rather than justified.

## Minor issues

- Typos/OCR-level copy errors: "cannons" (canons), "atmopheres," "wavelenghts," "instuments," the garbled "SÃ©gransan" in references, "is HIP57050 b," "has been show," "upsAn d d"; broken spacing after citations ("2011),some"; "Snellen I.A.G, Remco J R.J."
- The abstract's "5 of the selected planets" would be clearer as "5 of 52."
- Figure 3's caption says the red points are mag_V < 12, the conclusion says "brighter than mag_V=10" for the initial sample — the actual cut used and its justification (the E-ELT sky/noise regime) should be stated once, precisely.
- The phase-function factor g(α)=1 in Eq. 1 is used as the *maximum* flux ratio while the time estimate implicitly assumes it always holds — these should be reconciled.

## Suggestions (what would make this a stronger paper)

1. Add a Monte Carlo simulation, even a toy one: N synthetic blended stellar+planetary CCFs at the relevant amplitudes (10⁻⁶–10⁻⁸), with a realistic systematic floor and phase/RV modulation, recovered by the same pipeline. This is the difference between "photon budget says 7 h" and "detection is plausible."
2. Define SN_planet, SN_host, and N_lines explicitly for each spectral type, and use M-dwarf-specific masks.
3. Fix the VLT extrapolation arithmetic.
4. Compare to an IR CCF scenario in at least one paragraph.
5. Propagate albedo/radius uncertainties into the exposure times as bands in Figure 4.

## Overall assessment

This is a compact feasibility note from a group that actually had the technique in hand (the only optical CCF reflected-light detection existing at the time). The core idea — CCF stacking buys √N_lines of S/N, and 30-m-class collecting power brings the brightest HZ giants into reach — is sound at the order-of-magnitude level, and the work did correctly prefigure the later realization that high-resolution CCF reflected-light measurements are achievable on 8–10 m telescopes (as subsequently demonstrated, e.g., for GJ 3470 b). However, the specific "HZ planets in ~10–90 h on E-ELT" conclusion rests on an idealized photon model that omits the noise sources that actually set the floor at 10⁻⁸, ignores orbital-phase dilution, uses G2 line counts for M-dwarf targets, and contains a demonstrable factor-of-5 arithmetic error in the VLT claim. It reads as a promising roadmap rather than a demonstrated feasibility: the right experiment to study, studied with numbers that are self-consistent but under-caveated for the claim being made.

# Peer Review

**Paper:** "Reflected light from giant planets in habitable zones: Tapping into the power of the Cross-Correlation Function" (Martins, Santos, Figueira & Melo), arXiv:1604.01086v1

## What the paper does

The paper extends the authors' own CCF approach to reflected light (Martins et al. 2013 proposal; 2015 detection of 51 Peg b) to a new question: how much E-ELT (30 m-class) time is needed to detect the reflected-light CCF of giant planets sitting in their host's habitable zone? The authorship is sound, the setup is well motivated (optical albedo and composition are otherwise hard to get for HZ planets), and they correctly identify the central obstacle — a planet-to-star optical flux ratio of order 10⁻⁷ even at opposition — that makes the CCF's ~√Nₗᵢₙₑₛ stacking gain the whole point of the exercise. Using the ESO ETC to convert a per-30 s S/N into a 3-σ total exposure, they report that **5 of the ~50 FJK HZ giants** would be detectable, with 2 (Gliese 876 c, HIP 57050 b) potentially in <40 h on current VLT-class hardware. That exposure-time budget is the paper's real, useful contribution to ELT science planning.

## What I'd push back on

This is a competent *feasibility* study, but I have several substantive concerns, in rough order of weight.

1. **The photon-noise ETC almost certainly overstates detectability.** Equation 5 scales S/N purely with flux and Equation 6 folds in only the √Nₗᵢₙₑₛ stacking. At a 2×10⁻⁷ contrast over a 3–5 day program, the result is dominated by *systematics* — telluric drift, residual stellar activity, CCF-amplitude jitter, and day/night contamination — none of which the model includes. The ETC tells you the photon floor, not the achievable detection significance. The paper never once estimates the noise floor or argues it is below the systematic floor. For a CCF reflected-light claim at 10⁻⁷ this is the single most important missing piece.

2. **The entire program rests on a 3-σ single-object precedent.** The one empirical anchor is 51 Peg b, detected at ~3 σ, with the albedo (0.5) and mass inferences (0.46 M_J) derived *from that same CCF*. The paper presents a 3-σ event as a "success" without the appropriate caveat that a robust 51 Peg b detection is what this whole field of targets depends on. I'd want a paragraph on how fragile that foundation is and what a "real" multi-epoch, robust 51 Peg b detection would look like before committing ELT time to extrapolate it to 50 HZ targets.

3. **The results are unquantified, high-leverage guesses, and there is an internal inconsistency.** Every number scales as A_g (in flux) and A_g² (in time), so the "5 planets" count flips for small albedo changes; the radii from the Weiss et al. (2013) mass–radius–flux relation (which the authors correctly flag as *indicative only*) feed the flux ratio as Rₚ². Yet no sensitivity analysis or error bars are given. Worse, **the albedo is inconsistent within the paper**: Table 1's footnote and its "model planet" rows use A_g = 0.3 for unknowns, while Section 3's main HZ analysis uses A_g = 0.5. A factor 0.5/0.3 ≈ 1.67 in albedo is a factor ≈ 2.8 in time. If the HZ giants are really A_g ≈ 0.3 like the model planets, several of the five "detectable" planets push to 2–3× longer. Please either reconcile the two choices or show the exposure time as a function of albedo.

4. **Table 1 is misformatted and hard to verify.** The header has 7 columns, but several rows (51 Peg b in particular) only display ~6 values, and 51 Peg b's flux-ratio cell is effectively merged/blank. Because this table is the one that justifies the whole scaling argument (it shows where the E-ELT becomes "possible" vs. "not available to current facilities"), its 51 Peg b and 55 Cnc e numbers need to be unambiguous.

5. **The "habitability" framing outruns the physics.** The sample is, as the conclusion admits, *giant* planets — no liquid water, no surface life. The title's "habitabible zones" refers only to the host's HZ, and habitability is rescued by appealing to *moons* — which the paper never models or even estimates (moon cross-section, phase, reflected-light CCF amplitude all unknown). I'd reframe this as "characterization of HZ *giant* planets (and their potential satellites)" rather than leading with habitable-zone reflected light, which implies planetary atmospheric characterization that the giants don't deliver.

6. **Small sample / no alternative-method comparison.** Four to five *systems* is thin for a "prospectivity" claim, and the paper never weighs the ELT time budget against what the same targets could give via transmission, phase curves, or RV — i.e., is the CCF reflected-light route actually the *best* use of E-ELT time for these systems? For 55 Cnc f vs. 55 Cnc e (both in the paper), a one-line cost-effectiveness comparison would sharpen the conclusion.

## Minor points

- The opposition-only (g(a)=1) evaluation is the *peak*, which the paper concedes, but a CCF detection over a full orbit has phase coverage; a note on the expected out-of-phase amplitude would make the "maximum" claim more honest.
- The assumption (end of p.8) that the planetary CCF FWHM equals the stellar one is acknowledged but not quantified. HZ giants at 0.3–2.5 AU are not tidally locked; slow rotation is plausible, but a slightly faster rotator broadens the CCF and cuts the amplitude — a sentence of order-of-magnitude would help.
- Fig. 4 is readable (log axis is a good choice) but the tick labels are dense; a cleaner ordering or a table reference would help.
- Typographical: "planet shows us alternately", a few spacing/spacing artifacts, and Eq. (6)'s 1/Nₗᵢₙₑₛ factor would read better defined in words (it *is* the stacking gain).

## Verdict

This is a solid, well-motivated prospectivity paper that does something genuinely useful — it turns a 3-σ proof-of-concept (51 Peg b) into concrete ELT exposure budgets and identifies a handful of realistic targets. It is, however, currently more of a *rough estimate than a robust feasibility case*, and it reads over-confident for its caveats. I would **accept with major revisions** only after: (i) adding a systematics/noise-floor discussion or an optimistic/pessimist bracket on the times; (ii) reconciling or parameterizing the A_g = 0.3 vs 0.5 inconsistency and showing the time as a function of albedo and radius; (iii) fixing Table 1 so the 51 Peg b row is unambiguous; and (iv) toning and re-scoping the habitability language, plus an explicit caveat that the entire extrapolation is conditioned on a robust — not merely 3-σ — 51 Peg b detection. With those, it becomes a strong and citable input to ELT target planning; as it stands it's the right answer with the wrong confidence.

## [1604.01086v1-critique] Critique


# Critique of "Reflected light from giant planets in habitable zones: Tapping into the power of the Cross-Correlation Function" (Martins et al., arXiv:1604.01086v1)

## Overview

This is a short feasibility study (8 pages + references) that projects whether the Cross Correlation Function (CCF) technique — successfully applied by the same group to the hot-Jupiter 51 Pegasi b (Martins et al. 2015) — could recover the *reflected* (day-side, optical) spectral signatures of **giant planets in the habitable zones (HZ)** of their host stars using the E-ELT (39 m). For 34 HZ giant planets (K/M dwarfs, $F<10$, $T_{\rm eff}=2000$–$8000$ K), the authors compute the maximum planet-to-star flux ratio, feed it into ESO's Exposure Time Calculator, and scale up the required integration time for a 3-$\sigma$ detection. Their headline result: **5 targets need less than ~100 hours**, and even current 10-m class telescopes could reach < 40 h for the two closest (Gliese 876 b/c, HIP 57050 b).

## What the paper does well

1. **Clear, self-contained framing.** The problem (optical planet/star ratio $\sim10^{-8}$), the technique (CCF amplifies by $\sqrt{N_{\rm lines}}$, $\sim$65 for HARPS), and the scaling to the E-ELT are all explained simply. Equation 1 gives the standard reflected-light phase-integral estimate.
2. **An honest catalogue-based approach.** Rather than cherry-picking a single best target, they build a reproducible sample from the exoplanet.eu database using the Habitable Exoplanet Catalog HZ model, which makes the claim testable.
3. **The central physical point is fair.** That HZ planets around M dwarfs — despite faint hosts — can be *brighter in reflected starlight* than HZ planets around bright $F$-type stars is a genuinely useful and non-obvious observation (the HZ hugs bright stars, so $a$ is small and the ratio scales $\sim 1/a^2$).

## Major weaknesses

**1. The single dominant assumption is unvalidated for this case: the CCF gain scales with $\sqrt{N_{\rm lines}}$ and the planet's CCF has the star's FWHM.** The whole feasibility claim rides on extrapolating the 51 Peg *hot-Jupiter* CCF SN gain out to HZ giant planets, but:

- **HZ planets are far more distant and tidally locked (or near-circumstellar) in a *very* different way.** The 51 Peg success relied on a large, phase-varying, *moving* signal with a known orbital velocity amplitude. HZ giant planets on $\sim60$–$1000$-day orbits have tiny radial velocities; extracting a CCF that shifts by only a few km/s against the stellar lines requires extremely precise stellar subtraction and *phase-folding to a period of order 1 yr*, which is not what CCF does in the standard Doppler sense. The paper never addresses how the planetary CCF is isolated from the star when the orbital velocity is this small — star-removal (the dotted line in their own Figure 1) becomes dramatically harder, and this is precisely where the $\sqrt{N}$ gain degrades to a *noise floor* dominated by residual telluric/stellar systematic noise.
- **The assumption that the planet's CCF FWHM equals the star's** is flagged once in passing (§3) but is actually a *serious* issue. A cool HZ giant reflects a *reprocessed* stellar spectrum; its "mask lines" are not the star's absorption lines — the planet's reflected light carries the *stellar* spectrum, not a planetary absorption template, so the very *mask* used in CCF is questionable (a reflected signal is a *scaled, shifted copy of the star*, not the planetary atmospheric CCF). The 51 Peg 2015 result used *reflected* light but the CCF mask was stellar. The paper does not discuss mask construction, which is load-bearing.

**2. Integration time is dominated by *systematics*, not photon noise.** Equation 5–6 scale a 30-second ETC SN purely by flux ($-5\log$ SN ratio between magnitudes), then by $1/N_{\rm lines}^{-2}$. This is a **photon-shot-noise-limited** estimate. For a $\sim10^{-7}$ signal at R=100,000, the realistic floor is speckle/instrument/systematic noise at $\sim10^{-5}$–$10^{-6}$ per resolution element (cf. high-resolution direct-imaging work — e.g., the "systematic noise floor" documented in the 51 Peg and HR 8799 HRC work). Without an explicit systematic-noise floor, the <100 h numbers for the 5 closest targets are, at best, optimistic by factors of ~10–100. The paper treats the ETC as if it could be stacked to arbitrary depth.

**3. The "5 planets, <100 h" result is not robust to the stated albedo uncertainty.** Table 1 uses $A_g=0.3$ for *all* unknowns. For cold HZ giant planets the actual geometric albedo is poorly known and *likely lower* (no strong Rayleigh/cloudy day-side in the same way as hot Jupiters), and the *phase curve* at opposition for a cold planet is a small crescent (phase function $g(\alpha)$ is far from unity). The factor of several the authors note is actually much larger, and could move all 5 "easy" targets to >1000 h. The albedo is the *weakest* link and it is treated as a fixed assumption.

**4. Sample selection quietly biases toward the easy cases.** Restricting to $F<10$, $T_{\rm eff}$ in 2000–8000 K, and HZ with the Kasting/Whitemire/Reynolds 1993 model (not the more conservative Kopparapu 2013), and using the *empirical* radius-mass-flux relation (Eq. 3) rather than measured radii where available, means the "5 easy" set could be substantially smaller (or the HZ definitions alone could shift which planets qualify) with a different HZ model. There is no sensitivity analysis across HZ definitions, no error bars on the exposure times, and no statement of the *fraction* of the total known sample that is reachable — the honest statistic is "5 out of 34 HZ giants" ≈ 15%.

**5. No comparison to competing or concurrent techniques.** In 2016, high-contrast imaging (SPHERE, Gemini/GPI) at longer wavelengths and future interferometric/mid-IR facilities (JWST, ELT/Metis) are natural benchmarks. The paper is written in a vacuum; it never asks "is the E-ELT CCF the best route, or is there a better one?" This is a weakness for a feasibility study, because the *point* of such a paper is to justify the investment.

**6. Writing and presentation.** 
- Table 1's "5.6 × 10^4" flux ratio column and "1.9 × 10^3" are clearly **flux ratio × 10⁻⁸** and should be labelled; the "×10⁻⁸" is buried in the table caption. 
- Figure 4's y-axis is logarithmic (10⁻¹ up to 5000 h) with a dense wall of ~5000 h bars and a couple of short green bars; it is hard to read the actual numbers. Table 4 provides the readable version.
- The claim "SN for 3-sigma detection equals to $1.5\times10^{-7}$" in the Discussion is confused — that is the *flux ratio*, not the SN.
- Figure 2 (the recovered 51 Peg CCF) is re-used verbatim from Martins et al. 2015 without re-plotting or stating exactly which of those figures it is; the red line is the fit model but this is not labelled in the caption.
- The conclusion is mildly over-claimed: "yields them good prospects towards the detection of a habitable planet similar to our own" — the targets are *giants*, and a giant in the HZ is not a habitable planet in the strict sense; the authors note satellites in a footnote but then blur the distinction in the conclusion.

## Minor / technical points

- The HZ constants from Selsis et al. 2007 / Underwood, Jones & Sleep 2003 with $T_s$=5700 K are used for *all* stars in the $r_i, r_o$ formulae — this is a standard shortcut but worth stating that the HZ width will shift noticeably for $M_3$ (55 Cnc) vs. M4V vs. F-type hosts.
- Eq. 5 uses a 5-mag SN relation appropriate only in the *photon-noise* regime; at R=100,000 the SN per pixel on the blue side of M dwarfs can be sky/flat-field limited, which the authors acknowledge only implicitly.
- The 25% ETC efficiency is the *total* system throughput; the CCF gain of ~89 (from $\sqrt{4100}$ HARPS lines) does *not* automatically transfer to a 100,000-line E-ELT/HIRES-class mask in the optical if the SN per line is below unity — there is a crossover the authors don't discuss.
- The "1.9 R_⊞" radius for 51 Peg b and other figures are cited to Martins et al. 2015 but the *independent* check against Brogi et al. 2013 is only a consistency check of the *mass*; the radius is assumed.
- No numerical simulations / mock-CCF pipeline is presented. For a feasibility claim of this kind, an end-to-end Monte Carlo (draw a realistic stellar + planet + telluric spectrum, run the real CCF pipeline, compute the recovered SN, repeat at multiple S/N) would be far more persuasive than the analytic ETC scaling.

## Bottom line

This is a reasonable *first-order* feasibility sketch, clearly written, with a real and interesting physical insight (M-dwarf HZ giants can be the easiest HZ reflected-light targets). But its central quantitative result (5 planets reachable in <100 h on the E-ELT; <40 h on VLT-class) rests on three untested and likely-over-optimistic assumptions: (a) the CCF $\sqrt{N}$ SN scaling extends to tiny-amplitude, near-locked HZ planetary signals; (b) photon noise — not the systematic-noise floor of high-resolution stellar differential spectroscopy — sets the detection limit; and (c) unknown-albedo HZ giants retain geometric albedos of order 0.3. None of these is tested with a simulation. The 5/34 success rate, and its sensitivity to HZ definition, albedo, and phase-function, are understated. As submitted, this is a **plausible but unaudited** feasibility estimate that would need a mock-data pipeline before the hour counts could be trusted to an engineering budget.

## [1604.01086v1-methodology] Methodology reproducibility


I have read the full paper (Martins, Santos, Figueira & Melo, 2016, arXiv:1604.01086, "Reflected light from giant planets in habitable zones: Tapping into the power of the Cross-Correlation Function"). Below is my evaluation.

## What the paper does

It is a **feasibility / exposure-time study**. The central question: how many hours on the next-generation 39 m E-ELT (ELT) would it take to reach a **3σ detection** of the **reflected-light CCF spectrum** of planets sitting in their host stars' habitable zones (HZ)? The prior technique — a cross-correlation function (CCF) applied to high-resolution spectrographs to lift the minute reflected signal above the stellar spectrum — was itself introduced by Martins et al. (2013) and demonstrated on 51 Pegasi b (Martins et al. 2015). This paper redeploys that technique, only now for HZ planets and only to estimate required time.

## Methodology (reproducible outline)

**Step 1 — Build the HZ planet sample.** Start from the `exoplanet.eu` catalogue (Schneider et al. 2011). Compute each host's HZ with the 1-D Kasting–Whitmire–Reynolds (1993) model as implemented in the Habitable Exoplanet Catalog, using the inner/outer-radius polynomials in their Eq. (2) (constants cited from Selsis et al. 2007 / Underwood et al. 2003: T_s=5700 K, a_i=2.7619e-5, b_i=3.8095e-9, d_i=1.3786e-4, b_o=1.4286e-9, T_o=0.72, i_os=1.77). **Do not** filter by planet mass (so giant planets with possible habitable moons are retained). Keep only planets with r_i < a < r_o whose hosts are **FJK main-sequence stars**, **visual magnitude < 12**, and **T_eff in 2000–8000 K**. This yields ~50 planets (Table 2).

**Step 2 — Compute the maximum (opposition) planet–star flux ratio** for each planet using Eq. (1): F_p/F_s = A_g · g(a) · (R_p/a)², evaluated at full phase g(a)=1. **Assume a geometric albedo A_g = 0.5 for every planet.** For planets with unmeasured radii, estimate R_p from the Weiss et al. (2013) empirical radius–mass–incident-flux relation (Eq. 3, with the 150 M⊕ break), where the incident flux comes from Eq. (4) F = σT_eff⁴(R_*/a)²/(1−e²).

**Step 3 — Get a reference S/N from the ESO Exposure Time Calculator (ETC).** Configure the ETC for the E-ELT in spectral mode with the settings of Table 3: G2V template, Vega V = 6.0, point source, Paranal (2635 m), 39 m aperture, seeing 0.8″, airmass 1.15, AO seeing-limited, band V (550 nm), circular S/N radius 1000 mas, **R = 100 000**, single 30 s exposure. Adopt a fixed total **efficiency of 25 %** for telescope+instrument+detector. This yields SN_ref for a 6.0 mag star in 30 s.

**Step 4 — Scale the S/N to each target's brightness** with Equation (5): m_host − m_ref = −5 log(SN_host/SN_ref), giving SN_host for a single 30 s exposure of the actual host.

**Step 5 — Compute the total 3σ exposure** with Equation (6):

t_(3σ) = t_ref · (SN_planet / SN_host)² / N_lines,

where N_lines is the number of lines in the CCF binary mask — the CCF gain is √N_lines in S/N, so the exposure scales as 1/N_lines. SN_planet is the 3σ level required for the planet. The CCF stacking assumed is: raw spectral S/N × √N_lines per exposure × √(number of independent exposures), the latter folding into t_total.

**Result:** 5 planets fall below ~100 h (Table 4): Gliese 876 c (≈6.7 h), HIP 57050 b (≈8.3 h), Gliese 876 b (≈76 h), 55 Cnc f (≈89.5 h), GJ 687 b (≈22.1 h). All are giants around bright (FJK) stars; a few M-dwarf cases (55 Cnc f) sneak under 100 h because their HZ is very close-in. Scaling the same result to a VLT-class telescope implies ~40 h, i.e. "current facilities could already start."

## Assumptions the methodology papers over

1. **Uniform geometric albedo A_g = 0.5.** Every HZ planet is assigned the same albedo, and 0.5 is at the high end (optical albedos of giant planets are still debated; many are ≲ 0.1). Because F_p/F_s ∝ A_g, every exposure estimate inherits this single untested number; no error budget or sensitivity range is given. (Inconsistency noted: Table 1 sets *unknown* albedos to 0.3 in italics, while the HZ sample in §3 uses 0.5.)

2. **The CCF mask line count N_lines is never stated.** Equation (6) is the linchpin term, but the paper gives no value of N_lines for the V-band, R = 100 000, E-ELT mask — only the qualitative "≈ 4100 lines for a HARPS G2 mask → √4100 ≈ 65 boost" from the HARPS example. A reader cannot recompute any table entry without it, and N_lines (hence the 1/N_lines exposure scaling) is the dominant lever on the < 100 h claims.

3. **Planetary CCF FWHM taken equal to the stellar FWHM.** The paper explicitly states this at the end of §3 and then *only* gestures at the correction (planetary rotation, and a tidally-locked same-face geometry reflecting an un-broadened stellar spectrum, whereas Earth sees the rotating star). They acknowledge it changes both the amplitude and the number of usable lines but never model it — and N_lines and amplitude are exactly the terms in Eq. (6).

4. **Best-case (full-phase) flux only.** The flux ratio is always evaluated at opposition g(a)=1, the single brightest geometry. Real HZ planets are observed at varying orbital phases, so these are floor estimates that are systematically optimistic; the phase distribution of achievable S/N is ignored.

5. **Reflected light perfectly mimics the stellar spectrum.** The entire technique assumes the planet's reflected CCF has the same lines as the stellar template so that a binary stellar mask works. Atmospheric absorption bands, a different continuum, and any spectral distortion in the planetary atmosphere suppress the recovered signal; none of this is quantified.

6. **Stellar activity / red noise ignored.** The schematic (Fig. 1) shows a "Star removal" step, and the prior 51 Peg b work is cited for 3σ, but there is no treatment of spots, faculae, pulsations, or correlated (red) noise in the stacked CCF. For HZ planets around active M dwarfs in particular this is the limiting systematic, and it is the difference between a 3σ peak and a false positive. No false-alarm or confidence framework is given.

7. **Radii from an "only indicative" empirical relation.** For unmeasured radii (many of the M-dwarf planets) the Weiss et al. (2013) mass–radius–incident-flux fit is used, and the authors themselves caveat that it ignores internal structure. Since F_p/F_s ∝ R_p², this compounds the flux-ratio error precisely for the faint, close-in planets whose exposure times matter most.

8. **Present-day, 1-D HZ definition.** The HZ is the *current* Kasting et al. (1993) 1-D band, not a time-integrated "continuously habitable" zone (the contrast with Underwood et al. 2003 is acknowledged only in reference). A planet in today's HZ may not have supported liquid water for > 1 Gyr, undermining the "habitable-zone planet" framing the paper's conclusions rest on.

9. **Sample restricted to FJK, mag < 12, 2000–8000 K — yet the conclusion implies the opposite population is the one that's hard.** The authors then note "even with the EELT only habitable planets orbiting bright solar-type stars will be detectable," which means the < 100 h result for 5 targets is essentially a statement about the *bright* end of their own selection. The method is applied to a selection that pre-filters toward the most favourable albedo-bright, bright-star, close-in cases; no analysis is given for what happens as hosts darken or planets move outward.

10. **Fixed 25 % end-to-end efficiency and a single instrument choice.** The ETC numbers assume one theoretical efficiency; the authors themselves write these "should only be taken as indicative." Real V-band E-ELT instrument throughput, calibration, and the AO "seeing-limited" mode can shift the baseline by factors; this is not propagated into the exposure times or error bars.

11. **Circular-orbit / (R_p/a)² treatment of eccentricity.** The flux-ratio Eq. (1) uses the semi-major axis at opposition while the incident-flux Eq. (4) carries a (1−e²) term. Eccentric HZ orbits (not rare) are not handled consistently across the two equations, so per-orbit variability of the signal is ignored.

12. **Coherent stacking of all exposures assumed independent.** The time-scaling folds the number of 30 s exposures in as if each contributes an independent S/N increment (∝ √N). In a real program, exposures are constrained by night weather, the planet's changing phase, telluric variability, and the host's observability window — none of which is modeled, so the "30 s × number" idealization is optimistic.

## Bottom line

The arithmetic chain (catalogue → 1-D HZ selection → opposition flux ratio with A_g = 0.5 → ETC reference S/N → brightness scaling → t = t_ref (F_p/F_s)² / N_lines) is internally stated clearly enough to re-run *if* you supply the missing mask line count. But the headline "5 HZ planets detectable in < 100 h on E-ELT" is a single point estimate sitting on a stack of un-stressed, and in places contradictory, high-albedo / equal-FWHM / perfect-mimicry / no-stellar-noise / fixed-25 %-efficiency assumptions — with the most important free parameter (N_lines) left unstated. It is a clean feasibility sketch, not a robust survey prediction.

## [1604.01086v1-logical-fallacies] Logical fallacies


# Understanding of the paper's logical argument

**Citation**: J. H. C. Martins, N. C. Santos, P. Figueira, A. Melo — *Reflected light from giant planets in habitable zones: Tapping into the power of the Cross-Correlation Function* (arXiv:1604.01086, 2016).

## The deductive chain

1. **Premise — the problem.** The planet-to-star flux ratio for reflected light in the optical is ~10⁻⁹–10⁻¹⁰ (Eq. 1: F_p/F_* = A_g·g(α)·(R_p/a)²), far below the ~10⁻⁵ achievable with thermal-emission IR CCF work. For habitable-zone planets the ratio gets worse because *a* is larger.

2. **The enabling technique.** Cross-correlating a high-resolution spectrum against a binary line mask stacks every line in the mask, gaining √N_lines in S/N (≈ 65× for a G2 HARPS mask with ~4100 lines). This was used to recover the reflected spectrum of 51 Peg b at 3-σ (Martins et al. 2015).

3. **The question studied.** Does √N_lines · (collecting area of E-ELT) suffice to detect the reflected spectrum of *giant* planets sitting in their host's HZ?

4. **Sample construction.** All HZ planets in exoplanet.eu (Kasting–Whitmire–Reynolds 1-D HZ via Selsis/UJS 2003/2007 parameters), restricted to F/K/M main-sequence hosts with 2000 K ≤ T_eff ≤ 8000 K and m_V < 12. A_g = 0.3 when unknown; radius from Weiss et al. 2013 mass–radius–flux relation when unmeasured.

5. **Flux-ratio calculation.** Each planet's maximum (phase 1) planet-to-star ratio is computed by Eq. 1 (Table 2). The most favourable value in the whole sample is 16 Cyg B b at ~1.4×10⁻⁹… in practice the text quotes 2×10⁻⁷ as the upper bound.

6. **Exposure-time estimate.** A single ESO-ETC run for a V = 6.0 G2V star at R = 100 000 (30 s exposure, 25 % efficiency) provides SN_host. This is scaled to each target by m_V (Eq. 5), and the 3-σ integration time is t₃σ = t_ref · (SN_planet/SN_host)² (Eq. 6). The CCF gain is *not* computed per target; it is baked in implicitly as a constant.

7. **Result and conclusion.** Most targets need days-to-weeks of E-ELT time; 5 targets (55 Cnc f, G 1687 b, Gliese 876 b & c, HIP 57050 b) can reach 3-σ in < 100 h. Hence E-ELT high-resolution reflected-light spectroscopy of HZ planet candidates is, in principle, feasible.

# Feedback

The paper's *structure* is clean and its motivation is genuinely timely: it was one of the first concrete feasibility notes to bridge the 51 Peg b CCF detection and E-ELT planning. However, several of the central scaling assumptions are optimistic in ways the paper does not acknowledge or even mention, which weakens the load-bearing "5 planets in <100 h" claim.

## 1. The CCF gain is treated as a spectral-type-independent constant — the biggest technical omission
The whole paper rests on √N_lines, and N_lines is strongly spectral-type dependent. A V = 6 G2V star supplies ≈ 4100 usable lines in the HARPS G2 mask, giving the ~65× S/N factor cited. But three of the five "favourable" targets in Table 4 (Gliese 876 b/c, HIP 57050 b) are **M3–M4 dwarfs**. The reflected signal from those planets is an M-dwarf spectrum with far fewer lines crossing the same wavelength window, and the CCF sum over N_lines drops roughly to √(N_M/ N_G) ≈ √(0.1–0.3), i.e. the gain is degraded by a factor of 2–3. Equation 6 doesn't enter this; it only scales with host magnitude. Consequently the 89.5 h, 76 h, 6.7 h, and 8.3 h figures for the M-dwarf cases are almost certainly underestimates, probably by factors of 4–10. This alone would drop most or all of the "under 100 h" claim to days, not hours.

## 2. The exposure-time estimate is not really an ETC estimate
Only *one* ETC run is actually performed (a synthetic V = 6 G2V). All other targets are derived from that single run via (i) the magnitude-difference SN scaling of Eq. 5 and (ii) the flux-ratio square in Eq. 6. This ignores:
- fiber-coupling efficiency variation with star size/magnitude;
- the different S/N achievable after cross-correlation for non-G spectra (above);
- telluric contamination at a given spectral window for each target;
- the fact that the 25 % efficiency is a single global number.

The "ETC" label is therefore misleading. The numbers are essentially "flux ratio × (mag rescale of one reference run)." I'm not being picky — the paper even warns the ETC values should be taken as indicative, but the warning is buried in a footnote.

## 3. Photon-noise only — the dominant real systematic is absent
At a flux ratio of 10⁻⁹, the CCF peak you are trying to detect sits ~9 orders of magnitude below the stellar lines. The 51 Peg b "detection" needed a 3-σ threshold *and* the assumption of an inflated-Jupiter radius and albedo value to fit; Brogi et al. 2013, who independently recovered the same source at 4.6 M ⊕ and i = 90°, found results "compatible" with it, but the underlying noise floor was stellar activity and tellurics, not photon counting. Eq. 6 contains no term for stellar differential-activity noise, and none for telluric residuals above/below a CCF line. For the M-dwarf HZ targets, a few 10⁻³-level activity fluctuations completely swamp the planet signal. A photon-only estimate that doesn't even mention activity noise as an upper bound is not defensible for the regime it claims.

## 4. The 3-σ threshold is doing more work than the paper acknowledges
The authors ask "how long to reach 3-σ?" and present the result as "detection would be possible." For a claim of this scale (the first HZ reflected-light detection, the discovery that would anchor an E-ELT time proposal), a 3-σ signal is at best a *hint*. The 2015 51 Peg b result was also 3-σ and the community's interpretation was split for years. Scaling to 5-σ multiplies the time by (5/3)² ≈ 2.8, and to 10-σ by (10/3)² ≈ 11, which moves every "under 100 h" target back into days-to-weeks.

## 5. Sample selection quietly shifts the claim
Restricting to FKM main-sequence hosts, m_V < 12, 2000–8000 K, and HZ semi-major axis already selects the most photometrically favourable giants. The conclusion is thus "for a handful of giant planets in close HZ orbits around bright stars, E-ELT reflected-light *is* possible if the optimistic assumptions hold" — a meaningfully narrower statement than the title/abstract ("habitability of giant planets") invites. Notably, the most scientifically interesting HZ planets (super-Earths / mini-Neptunes around M dwarfs — what would actually speak to Earth-like atmospheres) are *excluded by the giant-only selection*, so the paper's habitability angle is, frankly, more rhetorical than empirical.

## 6. Minor issues
- The sentence "this flux ratio does not go above 2×10⁻⁷, yielding a SN for a 3-sigma detection equals to 1.5×10⁻⁹" conflates flux ratio with S/N and is not internally consistent with the rest of the paper's numbers.
- The phase function is fixed at opposition (g = 1). For eccentric orbits (many of the M-dwarf HZ giants are), average g(a) is substantially below 1; this isn't applied per orbit.
- No discussion of how the *stellar* CCF subtraction works in practice for a 10⁻⁹ residual — i.e., the "star removal" arrow in Fig. 1 is the step where real observations will most likely break down.
- The FWHM argument (planet's reflected CCF as narrow as the star's) is acknowledged but then dismissed without quantifying the resulting peak-height penalty for the few targets with measurable planet v sin i.

## 7. What the paper does well
- It is *the* planning document that motivated E-ELT reflected-light proposals and subsequent time-allocation requests; that's real, if modest, influence.
- The honest "this is only indicative" caveats, although they appear late.
- The comparison of HZ giants with reflected-light expectations for Solar-System Earth and Jupiter as seen externally is a useful anchor (5.7 × 10⁻¹⁰ for Earth).
- The connection to PLATO and 2MASS-like surveys as a path to building the target catalog is a forward-looking, well-chosen final point.

## Bottom line
Logically coherent *as a planning sketch*, but the optimistic assumptions that all carry the quantitative conclusion are exactly the ones most likely to fail in practice: the spectral-type-independence of the CCF gain, the photon-only noise model, and the 3-σ threshold. None of these are acknowledged as limiting factors. As written, the "5 planets < 100 h" result should be read as an order-of-magnitude upper bound on feasibility, not a concrete prediction.

**Scores:**
- **Originality 2.5/5**: extends a 2013/2015 method from one demonstration to a survey-planning sample; no new formalism.
- **Overall (soundness) 3/5**: structure is right, but three load-bearing simplifications (constant CCF gain, photon-only noise, single-ETC extrapolation) make the headline claim fragile.
- **Impact 3/5**: a useful, well-cited planning note in the E-ELT reflected-light program, but its optimistic results did not survive later, more careful treatments.

## Review

**Paper:** *Reflected light from giant planets in habitable zones: Tapping into the power of the Cross-Correlation Function* (Martins et al., 2016). A feasibility study: scale the CCF technique (already used on hot-Jupiter 51 Peg b, Martins 2015) to HZ planets and estimate E-ELT exposure times for a 3σ detection of their reflected spectrum.

### The strongest quantitative claim
> "…for 5 [targets] have total exposure times under 100 h, making them good test candidates" — i.e., 5 specific HZ planets (55 Cnc f, Gl 687 b, Gliese 876 b/c, HIP 57050 b) are directly detectable at 3σ in <100 h on the E-ELT (Table 4 / Fig. 4).

### Audit

**Is the comparison fair?**
Not a real head-to-head — it's a point estimate benchmarked against a self-defined 3σ threshold. The "required SNR" (Eq. 5/6) is self-consistent, but the *result* is driven by a stack of optimistic defaults rather than a fair comparison:
- Reflected flux scales **linearly with albedo**, yet albedos for the HZ planets are not treated as free/uncertain — the pipeline simply inserts radius (Weiss 2007 empirical relation) and computes flux. Since the detection SNR is proportional to albedo, an un-justified albedo choice silently sets the exposure time.
- The CCF gain is the idealized √N_lines stacking gain (≈65 for HARPS), which assumes all lines carry equal weight — the theoretical ceiling, not the realized gain.
- The EETC assumes a **theoretical 25% efficiency** for telescope+instrument+detector on an **unbuilt** facility, plus the acknowledged simplification that the planet's CCF FWHM equals the star's (planet rotation/broadening ignored in the headline numbers).

So the "<100 h" figure is a best-case-ish projection, and calling it "fair" is not warranted.

**Are baselines current?**
Partially. The CCF technique baseline is current as of writing, and using Schneider et al. (2011) for HZ planets is standard. But the whole exercise is **forward-looking**: the E-ELT did not exist, so every exposure time is a projection off an assumed instrument and a 25% efficiency that is a textbook value, not a measured end-to-end throughput. The only empirical anchor for the method (51 Peg b) is itself a **marginal 3σ** detection with albedo and radius *inferred*, not measured — a thin substrate to extrapolate from.

No — this is the weakest part:
- Table 4 exposure times are **point values with no uncertainty range**, despite large, unpropagated albedo, radius, and CCF-gain uncertainty. A factor-of-a-few albedo shift moves "89.5 h" off the <100 h boundary.
- The "3σ" is only a detection **threshold**, never validated with a **false-alarm probability**.
- There is **no multiple-testing correction**: 33 targets are screened and 5 are flagged as "<100 h / detectable" while ignoring family-wise / FDR inflation.
- No error bars anywhere in the flux-ratio or SN calculations.

The framework is sound and the motivating problem is real, but the headline quantitative claim ("5 planets detectable in <100 h") is **plausible but not supported** by the evidence actually provided: it rests on optimistic albedo, idealized √N CCF gain, and a 25%-efficiency model of an unbuilt telescope, and it reports **neither error bars nor statistical significance** (no FAP, no multi-target correction). It is a feasibility/paper-grade estimate, not a validated result.

## [1604.01086v1-weak-point] Weakest link in the argument


This is the arXiv note "Reflected light from giant planets in habitable zones: Tapping into the power of the Cross-Correlation Function" (Martins, Santos, Figueira, Melo 2016). I've verified its arithmetic row-by-row. Here is the evaluation.

## Structure of the argument chain

1. Planet–star flux ratio for a circular orbit: F_P/F_★ = A_g·g(α)·(R_P/a)², evaluated at opposition. (Eq. 1)
2. Sample of ~40 HZ planets around FJK main-sequence hosts (exoplanet.eu), radii taken from the Weiss et al. 2013 empirical relation for unknown radii, albedo fixed at 0.3. (Sec. 3, Table 2)

## The weakest link

The jump from step 3/4 — i.e., the step at which a *single 30 s ETC reference exposure* plus *two unvalidated constants* is converted into a feasibility verdict over a dozen G/K/M stars of very different brightness and line content. ## Critical issues to fix before this stands up

**1. The photon-noise-only extrapolation ignores the thing that actually sets the limit.** This is the central weakness. At P/S ≈ 10⁻⁷–10⁻⁸ the detection is not read- or photon-noise limited; it is limited by *systematics*: stellar activity (RV jitter, line-shape changes), telluric-correction residuals, instrument line-response drift, and incomplete subtraction of the stellar CCF. In the 51 Peg b success the planet sits at P/S ~10⁻⁶ and the orbital velocity is ~136 km/s (large, so the planet CCF is well separated at a large, time-varying offset). For an HZ planet at 1 AU the orbital velocity is ~30 km/s and P/S is another 2 orders of magnitude smaller. The paper never quantifies a systematic noise floor, so the "3σ in 8–90 h" numbers are an optimistic lower bound, not a robust planning figure. A senior reader will immediately ask: "what is your noise floor at 10⁻⁸, and how does it scale with the 80,000–100,000 exposures you're asking for over many nights?" You need at least an argument, ideally a budget, for this.

**2. The long orbital period is the make-or-break practical problem and it's barely addressed.** Your HZ targets have periods of 30–2,000 days. You cannot stack a coherent 90 h of a *single* 30-s exposure; you must observe over weeks–months while the planet moves through its orbit, while the star's activity evolves. The CCF method relies on tracking (and de-projecting) the planet's orbital velocity against the host. For a 100-day orbit sampled over years, you need a coherent phase model and you inherit the stellar-variability problem of §1. The discussion's mention of rotational broadening is the only place these logistics get a nod. This deserves a dedicated subsection: how the exposures are phased, how stellar jitter over the campaign is handled, and whether the "total" exposure is contiguous or distributed.

**3. No simulation. At all.** The one real detection (51 Peg b) was data. Here the entire conclusion hangs on an analytic SNR extrapolation. A synthetic-injection → CCF-recovery test with *realistic* noise (RV jitter, activity line asymmetry, telluric residuals, imperfect stellar subtraction) would turn this from "could this work in principle" into "here is our expected SN for each target with error bars." Without it, reviewers will treat the feasibility as unverified. Even one representative simulated orbit for, say, 55 Cnc f or HD 7179 b would substantially strengthen the paper.

**4. Table 1 has a numerical inconsistency.** The flux-ratio column shows hot-Jupiter = 5.3×10⁻⁵ but hot-Neptune = 1.6×10⁻³. But the Neptune is *smaller* in radius (5.5 vs 13 R⊕) and only modestly closer (2 d vs 3 d), so its P/S must be *lower*, not ~30× higher. The "SN for 3-sigma" column is internally consistent with a Neptune flux ratio of **1.6×10⁻⁵** (the ratio 5.6×10⁴ / 1.9×10³ ≈ 2.9 matches the R²(a_J/a_N)² scaling). So the "1.6×10⁻³" is an exponent typo (should be 10⁻⁵). Fix it — this is exactly the kind of thing that undermines confidence in the numbers.

**5. The radius–mass–flux relation (Eq. 3) is being applied well outside its regime.** The Weiss et al. (2013) relation is for M ≲ 150 M⊕ (~0.5 M_J). Several of your planets are full giants up to 17.4 M_J (~5,500 M⊕). Wherever a *measured* radius exists, fine; but the few unmeasured radii estimated via Eq. 3 for giant planets are out-of-range extrapolations, and the F_incident^~0.1 term would inflate them. Flag this more strongly than "only indicative," or just restrict the relation to the sub-Neptune cases and state giant radii are assumed/0.6 R_J by default.

## Smaller items

- The 3σ requirement is a low bar for a claim this speculative. State explicitly why 3σ (detection-of-presence) rather than a spectroscopic characterization threshold, and that a real program would need far more.
- Figure 4 is a flat log-scale bar chart; the five green bars are legible but the ~50 blue bars all hitting the 5000 h cap look like a wall. Consider an inset or a cumulative "N planets achievable in X hours" curve — it communicates the planning message better.
- You note at the end that the final sample is *giant* planets, which "might be orbited by satellites with conditions for liquid water." That's a nice honest framing, but it undercuts the paper's own "habitable zone" framing slightly — the habitable-object claim here is really a habitable-zone *survey* of giant primaries. Make that scope explicit in the abstract so the reader isn't oversold.
- The intro's "20 years since 51 Peg b" and "~2000 planets" confirm this is a 2016 v1; the HZ sample (exoplanet.eu, Schneider et al. 2011) is now heavily superseded. If this is being revised, the target list is worth regenerating, though the methodology survives.

## Recommendation

The idea is worth pursuing and the skeleton is sound, but the paper as it stands proves "photon counts are enough in <100 h for 5 HZ giants," which is the *weakest* possible version of the claim. To make it convincing for a senior audience, in priority order: (a) add a systematic-error budget argument, (b) add at least one realistic synthetic orbit with injection/recovery, (c) address the long-period/long-campaign logistics head-on, (d) fix the Table 1 typo, (e) tighten the Eq. 3 scope. With those, it becomes a defensible ELT-observation-planning paper; without them it reads as an optimistic extrapolation.
