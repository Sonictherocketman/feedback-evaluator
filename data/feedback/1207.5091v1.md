## What the paper argues

The paper studies learning an unknown *reactive* (simple) LPTS that is **consistent** with a finite set of positive and negative *stochastic tree* samples, where consistency is strong-simulation conformance (≤). It contributes, in order:

1. A tree counterexample result (Thm 1): if L₁ ≰ L₂, some **tree** is a counterexample — justifying a teacher that returns only trees.
2. Two algorithms for a **consistent LPTS** of small size from fixed samples: classical *state-partition* quotients (§III‑A) and a new **stochastic partition** quotient (§III‑B), both cast as satisfiability over linear rational arithmetic (LRA) — hence decidable (Thms 2–3).
3. An **active-learning** formulation with a conformance-querying teacher: undecidable in general (Thm 4), but decidable under a *friendly teacher* condition (every counterexample has an execution mapping, Thm 5) and, with a *minimum-state-consistent learner* condition, yields a *minimum-state consistent* LPTS (Thm 6, semi-algorithmic).
4. An application to **compositional assume‑guarantee (ASYM)** reasoning, where the learner discovers a small intermediate assumption; a spuriousness check is included.

## Strengths

- **Stochastic partitioning is the real contribution, and it's clean.** The key observation (Figure 6) that a minimum *consistent partition* does not yield a minimum *consistent LPTS* — because a single positive-state may need to be split across multiple target groups — is the correct insight. Defining a quotient over a "grouping" where a state maps stochastically into several classes (Def 9–10) and proving *minimum LPTS ⟺ minimum stochastic partition* (Cor 2) is a genuine, well-motivated idea that classical partitioning cannot express.
- **The tree-counterexample sufficiency (Thm 1)** is exactly the technical fact that makes restricting the teacher to trees legitimate, and it is used correctly downstream.
- **Honesty about undecidability.** Thm 4 (unlearnable in general) is stated and proved rather than hidden, and the paper is careful to attribute undecidability to the *active* variant, not the sample-based one (the abstract does this correctly).
- **Effective algorithms, not existence proofs:** everything reduces to LRA, which is implementable.
- The writing is generally well-organized, with the hard proofs deferred to the appendix where they are in fact complete (e.g., the induction in Lemma 4/Appendix B and C).

## Weaknesses / critique

**1. The decidability results rest on strong, under-motivated teacher conditions.** Condition 1 (every counterexample must have an *execution mapping* to H) is what repairs Thm 4. But the paper never gives a constructive way to *build* a friendly teacher for an arbitrary real target, leaning on cross-references to [19]. One has to ask: is a teacher derived from a given system LPTS guaranteed to satisfy Condition 1? This is the main unsolved gap in the active-learning story, and the "decidable" theorems are only as credible as the realizability of the condition the reader cannot independently verify. Thm 4's "adversarial teacher" is a legitimate undecidability proof but reads like a strawman for the practical regime (a *fixed* target U), which is a bit of a framing mismatch.

The motivating claim — that this is "useful for automating assume‑guarantee reasoning" — has zero empirical support: no benchmark, no toy example quantifying the "in practice … better than checking L₁ ∥ L₂ ≤ P directly" assertion in §V. The relationship between "minimum‑state consistent" and "a good assumption" is also left implicit: minimality is purely computational (cheaper downstream checks), not semantic — the paper should state that the small model is only a *heuristic upper bound* on the useful assumption, which it largely is by its own wording but should make explicit.

**4. Notation density and terminology cost the reader.** Section II–III introduce a large symbol budget (R, ⊩_R, lift, weight function w, [s], par(·), [s](g), v_i, d_σ, …) with no summary table. **6. Generality is quietly narrowed.** The whole development is for *strong* simulation conformance; weak simulation is future work. For AG, strong-simulation vs. trace-based conformance is a real modeling choice that the paper takes as given (inherited from [19]) rather than arguing for. Reactive (at-most-one-transition-per-action) LTSs are assumed throughout, which is fine, but the "minimum‑state" objective is asserted as the right size proxy without a deeper argument.

## Bottom line

A competent, well-scoped theory paper with one genuinely new idea (stochastic partitioning), a useful tree-counterexample lemma, and an honest undecidability/decidability boundary. The argument is internally sound. The argument-level weaknesses are about **thinness rather than correctness**: the teacher conditions are strong and not constructively justified for real systems, the AG application (the motivation) is underdeveloped, complexity is unanalyzed beyond "decidable/exponential," and there is no empirical content. Readability suffers from symbol density and confusing counterexample terminology.

# Review of "Learning Probabilistic Systems from Tree Samples" (Komuravelli, Păsăreanu, Clarke)

## Summary

The paper studies learning an unknown non-deterministic Labeled Probabilistic Transition System (LPTS) from finite sets of positive and negative *stochastic tree* samples, with consistency defined by strong simulation conformance. Two passive learning algorithms are given: one based on **traditional state-space partitions** (yielding a minimum-quotient / "least number of partitions" consistent LPTS) and one based on a novel **stochastic partition** (yielding a minimum-state consistent LPTS). Both are formulated as satisfiability problems over linear rational arithmetic. In the active-learning setting (an equivalence/membership-style teacher over tree samples), the authors prove the learning problem is **undecidable** in general, but **decidable** under a "friendliness" condition on the teacher (every returned counterexample carries an execution mapping to the conjectured hypothesis); imposing an additional "minimum-state hypothesis" restriction on the learner recovers a guaranteed minimum model. An application to probabilistic assume–guarantee compositional reasoning (the ASYM rule) with a spuriousness check and a full complexity analysis closes the paper.

## Strengths

1. **Well-motivated, genuinely new problem setting.** Prior active-learning work (Angluin-style, L\*, tree-counterexample DFA learning) is non-probabilistic or works over trace semantics; there is no probabilistic analogue over *tree* languages/tree automata. Learning LPTSs from stochastic tree counterexamples for compositional verification is a real gap, and the paper attacks it head-on. The motivation (automating probabilistic assume-guarantee for compositional reasoning) is concrete and credible.

2. **The technical core is elegant.** The "matching" of distributions via a weight function (Def. 2) and the ability to *split* probabilities (the Figure 3 example) is the right abstraction for simulation over distributions. The central novelty — replacing deterministic one-state-per-group partitions with a *stochastic* grouping (Def. 9), the "weighted analog of Hall's theorem" (Lemma 1) — is a clean and non-trivial idea, and it is what separates the two algorithms (minimum partitions vs. minimum states). The Figure 6 separation (H₁ vs. H_λ, one fewer state under the stochastic quotient) correctly justifies why the second algorithm is needed.

3. **Undecidability/decidability analysis is sound and honest.** Theorem 4 identifies the adversary (a teacher that can select infinitely many probability values without an execution mapping) that breaks convergence; the friendliness condition is the minimal natural repair, and the authors explicitly note that the Section III counterexample algorithm satisfies it — a good self-consistency point. Theorem 3 (decidability of learning the *minimum-state* consistent LPTS in the passive case) is a strong and pleasant result.

4. **The assume–guarantee application is a real value-add**, and the complexity of checking the ASYM premises is worked out explicitly, including the `2^{k^{d+1}}·(d!k)^d·2^{k^{d+1}}` bound and the observation that a small assumption makes it better than checking L₁ ≈ L₂ directly.

## Weaknesses

1. **The decidability guarantee is conditional on two assumptions, one of which is asserted rather than established.** The "friendliness" (Condition 1) teacher bound is justified by pointing to the authors' *own* counterexample-generation algorithm, which is reassuring, but in practice the teacher is a model checker; it is not demonstrated that the counterexamples such a tool actually emits in a probabilistic setting carry execution mappings. This is the paper's softest load-bearing assumption, and it is the reason the result reads as "decidable for a well-behaved teacher" rather than "decidable." A short discussion of which realistic teacher implementations satisfy Condition 1 would strengthen it.

2. **The undecidability is a black-box, no-positive-result statement.** The proofs are adversarial (λ driven to a limit by Algorithm 2; the two-state-vs-one-state H_λ / H_1 oscillation). They convincingly rule out the naïve framework but give no characterization of *which* targets are learnable up to ≈ under Condition 1, nor any lower-bound. A reader left wanting "under what conditions does a learner converge" gets a sufficient condition, not a boundary.

3. **Large gap to practice.** Everything is decided by enumerating candidate partitions and candidate assumptions and encoding into LRA; the bounds are exponential in the hypothesis size and the support size, and no implementation, benchmark, or case study is provided. For a theory venue this is acceptable and the authors are explicit about leaving "better algorithms" to future work — but the stochastic-partition LRA encoding in particular (nested weight-function variables, witness-subset booleans, per-transition consistency constraints) is exactly the part whose practical tractability is most in question, and it is left as a black box.

4. **Properness of the exposition.** A few key steps are deferred ("the details are straightforward and left to the reader," "the rest of the encoding is similar to before"), most notably the LRA encoding of the weight function / witness-subset S and the consistency constraints for the *stochastic* case (Theorem 3 rests on this encoding being correct, but its proof is only sketched via "similar to before"). The notation in the stochastic-partition/Quotient-LPTS section (Defs 9–10, the lift μ = lift(μ_P, g)) is dense; a worked end-to-end encoding example would have made the core method more checkable. I found no errors, but the load-bearing encoding deserves more than a cross-reference.

5. **Non-determinism bookkeeping.** Allowing LPTSs to mix deterministic and nondeterministic choice (dissolving the choice for counterexamples, "at most one transition from a given state per action" for the reactive variant) is a defensible modeling choice, but it adds a layer of cases that the presentation handles somewhat in passing; a cleaner separation of the reactive/deterministic core from the nondeterministic extension would have helped.

## Verdict

A rigorous, well-motivated theory contribution that is a genuine extension of the automata-learning + compositional-verification line into the probabilistic tree-sample regime, with a legitimately novel device (the stochastic partition) at its heart. The main results are correct; the limits are the conditional (two-assumption) decidability, the asserted naturalness of the teacher condition, and the distance from implementability. Solid LICS-caliber work — original and sound, not yet practice-ready.

**Recommendation:** accept, with the above as fair limitations rather than fatal flaws.

# Critique: "Learning Probabilistic Systems from Tree Samples" (Komuravelli, Păsăreanu, Clarke — ICICS 2012)

## Overview and Positioning

The paper studies learning an unknown *reactive Labeled Probabilistic Transition System* (LPTS) from positive and negative *stochastic tree* samples, framed in simulation-equivalence. It builds two consistent-model learners — one using a classical state-space partition of the samples, one using a novel *stochastic partition* that guarantees minimum state count — and then folds both into an active learning loop for compositional **assume-guarantee** verification of probabilistic systems.

The work sits squarely in the L* / Angluin lineage and its probabilistic extensions (Caroescu–Onica, Feng–Kwiatkowska, Tzenng). The contribution is to port the *tree-counterexample + partition* machinery to a probabilistic setting where the samples are **trees** (reactive, probabilistic branching) rather than traces (linear), and where the equivalence notion is coarsest *strong simulation* rather than trace-equivalence. That framing is the paper's main conceptual move: reactive LPTSs correspond to a standard LTS, so trace-based learning techniques do not apply, and the tree-counterexample / Hall's-theorem-for-`≤_R` machinery has to be rebuilt.

## Strengths

1. **Correct, clean core.** The probabilistic simulation relation (Definition 3) via the weight function `w` and the Hall-style witness `S ≤ Supp(μ₁)` (Lemma 1) is a sound generalization; reducing distribution matching to a max-flow (cf. [4]) is the right tool and keeps the decision procedure polynomial-time for the conformality check itself. The "execution mapping" feature of the counterexample algorithm is correctly identified as the load-bearing property for termination.

2. **The undecidability/decidability separation is honest and well done.** Theorem 4 (undecidability under an unrestricted adversarial teacher) → Theorem 5 (decidability under the "friendly teacher" Condition 1) → Theorem 6 (semi-algorithm under the "minimum-state" learner Condition 2) is a crisp, three-way characterization. The adversarial-teacher constructions in the appendix (Algorithm 2, the `U_λ` / `H_λ` examples with incrementally nudgeable `λ ∈ (0,1)`) are convincing: the infinitely-many-rationals argument for non-convergence up to `≃` is real.

3. **Stochastic partition idea (Def 9, 10) is a genuine, if modest, insight.** The observation that the one-to-one grouping from the classical partition is the obstacle to minimality, and the clean fix of *stochastically* grouping sample states (each non-root sample state has a *valid group* `par(s)` whose support matches the target's distribution via a unique weight `w`) is elegant, and its encoding as a linear-rational-arithmetic satisfiability problem is a natural, terminating solver.

4. **The assume-guarantee section (V)** is a nice application: ASYM under simulation semantics, the spuriousness check, and the note that complexity can beat checking `L₁ ≤ L₂` directly when the assumption is small. The connection to the existing (mostly non-probabilistic) assume-guarantee literature is clearly laid out, with the novelty of bringing *learning* (rather than model checking) to the probabilistic case.

## Weaknesses

1. **No empirical evaluation.** This is the paper's biggest shortfall for a venue like ICICS that hosts both theory and practice. There is not a single experiment, benchmark, or case study. The `O(poly·2^k)` complexity is never instantiated; the "in practice this can be better than direct conformality" claim rests entirely on intuition. A reader cannot tell whether the learned LPTSs ever shrink the verification problem, what tool is used to implement the teacher/learner (the paper never even identifies one concretely), or how the ASYM rule scales. For a "learning" paper, the absence of data makes it a pure theory note.

2. **The "friendliness" condition (Condition 1) is asserted, not motivated.** The whole decidability result hinges on the positive counterexamples having an *execution mapping to `H`*. This is a very specific, somewhat surprising property of a teacher, and the paper only shows it happens to hold for the particular non-probabilistic algorithm of [14] "when adapted." It is not argued to be what *real* model checkers or the authors' own counterexample generators actually do. So the gap between "decidable in principle" and "useful in practice" is left open and, arguably, unbridgeable by this paper. This is the paper's clearest overclaim of applicability.

3. **Condition 2 (learner is restricted to minimum-state hypotheses) is a somewhat artificial restriction.** Forcing the *learner* (not the teacher or the problem) to conjecture only minimum consistent LPTSs is a knob that the practitioner controls at the learner's implementation level; it feels like the authors discovered a semi-algorithm and named it a solution rather than showing the natural learner is sound/complete. The paper is candid that this yields a semi-algorithm, which is good, but it leaves the reader uncertain whether a more natural (unrestricted) learner can be made to terminate by a small change to line 6 rather than a new encoding.

4. **Novelty is largely a probabilistic extension of a known recipe.** The abstract and related-work sections themselves say most of this is borrowed: the partition-based learner inherits from [24, 16, 6, 7, 18, 15], the active learning from [14], the complexity of simulation from [4], the `≤_R` / Hall machinery from [28]. What is genuinely new is (a) the *tree* (reactive) setting versus the trace setting, and (b) the *stochastic partition*. Both are real but incremental; the reader already knows the shape of the result if they know the non-probabilistic paper. The paper would benefit from a table stating exactly which results are new versus inherited.

5. **Complexity is given, not analyzed.** The worst-case bound `O(m 2^{dm^{dm+1-1}})` in Section V is presented without a discussion of whether it is tight, whether the `2^{dm}` dominates, or what `d`, `m` look like on realistic inputs. "Better than direct conformality in some cases" has no quantitative content.

6. **Notation density in the stochastic-partition section.** Definitions 9–10 with the `|s|` grouping function, the "valid group of its parent," the double use of `w`, and the lift machinery across the quotient introduce a lot of new symbols in roughly 1.5 pages. A worked numerical example (a single distribution being stochastically split across two target states) — which Section III-B begins to build but abandons — would pay for its length several times over.

7. **Minor.** Theorem 4's proof sketch ("we show there is no algorithm…") is somewhat compressed for a non-trivial undecidability-style result; the full adversarial construction lives in the appendix and is only loosely referenced from the theorem. Definition 2 (`w` weight function) is reused for two distinct purposes (distribution matching in §II and the group weight in the stochastic partition, §III/APP), which is a mild notational clash.

## What would make it a substantially better paper

- At least one end-to-end worked example (small LTS pair, full learned model) and a toy benchmark showing the ASYM rule produces a *smaller* assumption than direct checking.
- An argument (even informal) that Condition 1 is the one natural counterexample generators satisfy, or a statement of exactly which tools produce execution-mapped counterexamples.
- A short complexity table separating inherited vs. new results, and an honest statement of the open problem: a sound + complete learner for the natural setting (no Condition 2) under Condition 1, or a counterexample showing this is impossible.

## Bottom Line

This is a solid, correctly-written, moderately original theory paper from a strong formal-methods group (CMU + NASA Ames). The probabilistic-tree framing and the stochastic partition are genuine contributions, and the decidability / semi-algorithm boundary is drawn carefully. But the absence of any experimental evidence, the strength and under-motivation of the "friendly teacher" condition, and the fact that the method is largely a probabilistic transplant of a known recipe keep it from being a high-impact contribution. It is the kind of paper that becomes a useful *reference point* and stepping stone in a longer research line (and clearly is — the authors' later work on probabilistic compositional verification builds on it) rather than a landmark. It earns its publication, but a practitioner or experimental reader will find the takeaways thin.

## The stated contribution, in my own words

The paper attacks a specific learning problem: given a finite set of **positive tree samples** (stochastic trees that the unknown "reactive" LTS *does* execute) and **negative tree samples** (trees it does *not* execute), recover an unknown LTS (Labeled Probabilistic Transition System — a nondeterministic system whose transitions carry probability *distributions*, not point probabilities) such that (a) every positive tree is simulated by it and (b) no negative tree is — "consistency" being formalized as **strong simulation conformance**. Two concrete deliverables follow:

1. **Two synthesis algorithms** that turn samples into a quotient model. One works over *traditional* partitions of the sample state space (yielding a consistent LTS of least "number of partitions" and a provable `O(2^k)` state bound); the other introduces a **stochastic partition**, a new grouping in which a single distribution's probability mass can be *split* across several groups (each group keyed to a "parent" group), which the authors show collapses to the **least number of states**. Both reduce the synthesis to a **linear‑rational‑arithmetic satisfiability query**, so they are complete and terminate.

2. **An active‑learning framework** where a knowledgeable teacher answers membership + equivalence queries (equivalence = "does simulation hold between hypothesis and target, and if not, give me a witness tree"). The authors then map out a **decidability landscape**: undecidable in general (Theorem 4), **decidable** under a "friendlier teacher" condition (every returned tree counterexample has an *execution mapping* to the target — Condition 1, Theorem 5), and again undecidable if the learner is constrained to always conjecture a *minimum‑state* model (Theorem 6), for which they give a terminating *semi‑algorithm* via stochastic partitions.

3. **Application to assume‑guarantee verification**: the same active loop is repurposed to *inferring an intermediate assumption* A for the ASYM compositional rule, and the authors show this is **sound and complete** (termination follows from the decidability result).

## Is it novel, given the citations?

Mostly **yes in a narrow, well‑delimited core; no for a large scaffolding that is deliberately borrowed.**

**What is genuinely new:**

- **The stochastic partition (Def. 9).** This is the paper's real conceptual contribution. In the deterministic/LTS setting of [3, 6, 16, 23, 24, 26, 18], a partition class corresponds 1:1 to a leaf of the sample, so a consistent partition is *at most* `2^k` but need not be minimum (cf. their Fig. 6, where `H_1` uses extra states while `H_3` doesn't). Because an LPTS transition emits a *distribution*, that bijection breaks: two different sample nodes can legitimately be realized by *one* target state, and one state can feed *multiple* distributions. The stochastic partition encodes this by letting a parent state's distribution be *fractionally* allocated over groups, with the `par()` parent‑function and the constraint that every non‑root node be grouped under *some* parent (the two disjunct condition in Def. 9). This is the first probabilistic analog of the partitioning approach for minimum models, and it is what upgrades "upper bound" into "least number of states" (Lemma 10, Cor. 2). Clean idea, not present in any cited reference.

- **The conditional decidability characterization of active LPTS learning.** The trichotomy in Thm. 4/5/6 — undecidable → decidable under the *execution‑mapping* teacher condition → re‑undecidable under a minimum‑state learner — is a precise and nontrivial technical result, and the adversarial‑teacher construction in the appendix (the `λ`/`λ⁺`/`λ⁻` forcing argument, Algorithm 2) is original. No cited work establishes decidability of *active* learning for *distributional* (vs. point‑probability) systems.

- **The sound‑and‑complete assume‑guarantee application.** Prior attempts (notably [15]) used an `L*` learner with a **sound but *incomplete*** rule, with no termination guarantee. Here completeness is inherited from the learning results, and the "spuriousness check for negative counterexamples" that detects when ASYM's conclusion fails is an original touch. This gives the whole enterprise a concrete "why."

**What is *not* new — carried over or a mechanical lifting of cited work:**

- **The core simulation machinery.** The "match all probabilities" / *lifting a distribution* construction (the weight‑function argument, `μ₁ ⊒_R μ₂`, Lemma 1, Def. 3/4) is Segala–Lynch probabilistic simulation [28]; the strong‑simulation preorder/coarsest‑simulation facts, their polynomial‑time fixed‑point decision, and the "split the distributions before matching" intuition are all from [19] (the authors' own 2012 companion paper), which the paper leans on heavily.
- **The counterexample framework.** Defs. 5–6, Theorem 1 (existence of a *tree* counterexample), and the **execution mapping** notion (which is the hinge of Condition 1) are explicitly imported from [19] and the earlier [2012/26] line.
- **The partition‑to‑SAT reduction** (Section III‑A, Thm. 2) is the standard minimum‑automaton‑from‑samples recipe in Gold [16], García–Oncina [17], Oliveira–Marquès‑Silva [23], and Gupta et al. [18], just re‑encoded for distributions.

## [1207.5091v1-methodology] Methodology reproducibility


# Evaluation

## The paper

*"Learning Probabilistic Systems from Tree Samples"* (Komuravelli, Păsăreanu, Clarke; arXiv:1207.5091, LICS 2012 version). The paper develops a theory and algorithms for learning a non-deterministic Labeled Probabilistic Transition System (LPTS — the probabilistic, "probabilistic on transitions" analog of an LTS) from finite sets of positive and negative **stochastic trees** (finite tree-structured samples whose non-deterministic nodes carry probability distributions). It covers three things: (i) offline learning of a *consistent* LPTS from the samples, (ii) **active learning** under a knowledgeable teacher that answers *simulation conformance* (two-way simulation) queries with tree counterexamples, and (iii) application of the machinery to automate **assume-guarantee** compositional verification for LPTS. There are **no empirical experiments**; every "experiment" is an algorithm + theorem, and reproducibility means re-implementing the SMT encodings and query protocols described below.

## Methodology in reproducible detail

**1. Formal objects (Sec. II).**
- *LPTS* $L = (S_L, s_L^0, \alpha, \tau_L)$: states, distinguished start state, finite action set, and a transition relation $\tau_L \subseteq S_L \times \alpha \times \mathrm{Dist}(S_L)$ mapping pairs to probability distributions (rational weights in $[0,1]$). Models are **reactive**: at most one distribution per state/action. The non-reactive case is "resolved" by distributing the choice; the paper explicitly says the theory is not thereby made simpler and treats reactive LPTS throughout.
- *Stochastic trees* (e.g. the $L_1, L_2$ in Fig. 1, $C_p$): a tree in which every non-root node is in the support of exactly one distribution; the target of learning.
- *Simulation conformance* $\sqsubseteq_R$ between distributions (Def. 2): there exists a weight function $w: S_1 \times S_2 \to \mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$ satisfying the two matching equations (1)–(2). This generalizes classical strong simulation (Def. 3) to distributions with **splitting** of probability mass (the Fig. 3 example: $\tfrac12$ split into $\tfrac16+\tfrac16$).
- *Strong simulation equivalence* $\simeq$ (Def. 4) = kernel of strong simulation; computed by a greatest-fixed-point algorithm (citing Baier–Bravetti–Segala [4] and its optimizations [30]); polynomial time (Lemma 2).
- *Counterexamples* (Def. 5–6): $L_1 \not\succeq L_2$ (language) iff there is a tree $C$ with $C \preceq L_1$ but not $C \preceq L_2$; **Theorem 1** (from [19]) guarantees such a $C$ can be taken to be a *tree*, and the GFA algorithm can be instrumented (by Lemma 4, which ties tree-relatedness $R\subseteq\preceq$ to distribution conformance) to produce counterexamples with an **execution mapping** onto the target when conformance fails.

**2. Offline learning of a consistent LPTS (Sec. III).**
Given finite $\mathcal{P}\neq\emptyset$ (positive) and $\mathcal{N}$ (negative) stochastic trees, with $S_{\mathcal P}=\bigcup_{P\in\mathcal P}S_P$, $S_{\mathcal N}=\bigcup N$ and the standing assumption that a consistent LPTS $L$ exists (checkable in poly time by trying to merge start states).

- *Algorithm A — state partitions (III-A):* a quotient $P/\Pi$ (Def. 7) of a tree-union $P$ by a partition $\Pi$ of $S_{\mathcal P}$ (equivalence classes $\varepsilon_1,\dots,\varepsilon_k$, the start state of *all* roots placed in $\varepsilon_1$) is always a well-formed LPTS and is consistent with $\mathcal P$ (Lemma 6). Consistency with $\mathcal N$ requires $N \not\preceq P/\Pi$. **Lemma 7** (from [23], [16], [18]) is the key reduction: a consistent $k$-state LPTS $\Rightarrow$ a consistent partition of size $\le 2^k$. The algorithm enumerates candidate sizes $k$ (from 0 up: by Corollary 1, bounded by $2^{|S_L|}$) and solves, **as a satisfiability problem over linear rational arithmetic**, the encoding:
  - integer-class choice variables $v_{\lceil s\rceil=i}$ (equivalence class $i$ for node $s$);
  - well-definedness: $\bigvee_{j\le k}v_{\lceil s\rceil=j}$ per node; all roots in $\varepsilon_1$;
  - consistency: for each $N\in\mathcal N$ a Boolean $R_{s,i}$ per pair with $\lceil s_2\rceil=i$, encoding $R_{s,i} \iff \bigwedge_{(h_i,(a,\mu_\mu),t_p)}\bigvee_{b\in X(s)} t_p\in \mathrm{act}(t_n)$ (Lemma 4 form); the nested existential over weight functions of Def. 2 is eliminated by introducing contribution variables $\iota_{\mu_P,\tilde\mu_P}$ per $(s, \mathrm{Supp}(\mu_P))$ witness-subset indicator variables, and $d_{\mu_P,\mathrm{lift}(\mu_P)}$ encoded subject to $\sum_{s\in\mathrm{Supp}(\mu_P)}\iota = \mu_P(s)$ and $\lceil s\rceil = i \Rightarrow \iota = 0$; and $\neg R_{s_1,i}$ for each $N$;
  - **Theorem 2**: the resulting algorithm (incrementing $k$) terminates, because a solution exists whenever a consistent $L$ exists (Corollary 1).
- *Algorithm B — stochastic partitions (III-B):* motivated by the observation (Lemma 7's bound is exponential and a *least-size consistent partition* is not guaranteed to yield a least-size LPTS — the $H_1$ in Fig. 6 counterexample) that in a *minimum* consistent LPTS the quotient map is necessarily one-to-one between $S_P$ and $S_L$. A stochastic partition (Def. 9) is $\Pi=(G, \{[s]\}_{s\in S_P})$ with groups $G\subseteq 2^{S_P}$ and $[s]: G\to\mathrm{Dist}(G)$ assigning each node a distribution over the groups it maps its children's probabilities into, subject to (1) the root group $g^\diamond$ maps all roots deterministically and (2) every non-root node is defined only for its parent's support group, with $\lceil s\rceil$ summing to 1. Its quotient $P/\Pi$ (Def. 10) lifts distributions by $\mu(g')=\sum_{s\in g}\lceil s\rceil(g(g'))\cdot\mu_P(s)$; **Lemma 8** shows the quotient is always a well-formed LPTS; the encoding is again linear rational arithmetic with non-negative rational variables $v_{\lceil s\rceil(g)}(j)$, constraints $\sum_j v_{\lceil s\rceil(g)}(j)=1$ or all-zero, root and parent-support constraints, and consistency conditions identical to before. **Theorem 3** gives termination/decidability and (via Lemma 9–10 and the proof in Appendix E) that learning a **minimum-state** consistent LPTS from the samples is achievable by taking the quotient of a least-size consistent *stochastic* partition.

**3. Active learning (Sec. IV).**
- *Algorithm 1 (learning loop)*: initialize $\mathcal P=\mathcal N=\emptyset$, $H$ = single-state LPTS with no transitions; repeat: ask the teacher whether $H \approx U$ (simulation equivalence); on failure, add the returned positive counterexample ($H\not\succeq U$, meaning $H\not\preceq U$) to $\mathcal P$ or the negative one to $\mathcal N$; recompute the least-size consistent (stochastic) partition $\Pi$; set $H\leftarrow \mathcal P/\Pi$.
- **Theorem 4**: this is **undecidable** in general — an adversarial teacher $U_\lambda$ (Fig. 7: a root $s_0^0 \xrightarrow{a} \lambda$ branching into a state with $b$-loop and one that deadlocks on $b$) can manipulate $\lambda$ forever so no hypothesis is simulation-equivalent, since a positive counterexample need not carry an execution mapping to $U$.
- **Condition 1 (Friendly Teacher)**: every returned counterexample has an *execution mapping* to $U(H)$. Under it, **Lemma 11** shows the loop terminates: the partition induced at the positive-counterexample side is a substructure of $U$, so the least-size consistent partition has size bounded by $\lceil|S_U|\rceil$; every future hypothesis is consistent with all previous counterexamples, and with the finitely many probability vectors the teacher can pick for a partition class (Condition 1 ⇒ finitely many distributions per partition size, since probabilities are rational), the teacher cannot keep producing new hypotheses ⇒ **Theorem 5**: decidability of active learning under Condition 1.
- **Condition 2** restricts the *learner* to the stochastic-partition (minimum-state) algorithm, yielding a semi-algorithm; **Theorem 6** shows learning a minimum-state LPTS equivalent to $U$ is still **undecidable** when both conditions hold (adversary in the appendix, Fig. 6's $H_\lambda$).

**4. Application: assume-guarantee (Sec. V).**
The rule **ASYM** (from [19]): from premises $L_1\|A \preceq P$ and $L_2\preceq A$ conclude $L_1\|L_2\preceq P$, where $\|$ is parallel composition for LPTS (Def. 12, following [28]) over shared alphabet. The teacher checks premise 1 (discharging the environment of the full composition) and premise 2 against the learned $A$ — sound and complete. If premise 2 fails, the returned positive counterexample is a positive one to the active loop; if premise 1 fails, the counterexample is **projected onto $A$** (composition with $L_2$ and dropping spurious parts, per [19]) and returned as a *negative* counterexample, preserving Condition 1. A **spuriousness check** prevents vacuous premises: since $L_1\|L_2\not\preceq P$ while no $A$ satisfies both premises would violate existence of a consistent LPTS (Sec. III), the learner verifies each negative counterexample $N$ is real ($N\not\preceq L_2$). The complexity analysis (V) is worst-case: premise checks are $O(\poly(|L_1|,|L_2|,\ldots)\cdot O(\poly(|L_1|\cdot|L_2|,|P|))$), i.e. polynomial per check; the number of *iterations* (candidate assumptions) is bounded by $O\big(2^{dm^{dm+1}}\big)$, **exponential** in $d=\max(|S_{L}|,|S_{L'}|)$, the maximum support size of an $L_2$ distribution — the paper concedes this is a "worst case" bound and practice is expected to be much better (small $A$), with "better algorithms" left to future work.

**To reproduce the central experiment** (active learning to discover $A$ verifying ASYM): obtain a knowledgeable teacher able to (a) answer $H\approx U$ by running the poly-time greatest-fixed-point strong-simulation check and returning stochastic **tree** counterexamples with execution mappings (e.g. via instrumented Segala–Lynch-style algorithms from [19]/[28]), (b) implement premise checks $L_1\|A\preceq P$ and $L_2\preceq A$ for compositional LPTS; then drive Algorithm 1 with the stochastic-partition SMT solver (rational linear arithmetic, one instance per $k$, starting $k=0$). The deliverable is the learned $A$ and the terminating learning trace; "success" (vs. non-termination) is the testable outcome, governed by Conditions 1–2.

## Assumptions the methodology papers over

1. **Teacher existence/oracle idealization (the biggest one).** Condition 1 ("friendly teacher" returning counterexamples with execution mappings) is *assumed, not constructed*. The paper says a concrete teacher algorithm that *guarantees* execution-mapped counterexamples in general "is not aware of" and "one needs to implement the teacher's algorithm" — i.e., the entire active-learning contribution rests on an unimplemented, unverifiable oracle. No tool, benchmark, or even a worked end-to-end example validating Condition 1 empirically appears.
2. **Simulation equivalence, not behavioral equivalence, is what's learned.** The learner only queries $H\approx U$ (two-way *strong* simulation), so the learned LPTS is guaranteed only up to the **coarsest strong simulation** (Lemma 2) — pointwise path probabilities of the true system are *not* recovered; the paper treats this as acceptable motivation ("assume-guarantee") but papers over that exact-model identification is simply out of scope.
3. **No membership queries.** Membership is "not straightforward to create" for the teacher and is explicitly dropped in favor of equivalence queries; the resulting decidability boundary (Thm 4 vs. Thm 5) depends heavily on this choice, which is argued *post hoc* rather than analyzed comparatively.
4. **Rational, exact probabilities with exact tree samples.** All weights are assumed rational in $[0,1]$ and fully specified in the sample trees — the entire SMT/linear-rational-arithmetic encoding, the finite-distribution-count termination argument (Lemma 11, "finitely many possible distributions for a given partition size" — because rationals are well-ordered by numerator/denominator… in fact finiteness relies on the execution mapping to $U$), and the undecidability proofs all silently presuppose exact arithmetic. No discussion of noisy/empirical probabilities, approximate sampling, or the statistical-error budget that a real probabilistic system would require (cf. the probabilistic-learning-related work [6], [7] the paper itself cites).
5. **Consistency of the sample is assumed up-front.** "We always assume the existence of a consistent LPTS" (Sec. III, opening) — existence is checkable in poly time by merging roots, but the algorithms' correctness statements (Lemma 7, Corollary 1, Thms 2–3) are conditional on it, and inconsistent sample sets have no defined behavior.
6. **The reactive-LPTS restriction.** Non-deterministic LPTSs are "disallowed" for a given action by a distribution; the reduction from general to reactive under-approximation is asserted ("This modeling is typically used for under-approximation") but the under-approximation *error* — how much of the source system's behavior is lost — is never quantified.
7. **Exponential size gap in the partition algorithm.** The bound $2^k$ (Lemma 7) is known to be loose (the paper itself exhibits $H_1$ in Fig. 6 being "consistent but not minimum") and the stochastic-partition fix is only guaranteed to be minimum *with respect to partitions*, not across all LPTS; the actual state explosion of $S_{\mathcal P}$ (all positive trees joined) is never discussed numerically.
8. **Complexity results are worst-case and asymptotic only.** The $O(2^{dm^{dm+1}})$-iteration bound (Sec. V) is stated without a matching empirical study; the paper explicitly flags "in practice… this approach can be better" and "we would need better algorithms; we leave this as future work" — i.e., the only evidence for tractability of the *applied* workflow is one inequality comparison.
9. **Spuriousness check is only heuristic-grade.** The definition of "real" vs. "spurious" counterexample against ASYM (Sec. V) is "a simple way to check $N\not\preceq L_2$" — a sufficient-not-necessary test, so the assume-guarantee procedure may spuriously reject a genuinely falsified premise and force the learner to "revise its hypothesis," a failure mode the paper merely points to ("A slightly more correct, but more practical way… is described elsewhere [19]").
10. **Theorem 6's undecidability is a "semi-algorithm" in the strongest sense**: under Conditions 1 *and* 2 there is no algorithm that terminates on all inputs, yet the paper's headline application (automated assume-guarantee) uses exactly Condition 2; the paper papers over this by saying "in practice… a smaller assumption satisfies the premises" — an appeal to practice with no data.
11. **Teacher power in Sec. V is quietly escalated.** Checking premise 1 requires conformance of the *composition* $L_1\|A$ to $P$, i.e., the teacher must itself be able to do compositional verification — exactly the capability the method is meant to *replace* by learning. The teacher is thus assumed to be at least as strong as the technique it drives.
12. **Tree counterexamples as the only sample type.** The whole framework assumes "membership queries are not needed" and equivalence-checks return trees; systems whose distinguishing behavior requires *looped* (non-tree) evidence would be unlearnable in this setup — an assumption made visible only through the $N_a, N_b, N_c^{\beta,\gamma}$ negative samples in Fig. 5, which are *trees with distributions*, so any non-tree distinguishing structure is excluded by the model class, not argued to be sufficient.
13. **No scalability analysis of the SMT encodings.** The linear-rational-arithmetic encodings (Def. 7, 9, 10) blow up in $|S_{\mathcal P}|\cdot k$ variables with $\mathrm{Supp}(\mu_P)$-sized nested constraints per pair $(s,i)\in\mathcal N$ per $N$ — the paper claims "satisfiability over linear rational arithmetic is decidable… terminates" (Thms 2–3) without any discussion of solver behavior, and no implementation exists (the LICS 2012 version has no tool; the "semi-algorithm" phrasing in the abstract confirms the gap).
14. **Proof sketches replace full proofs for the load-bearing results.** Thms 4–6, Lemmas 7, 8, 9, 11, 12 carry "Proof Sketch" labels; the Appendix supplies full proofs of Lemmas 2, 4, 7–10 but the adversarial constructions underlying Thms 4 and 6 are only sketched (the $U_\lambda$ adversary and the Appendix's $H_\lambda$ strategy give intuition rather than a fully formal non-halving argument).

## Verdict

A technically substantial LICS-grade theory paper: the distribution-conformance lifting, the two-partition learning duality (exponential-but-complete vs. minimum-state-via-stochastic-partition), and the undecidability-vs.-friendly-teacher separation are genuine contributions to a real gap (no probabilistic counterexample-driven learner existed before this). Reproducibility of the *claimed* methods is in principle possible (SMT encodings are written out; algorithms are explicit), but the paper's own central *application* — the assume-guarantee loop — depends on a teacher oracle whose existence is neither built, benchmarked, nor even sketched for the general case, and there are zero empirical experiments. The methodology is sound-as-written but leans on several quietly strong external assumptions (exact rational trees, execution-mapped counterexamples, a teacher that can do compositional checking itself, worst-case-complexity hand-waving).

## Understanding of the paper's logical argument

**Problem and motivation.** The paper studies learning an unknown non-deterministic *Labeled Probabilistic Transition System* (LPTS) whose "language" is a set of *stochastic trees* (trees with probability distributions on the transitions). The samples are positive trees 𝒫 and negative trees 𝒩, and "consistency" means finding a model L such that every P ∈ 𝒫 is strongly-simulated by L and no N ∈ 𝒩 is (L is *consistent with the tree samples*). The motivation is compositional, assume-guarantee verification: strong-simulation conformance between LPTSS is decidable in polynomial time, and *stochastic trees* are precisely the re-shapable counterexamples it yields. Since no probabilistic active-learning algorithm for tree languages/tree automata existed, they build one.

**Preliminaries (Sec. II).** The key technical tool is Definition 3: a probability-matching relation μ₁ ⊑_R μ₂ between two distributions, defined via a *weight function* that "splits" probabilities so all mass under μ₁ can be matched exactly against μ₂ while pairing states in R. The authors show this is the probabilistic analog of Hall's theorem on saturating sets, and (Lemma 3–4) that on trees the coarsest such matching coincides with the strong-simulation preorder restricted to the tree — a crucial fact that later makes teacher conditions checkable. They also define tree counterexamples: C is a counterexample to L₁ ⊴ L₂ iff C ⊑ L₁ and not C ⊑ L₂ (Theorem 1 guarantees a *tree* counterexample always exists).

**Passive learning (Sec. III).** Given (𝒫, 𝒩), learning a consistent LPTS reduces to partitioning the state space of the union tree 𝒮_𝒫:

- *Section III-A (classical partitions):* a quotient of 𝒫 by any partition Π is again an LPTS and is consistent with all positives (Lemma 6); any consistent LPTS of k states has a consistent partition of size ≤ 2^k (Lemma 7). Finding a *least-sized* consistent partition is cast as an SMT/linear-rational-arithmetic problem, which is decidable and gives an upper bound on model size (Theorem 2).
- *Section III-B (stochastic partitions):* the paper's main new idea. Classical partitions fail because probabilities in the target are realized by *splitting* mass over several successor distributions (one source state "feeds" several states of L), so states of 𝒮_𝒫 need grouping, not 1-to-1 partitioning. A *stochastic partition* assigns each non-root state to exactly one group of its *parent's* grouping, via a distribution over the groups (Def. 9). Quotients by stochastic partitions are well-defined LPTSS (Lemma 8), consistent (Lemma 9), and — the payoff — for *every* consistent LPTS of k states there is a stochastic partition of size exactly k (Lemma 10, Cor. 2). Hence the least consistent stochastic partition yields a *minimum-state* consistent LPTS, decidable via a similar rational-arithmetic encoding (Theorem 3).

**Active learning (Sec. IV).** Algorithm 1: in each round the learner conjectures a quotient H of an (stochastic, under condition 2) partition of the tree spanned by the counterexamples; a black-box teacher answers equivalence queries with a simulation counterexample H ≄ U (or U ≄ H), which is folded into 𝒩 (or projected and then moved to 𝒩 under the "spuriousness check" of Sec. V).

- *Undecidable in general* (Theorem 4): an adversarial teacher that merely tweaks a loop probability λ forever can keep generating consistent counterexamples.
- *Decidable under a "friendly teacher" condition* (Condition 1: every returned counterexample has an **execution mapping** back to the conjectured H; Theorem 5, via Lemma 11): the hypothesis is always a substructure of U (hence simulates it), partition sizes are bounded by |𝒮_C|, and the set of lifted distributions over a fixed partition is finite — so hypotheses cannot recur and the loop terminates.
- *The minimum-state variant is undecidable* even with a friendly teacher (Theorem 6): Algorithm 2 gives an explicit adversarial teacher (the 3-way tree U_λ of Fig. 7) that, by nudging λ each round, forces the learner to keep conjecting ever-larger minimum models and never converge. So under Condition 2 the procedure is only a *semi-algorithm*.

**Assume-guarantee reasoning (Sec. V).** The application: automate the ASYM rule (L₁ ∥ A ≤ P and L₂ ∼ A ⇒ L₁ ∥ L₂ ≤ P). Premise 1 is learned with the friendly-teacher condition (execution-mapped counterexamples keep the rule sound); premise 2 is learned by checking N ≼ L₂ on counterexamples. A spuriousness check (N not ~ L₂) prevents sound but vacuous assumptions. The whole procedure is sound and complete, but exponential in the size of the learned assumptions (roughly O(m·2^dm·d^dm·…) total).

**Strengths.**

1. **The stochastic-partition construction is the paper's genuine core and it is well motivated.** The failure mode of classical partitions (a source state's distribution feeding several target states, as in Figs. 3 and 5) is cleanly diagnosed, and the fix — grouping relative to a parent's grouping, Def. 9 — is exactly what Def. 10's quotient needs to be a well-defined *minimum* model (Lemma 10's "≤ k" bound, versus Lemma 7's "≤ 2^k", is a sharp, well-chosen contrast). (c) The composition of LPTSS (Def. 12) and the spuriousness check in Sec. V are described rather than defined in full (deferred to [19]), so a reader cannot verify soundness/completeness of the assume-guarantee rule from this paper alone.
5. Related work is thorough but the differentiation from PA-learning literature (refs. [13], [21], [29]) is somewhat asserted: it's not fully clear why the tree-based, execution-mapping approach is strictly preferable to adapting PA-learning when the target is in fact a PA.

Proofs are sketched in the main text with details in the appendix, which is acceptable but occasionally terse (Def. 9's "implicitly assume [s | g](g′) = 0" convention is exactly the sort of point that needs a one-line justification).

**Overall.** A solid, well-organized theoretical extension of counterexample/partition-based learning from deterministic tree automata to the probabilistic nondeterministic setting, with one genuinely new construction (stochastic partitions yielding minimum consistent models) and a nuanced set of decidability/undecidability results. Its limits are the absence of any empirical validation and the heavy, only-semi-formalized teacher assumptions — the paper is a compelling theory paper with an application story that remains, by the authors' own admission, open.

## Evaluation of "Learning Probabilistic Systems from Tree Samples" (arXiv:1207.5091, LICS 2012)

### Strongest Quantitative Claim

The paper's strongest quantitative claim is the pair of **decidability/undecidability results for active learning of LPTs**:

- **Theorem 4**: Learning an unknown LPT under simulation equivalence is **undecidable** in the active learning framework (without additional restrictions).
- **Theorem 5**: The problem becomes **decidable** under Condition 1 (every counterexample has an execution mapping to the hypothesis), with the number of distinct intermediate hypotheses bounded by |S_U|.
- **Theorem 6**: Imposing both Condition 1 (friendlier teacher) and Condition 2 (learner always conjectures minimum-state LPTs, i.e., uses stochastic partitions) yields a **decidable** algorithm even with stochastic partitions.

These results are supported by a complexity analysis in Section V giving the total worst-case complexity as O(2^{d·k} · k^{d-1} · poly(|A|, |P|) + poly(|L_2|, |P|)), where k is the number of states in the final assumption and d is the maximum support size.

### Audit of Evidence

**Is the comparison fair?**
There is no empirical comparison — this is a pure theory paper. The "comparison" is internal: standard state-space partitioning (Section III-A) vs. stochastic partitioning (Section III-B). The paper demonstrates via Figure 6 that standard partitioning can yield a 2-state quotient where stochastic partitioning yields a 1-state quotient, establishing strictness. This comparison is fair because both methods operate on the same sample space S_P and the same consistency requirements — the only difference is the grouping mechanism. The 2^k upper bound for standard partitions vs. the k bound for stochastic partitions (Lemma 10) makes the advantage precise.

**Are baselines current?**

## [1207.5091v1-weak-point] Weakest link in the argument


## Evaluation

**Paper:** Komuravelli, Păsăreanu, Clarke — "Learning Probabilistic Systems from Tree Samples" (arXiv:1207.5091v1).

The paper's central claim is that an unknown non-deterministic LPTS can be *actively learned* to simulation equivalence: it is undecidable in general (Thm 4), becomes decidable if the teacher is "friendly" — every counterexample it returns comes with an *execution mapping* into the target (Condition 1, yielding Thm 5) — and is undecidable again even if the learner is restricted to minimum-state hypotheses (Thm 6).

### The weakest link: the termination proof of Lemma 11 / Theorem 5

This is the load-bearing step, and it is the one place where the conclusion "learning is decidable" is *not* actually entailed by what is shown. The termination argument has three ingredients, and two of them are soft:

1. **The finiteness claim is asserted, not proved.** The proof sketch of Lemma 11 terminates the loop because (a) each hypothesis is a *least-size* consistent quotient, so its size is bounded by |S_U|, and (b) "we can show that there are only finitely many possible distributions for a given partition size." That last sentence is the entire engine of the argument — the bound on the *number* of hypotheses is not a bound on size (size alone is useless, since |S_U|-state LPTS have infinitely many rational transition probabilities) but a claim that Condition 1 collapses the probability degrees of freedom to a finite set inherited from the teacher's returned, execution-mapped counterexamples. The authors flag it with "we can show" and give no derivation. If distribution values can drift with each new counterexample, the "finitely many hypotheses" bound — and hence termination, and hence all of Theorem 5 — falls. Since decidability is the paper's headline positive result, this is precisely where the data stops entailing the conclusion.

2. **Decidability is really a *relative* decidability result, and the paper concedes this.** Theorem 5 is conditional on Condition 1, but the very next paragraph admits: "in practice, one needs to implement the teacher's algorithm and we are not aware of any algorithm to generate counterexamples other than the one discussed in Section III," and that algorithm does *not* produce execution mappings. So the condition that rescues decidability is an oracle whose existence/satisfiability is never established. The result is therefore "decidable relative to a hypothetical oracle" dressed as "decidable." Theorem 4's adversarial proof and Thm 6's undecidability sandwich actually show the real, implementable setting is undecidable; the positive result only holds against a teacher nobody has shown how to build. So the argument chain's positive conclusion leans entirely on an unauxiliary-assumption that is simultaneously (a) unproved in its finiteness content and (b) unrealizable in practice.

### Other (lesser) soft spots

- **Lemma 10 / Corollary 2 (stochastic partitions ⇒ minimum state).** The contrast with classical partitions (bounded by 2^k, Cor 1) is the paper's second technical contribution. The proof sketch that a *k*-state consistent LPTS gives a *k*-group stochastic partition is plausible but compressed; the one-group-per-target-state construction in the motivating figure depends on the lifting being a valid distribution (Lemma 8), which is where the algebra is quietly doing work.
- **Section V (assume–guarantee).** The complexity bound relies on the final assumption A being *small*, and the authors themselves note "we would need better algorithms to address the problem" when it is not — an acknowledged, rather than argued, gap.

None of these is fatal to the paper; the definitions of strong simulation matching via weight functions and Hall's theorem (Lemma 1) and the satisfiability encodings (Thm 2, 3) are sound and the undecidability results are clean. But the decisive step — Lemma 11's termination, underpinning Theorem 5 — is where "we can show" is doing the work a proof should.

**Soundness verdict:** The paper is largely sound in its local lemmas; its overall positive contribution (decidability of active LPTS learning) rests on an unproven finiteness assertion and on a teacher condition the authors themselves cannot implement. That makes the headline positive result weaker than it reads, without invalidating the framework, the constructional algorithms, or the negative results.

## Summary

The paper studies learning an unknown non-deterministic Labeled Probabilistic Transition System (LPTS) from a finite set of positive and negative *stochastic tree* samples, with consistency defined w.r.t. strong simulation conformance. Two learning algorithms are proposed: (i) one based on traditional state-space partitioning of the positive samples, which yields a least *partition* (and hence 2^k-state quotients for k-state targets), and (ii) a novel "stochastic partition," in which a sample state is distributed *stochastically* across multiple groups (motivated by the flow-network/weight-function structure of the distribution simulation relation ⪯R), which yields a *minimum-state* consistent LPTS. Both reduce to satisfiability over linear rational arithmetic. The algorithms are then plugged into an active-learning loop using equivalence queries only. The paper proves: undecidability in general (Theorem 4), decidability under a "friendly teacher" condition (positive counterexamples must carry execution mappings to the target, guaranteed by the standard coarsest-simulation counterexample algorithm — Theorem 5), undecidability under an added minimum-state-learner condition (Theorem 6), yielding a semi-algorithm via stochastic partitions. Finally, the framework automates intermediate-assumption discovery in an assume-guarantee rule for strong simulation conformance of LPTSes, claimed to be the first complete, fully automated learning framework for compositional verification of probabilistic systems.

## Strengths

1. **Genuinely new problem and method.** Active learning of *non-deterministic* probabilistic systems from tree counterexamples had not been addressed; the related work on learning PAs [de la Higuera/Oncina, Zeng, Beimel et al.] targets different models, teachers, or is approximate. The stochastic partition — a probability-weighted generalization of state folding, dual to the weight functions witnessing µ₁⪯Rµ₂ (Lemma 1, the Hall-type characterization) — is a clean and original idea.

2. **Precise decidability map.** The triad of results (undecidable / decidable under teacher friendliness / undecidable with minimum-state learner) is exactly the right way to characterize the boundary, and the adversarial-teacher constructions (Algorithms 2, the Hλ/H1 chase in Appendix G–H) are tight and convincing — a teacher manipulating an unknown λ, or an infinitely descending family of consistent two-state hypotheses Hλ with λ<β, is the canonical refutation technique done carefully.

3. **Coherent motivation and completeness.** Framing membership queries as unavailable (the learner cannot guess transition probabilities *and* tree structure) is a defensible and useful observation for the counterexample-guided setting, and it is what forces the tree-based sample theory in Section III. The assume-guarantee application closes the loop nicely: the completeness of the AG rule (A := L₂ is discharging) is precisely what guarantees termination, and the teacher's Condition 1 is not an assumption but a theorem about the counterexample generator.

4. **Good exposition.** Figures 2–4 (direct matching vs. split-then-match, and the Figure 4 counterexample showing Lemma 4 requires L₁ to be a tree) make the core probability-matching idea very legible. The complexity analysis of the AG loop is concrete.

## Weaknesses

1. **No experiments — the most significant defect for a journal-grade paper.** The contribution is heavily algorithmic and its selling point is practical (automating AG verification at NASA-scale systems), yet there is not a single case study, toy benchmark, or complexity-of-satisfiability-instance measurement. The paper asserts "in practice, if the assumption is small (|A| ≪ |L₂|) this approach can be better than checking L₁∥L₂ directly," and the whole Section V payoff rests on such empirical behavior. This claim, and the feasibility of the QRA/NLRA encodings on anything but trivial instances, are entirely untested.

2. **Main positive result for minimum-state learning is only a semi-algorithm.** Theorem 6 shows even a friendly teacher does not yield a decision procedure under the most natural (minimum hypothesis) learner restriction, so the minimum-state guarantee of the second algorithm is available only offline (given P, N) and not in the active loop. For the flagship AG application, the algorithm used is the *classical* partition one, which is the weaker algorithm; the stochastic-partition semi-algorithm's non-termination is acknowledged but not mitigated (e.g., by a bound on hypotheses, or by showing empirically that it converges). A theorem characterizing when the semi-algorithm does terminate would materially strengthen the paper.

3. **Proofs and encoding details deferred.** The QRA encodings for ¬dµn,µp (Section III-A, "the details are straightforward and left to the reader") and for the stochastic variant are where most of the technical substance sits, and their correctness is asserted, not shown. It is correctly placed at LICS and the technical core is sound. The main things keeping it from being exceptional are the absence of *any* empirical validation, a central result that degrades to a semi-algorithm, and a couple of load-bearing steps that are deferred to "the reader."

## What works well

- **The problem is new and well-motivated.** Prior probabilistic learning lived on trace-acceptance probabilities (Feng et al.) or on stronger teacher assumptions (Tzeng) or stochastic tree languages with acceptance probabilities. Nobody was learning from raw stochastic trees with simulation semantics, and you explain why the tree-automaton route is unavailable. This is a real gap you've filled, not an incremental re-skin.

- **Stochastic partitioning (Section III-B, Definition 9) is the best idea in the paper.** The observation that classical partitioning gives the *fewest partitions* but *not* the fewest states (Lemma 7's 2^k bound vs. the H₁ vs. H_λ example in Figs. 5–6), and the fix — a "partition" where a state is placed stochastically into multiple groups by fractional, parent-dependent membership — is elegant and genuinely original. Corollary 2 (size ≤ k, no 2^k overhead) is the clean payoff.

- **The active-learning trichotomy is satisfying.** Theorem 4 (undecidable in general, adversarial teacher manipulating λ) → Theorem 5 (decidable under the "friendly teacher" Condition 1, via classical partitions + Lemma 11 monotonicity argument) → Theorem 6 (undecidable even *with* a minimum-state-hypothesis Condition 2). Mapping the boundary this cleanly, with concrete adversarial-teacher constructions (Algorithms 2, Figs. 7), is exactly the kind of result that makes a theory paper solid. Condition 1 being "natural" because it comes from how the conformance checker [19] actually generates counterexamples is a nice touch — it's not a straw assumption.

- **Correct reduction of the probability-matching core.** Leaning on maxflow + the Hall-theorem analog (Lemma 1, [30]) for µ₁ ⊑R µ₂, and casting consistency to satisfiability over linear rational arithmetic, is the right and standard machinery — it makes decidability immediate rather than hand-waved in principle.

## Where it needs work

1. **No experiments, at all.** This is the single biggest gap. You claim "the **first** complete and fully automated learning framework for compositional verification of probabilistic systems" (intro, and it's repeated as the payoff of Section V). For LICS a theory-only paper is acceptable, but making a "first ... fully automated framework" claim with zero case studies, benchmarks, or even a small worked end-to-end example is an overclaim relative to the evidence. I'd want at least one concrete system (an MDP pair) where you actually run the assume-guarantee loop, and numbers showing the learned assumption A is *smaller* than |L₂|, as you assert "in practice" in the complexity analysis. Without that, the most application-forward sentence in the paper sits on assertion.

2. **The headline "minimum state" result is only a semi-algorithm.** This is the tension at the heart of the paper and you handle it honestly, but I'd foreground it more. You *want* to learn the minimum-state LPTS (that's your stated goal and the whole point of stochastic partitioning), yet under Condition 2 (the natural "always conjecture minimal" restriction) the problem is *undecidable* (Thm 6), so the stochastic-partition route only gives "if it terminates, it's minimum-state." And the route that *does* terminate (classical partitions, Lemma 11) gives bounded size, *not* minimum. So in the setting that actually matters for the application you have to choose: guaranteed-termination-but-large, or minimal-but-maybe-nonterminating. The conclusion gestures at fixing this ("investigate further conditions ... with stochastic partitions decidable") — but that *is* the missing piece. As it stands, the theoretical guarantee and the ideal objective don't meet. That's an honest result, but it should be framed as the paper's central open limitation, not buried.

3. **The LRA encodings are deferred.** In both III-A and III-B you build the reduction toward an SMT-over-LRA formula and then say "the details are straightforward and left to the reader." The encoding *is* the decidability argument's workhorse — that's the step that turns "find a consistent partition of size ≤ k" into something the solver will actually run. For a paper whose theorems (2 and 3) are decided by termination of this search, I'd want either the full encoding in an appendix or at least one worked formula on the Fig. 5 instance. "Straightforward" is doing a lot of quiet work here, and a reviewer (or I) can't verify the encoding's correctness from the prose. The quotient/well-definedness proofs (Lemma 8 appendix) are solid, but the constraint construction is not shown.

4. **Complexity is analyzed only for Section V, not for the core learning algorithms.** You give a careful O(m²dᵐ) bound for the assume-guarantee learning loop — good — but Thm 2 and Thm 3's algorithms (search k from 0, SAT@LRA at each step) have no stated complexity, and Thm 3's search is bounded only implicitly (by |S_P|, since the merged L_P is an a-priori consistent LPTS). Please make that bound explicit; right now a reader has to reconstruct that the minimum-state search terminates by k ≤ |S_P|.

5. **Reactive-vs-general is a bit ambiguous.** Your definitions cover general LPTSes (multiple distributions per action), but the counterexample structure, the Fig. 1 Cp analysis, the "structures simpler than trees are insufficient" remark, and the assume-guarantee composition all lean on tree/reactive structure. I'd state once, crisply, which of your results assume reactivity and which are fully general, because it affects how strong the "learning general LPTSes" framing really is.

## Smaller items

- Figures 5/6 carry the key H₁-vs-H_λ argument; make sure the caption stands alone (a reader should be able to see *why* H₁ is least-sized-classical-but-not-minimum and H_λ is minimum, purely from the figure).
- "Proof Sketch" labels in the body + full proofs in the appendix is the right structure; keep it, just make sure every "sketch" name-drops the specific mechanism (you mostly do — the Lemma 4 induction on tree height is a good model).

## Bottom line

Theoretical quality is high and the stochastic-partitioning idea deserves credit — this is a paper I'd be comfortable defending as a solid contribution. Before I'd call it publication-ready in a venue with an applications bar, I want: (a) at least one concrete assume-guarantee case study with the assumption-size numbers, (b) the LRA encodings made explicit, (c) the termination-vs-minimality tradeoff (Thm 5 vs. Thm 6) stated up front as the paper's main open line, and (d) a one-line clarity fix on where reactivity is assumed. The first of these is the one that would most change how the work is received.
