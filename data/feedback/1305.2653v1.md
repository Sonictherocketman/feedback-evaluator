## [1305.2653v1-general-review] General Review


# Review: "Ferromagnetism and Fermi‑surface transition in the periodic Anderson model: Second‑order phase transition without symmetry breaking" (Kubo, 2013)

## The argument in short

The paper extends the Gutzwiller variational method (at U→∞, T=0) for the periodic Anderson model (PAM) to the *magnetically polarized* case, previously treated only paramagnetically. By minimizing the single‑band energy with variational amplitudes a_σ(k), the author identifies three ferromagnetic (FM) regimes distinguished by their Fermi surface (FS): **FM0** (weak FM, large FS), **FM1** (half‑metallic, no majority‑spin FS), and **FM2** (small FS, almost fully polarized f electrons). With a magnetic field, the PM→FM "transition" turns into a crossover (the field breaks the symmetry), but the **boundary between two FM phases can still be a true phase transition**, and the author argues these between‑FM transitions can be *second order* even though no symmetry is broken and no conventional order parameter changes qualitatively — i.e. Lifshitz transitions driven by the FS topology change. Phase diagrams for n = 1.25, 1.55, 1.75 show second‑ vs first‑order lines and a first‑order end point; an appendix gives the derivative argument (M continuous but dM/dx discontinuous ⇒ jump in dF/dx ⇒ first order; both continuous but d²F/dx² jumps ⇒ second order). The model is then loosely mapped onto pressure‑driven FS reconstructions and de Haas–van Alphen anomalies in CeRnSi₂, YbRnSi₂, UGe₂.

## Strengths

- **Clear, internally consistent mean‑field treatment.** The Gutzwiller equations (19)–(24) are worked out carefully, with the n_σ>1 and n_σ<1 branches handled separately and the momentum‑distribution weights derived. Equivalence to the slave‑boson/Kotliar–Ruckenstein MFT is stated explicitly (helpful for placing the results in context).
- **A well‑motivated effective‑mass definition.** Defining m_σ* through the k_F jump in the *momentum distribution* (Eq. 24) rather than the band renormalization factor is the right thing to do once f‑character sits at the FS; recognizing that m* is undefined in the half‑metallic FM1 (no majority FS) is an honest, correct limitation.
- **A genuinely useful unifying picture.** Tying FM0/FM1/FM2 to large‑FS → small‑FS reconstruction and connecting it to the Ce/Yb rare‑earth contrast (δε_f<0 vs >0) and to the UGe₂ FMI/FM2 picture gives the community an organizational framework for "FS reconstruction as a quantum‑critical endpoint."
- The **appendix** is a nice, short, self‑contained statement of why a second‑order transition can occur when the quantity M is continuous, and the reminder that "spontaneous symmetry breaking ⇒ transition" does not reverse is pedagogically valuable.

## Critique and limitations

1. **The "second‑order transition without symmetry breaking" is a known object (Lifshitz, 1960), not a new phenomenon.** The paper's headline is a well‑established class of transition; the author even concedes the refinement (2nd vs the original 2.5 order). The genuine novelty is *applying and cataloguing* this in the magnetic‑field PAM. This is legitimately useful but should not be read as a conceptual discovery — the framing oversells it relative to what is new.

2. **Zero temperature and a flat conduction band dilute the "FS transition" content.** With ρ(ε)=1/(2W) for |ε|<W and no real ε_k dispersion, k_F is essentially a variational parameter delimiting regions of phase space, not a FS emerging from a true dispersion. Consequently the **topology** central to a Lifshitz transition is reproduced only as coarse band‑filling (bold lines in Fig. 1), not as pocket creation/annihilation in genuine k‑space. The "FS reconstruction" is closer to a heavy(fermion)↔light(Kondo‑breakdown) crossover than a geometrically defined FS transition. A reader should weigh the central claim accordingly.

3. **No finite‑temperature or fluctuation analysis.** Everything is a T=0 ground‑energy minimization. The author correctly notes FM↔FM second‑order lines become crossovers at finite T (FS undefined), but the *asserted* divergences—susceptibility diverging on the second‑order line and dn_f/dε_f diverging at the end point (p. 4)—are never actually demonstrated or characterized (no exponent, no correlation‑length scaling, no check that it isn't a mean‑field artifact). At a true continuous endpoint one would expect to say something about critical indices; instead the "mass enhancement" (the m_f*/m peak in Fig. 3c) is presented as a candidate QCP "mechanism" without the critical theory to back it. This is the weakest link in the argument from model to the de Haas–van Alphen / resistivity observations it invokes.

4. **The antiferromagnetism section (V, Fig. 8) is purely schematic.** No AFM calculation is performed; it is an analogy to the Kondo‑lattice results of Refs. 29–35. Yet the conclusion leans on it ("we have shown a second‑order transition is possible without symmetry breaking" is supported by FM, but the *material* story in Sec. V/VII is AF‑driven). The gap between what is computed (FM) and what is interpreted (AFM QCP, Hall‑coefficient sign change, de Haas–van Alphen anomaly) is larger than the paper makes explicit.

5. **No quantitative parameter fitting.** Parameter sets (V/W=0.2, n=1.75, etc.) are chosen to be "representative," not fitted to any one compound. Single orbital, no orbital degrees of freedom, no crystal field, no magnetic anisotropy—though the author flags anisotropy as important. Combined with U→∞, this means the model captures the *type* of physics (FM bands, FS reconstruction) but not numbers. The U→∞ limit is also a real restriction for heavy‑fermion compounds where U is large but finite; the author lists finite U as future work.

6. **Minor.** The effective‑mass denominator m (the total distribution weight) is used loosely; in FM2 the f* mass is said to be small because M→1, but the definition mixes c and f contributions in a way that is arguably the *inverse* of what a heavy‑fermion reader expects, and this is only glossed over. Figures 5 vs 6 overlap heavily (both H‑scans at almost identical parameters) and would benefit from consolidation.

## Bottom line

A competent, transparent mean‑field extension of an established method that delivers a clean taxonomy of FM phases and a reasonable (if loose) bridge to Ce/Yb/Yb heavy‑fermion FS‑reconstruction phenomena. Its main limitations—flat band, T=0, schematic AFM, un‑quantified "critical" divergences, no parameter fitting—are mostly acknowledged, which is to the author's credit, but several of them (especially the topological dilution of the "FS transition" and the unsupported susceptibility/mass divergences) sit directly under the paper's central claim and should be more carefully caveated. The "second‑order transition without symmetry breaking" framing is accurate but incremental rather than new.

# Peer review

**Paper:** *Ferromagnetism and Fermi-surface transition in the periodic Anderson model: Second-order phase transition without symmetry breaking* — K. Kubo (arXiv:1305.2653, cond-mat.str-el)

## What the paper does

It applies the spin-polarized Gutzwiller variational approximation to the periodic Anderson model (PAM) in the U→∞ limit, with a flat (box) conduction-band DOS, and maps out three ferromagnetic (FM) phases at zero temperature:

- **FM0** — weakly polarized, $n_\uparrow<1$, both spins cross the Fermi level,
- **FM1** — half-metallic, $n_\uparrow=1$, no majority-spin Fermi surface (hybridization gap present),
- **FM2** — $n_\uparrow>1$, the lower hybridized band fully filled by up-spin electrons, the $f$ orbitals almost fully polarized ($n_{f\uparrow}\simeq1$, $n_{f\downarrow}\simeq0$), and a *small* Fermi surface.

The headline result is that the boundaries between these phases can be **second-order (Lifshitz) transitions even though no symmetry is broken**, characterized by a change in Fermi-surface (FS) topology rather than an order parameter. An auxiliary but genuinely clear result is in the Appendix: minimizing $F(x,M)$ and using the chain rule, a jump in $d^2F/dx^2$ follows whenever $M(x)$ is continuous but $dM/dx$ is discontinuous — so a second-order transition does not generically require symmetry breaking.

## What I think is good

1. **The method is the right tool, and the author uses it well.** The distinction he draws from the Kotliar–Ruckenstein slave-boson mean-field theory is fair and important: the slave-boson (one-particle) approximation returns a step-function momentum distribution, so $\Delta n_c(k_F)+\Delta n_f(k_F)=1$, whereas the Gutzwiller constraint gives the quasiparticle-weight renormalization in Eqs. (8)–(15). That renormalization is precisely what lets him define a physically meaningful effective mass in the magnetically ordered state and track the FS reconstruction. Defining $m^*_\sigma$ via the quasiparticle jump, Eq. (24), rather than the naive inverse $f$-electron weight, is the correct call.

2. **The Appendix is a nice, correct, and under-appreciated point.** It is a clean proof that Landau's symmetry-breaking picture is not a logical closure. I would have expected it only as pedagogy, but it is directly on point.

3. **The phase-diagram physics is physically sensible.** Showing that the FM1–FM2 transition changes from second order at $n=1.25$ to first order at $n=1.75$ with a critical endpoint in between — tied to the jump in $M$ being larger for larger $n$ — is a reasonable and well-argued mechanism (the jump in $M$ from $\simeq2-n$ in FM1 to $\simeq1$ in FM2). The argument that $d n_f/d\epsilon_f$ diverges at the end point is a good observation.

4. **Honest about the heavy-fermion connection.** He explicitly concedes (Sec. IV.C) that the chosen field-driven parameter set has a *small* effective mass, and (Summary) that a large-mass paramagnetic state could not be obtained because magnetic order preempts the mass divergence. This is the paper's honest core, and it should have shaped the claims more strongly — see below.

## What the peer should push back on

1. **The U→∞ limit hollows out the very phenomenon being used to motivate the paper.** The motivation is heavy-fermion metamagnetism (CeRu₂Si₂, YbRh₂Si₂, UGe₂), where the effective masses are $m^*/m\sim10$–$100$. Because he takes $U\to\infty$, the *maximum* effective mass he ever reaches is of order 2 (Fig. 3c), and the paramagnetic Kondo (heavy) ground state is unstable against FM order before $m^*$ diverges. So the "we understand heavy-fermion FS transitions" framing is substantially overstated: the theory reproduces the *qualitative* shape of $M(\epsilon_f)$ and $M(H)$, not the mass scale. The comparison in Sec. IV.B ("overall behaviours ... similar to those ... in UGe₂") should be read as suggestive, not quantitative, and the paper should say so more prominently. This is the single most important limitation and it is currently buried.

2. **The "second-order without symmetry breaking" claim is more a restatement than a new finding.** Lifshitz-type FS reconstruction is of course a continuous transition, and the broader Kondo-lattice / Fermi-liquid-quantum-critical-point literature (which he cites — Watanabe–Ogata, Lanatà–Fabrizio, Martin–Bercx–Assaad) already treats FS-reconstruction instabilities between same-symmetry phases. The genuinely new content is modest: (a) doing it in the constrained/Gutzwiller PAM and (b) the clean Appendix argument. Framing it as the "converse" of the symmetry principle ("the symmetry is broken ⇒ transition; the converse is also true ... as in the liquid–vapor transition of water, which is *first* order") is a little loose — liquid–vapor is first order, so it is not a parallel to a *second*-order no-symmetry-breaking transition. I would trim the rhetoric. Also, the claim that these field-driven FM transitions "are possible even for $U=0$" is asserted without being established as a general statement for all $U$; at $U=0$ the model is a band problem and the FM1 half-metal is hard to stabilize — a short argument or caveat would strengthen this.

3. **Flat DOS is an assumption, not a controlled approximation.** Stating "a change in the form of ρ(ε) will affect the results little unless ρ(ε) has strong peaks" is asserted. Near a Lifshitz point, where a band edge / FS is exactly forming, the DOS shape *near the relevant band edge* does matter for the order (2 vs 2.5) and for the exponents. A single numerical check with a different band (e.g., a parabolic or cosine dispersion) would have substantially bolstered this.

4. **No finite temperature.** Quantum-critical-point physics (the YbRh₂Si₂ AFM QCP, the UGe₂ pressure dome) lives at finite but low $T$, and the 2.5-order-vs-2-order Lifshitz question has a genuinely different thermal story. The paper is entirely $T=0$; the one paragraph acknowledging this (Sec. IV.D) is insufficient. The connection to the de Haas–van Alphen QCP data in Sec. V is therefore speculative.

5. **Numerics/self-consistency details are thin.** The variational equations (20)–(22) are nonlinear integral equations solved for $\tilde\epsilon_{f\sigma}$ and $n_{f\sigma}$ at fixed total polarization, then minimized over $M'$. The author does not discuss (i) uniqueness of the self-consistent solution, or (ii) whether metastable/coexisting branches were checked to locate the first-order line and the end point, or (iii) the accuracy of the energy differences that distinguish second from first order in Fig. 7 (panels b–c are quoted to the $10^{-4}$–$10^{-5}W$ level — a polynomial fit at that precision warrants an error estimate). For a first-order vs. second-order determination this matters.

6. **No transport.** He himself notes the $A$ coefficient of the $T^2$ resistivity must be computed to compare with experiment but it is absent. Without it the heavy-fermion comparison stays at the level of $M$-curves.

7. Minor: $g_c=0$ (no conduction-band Zeeman term) is stated to be justified by $M_c$ being small — fine and self-consistent, but worth a one-line quantitative bound on the fields over which it holds. The prose has several small grammar issues ("Another examples", "the strongly polarized ferromagnetic phase phase"), none of which affect the physics.

## Bottom line

This is a **competent, technically sound, and physically thoughtful** mean-field study that correctly extends a standard variational method into a new regime (spin-polarized PAM) and extracts a clean, correctly argued result about continuous same-symmetry transitions. Its main weaknesses are (a) the $U\to\in$ limit, which removes the large-mass heavy-fermion state that is the paper's raison d'être and caps the achievable mass enhancement at $\sim2$, (b) a somewhat over-framed novelty claim for the "no-symmetry-breaking second-order transition," and (c) the qualitative-only, $T=0$, flat-band connection to the experiments that motivate the work. It is a solid contribution that fits coherently within the 2010s heavy-fermion FS-reconstruction literature, but it is an extension rather than a conceptual advance, and the experimental claims exceed what the U→∞ model actually delivers.

I would recommend **acceptance after minor-to-moderate revision**: (1) substantially downplay the quantitative heavy-fermion comparison and foreground the U→∞ limitation; (2) add a short caveat/argument for the $U=0$ field-driven claim; (3) add an error estimate to the Fig. 7 energy differences; (4) soften the "converse of the symmetry principle" rhetoric.

## Paper: Kubo, "Ferromagnetism and Fermi-surface transition in the periodic Anderson model: Second-order phase transition without symmetry breaking" (JPSJ, 2013)

### Summary of content
The paper applies the Gutzwiller method in the $U\to\infty$ limit to a single-orbital periodic Anderson model (flat conduction band) and maps four phases — paramagnetic (large FS), weak ferromagnet (FM0), half-metallic (FM1), and fully polarized (FM2) — as a function of $V/W$ and the $f$-level $\epsilon_f$ (and magnetic field $H$). The central claim is that the FM–FM transitions are **second-order transitions without any symmetry breaking**, i.e. Lifshitz-type transitions governed by a change in Fermi-surface topology, and that the accompanying magnetization change is continuous (second order) rather than the $2\frac12$ order of the original Lifshitz transition.

### Strengths
- **Coherent and well-organized.** The schematic band picture (Fig. 1) tied to the variational parameters, and the clean separation into the PM/FM0/FM1/FM2 sequence, makes the physical argument easy to follow.
- **Genuinely useful conceptual clarification.** Arguing that transitions *between* ferromagnetic states need not be first order — because there is no symmetry change and only the Fermi-surface topology changes — is a correct and important point, and the appendix gives a defensible argument for the sufficient condition (continuous $M(x)$ with a discontinuous $dM/dx$).
- **The effective-mass peak at FM1** (diverging as the up-spin FS vanishes) is a natural emergent consequence of the method and a nice, physically transparent result.
- **The equivalence argument** between the Gutzwiller quantities and the $f$-slave boson mean field, plus the explicit contrast to its failures (one-particle approximation, inability to capture $\Delta n$), is honest about scope.

### Weaknesses
- **Extreme simplifications limit quantitative relevance.** A perfectly flat conduction band ($\rho(\epsilon)=0$ outside $|\epsilon|<W$), a single orbital, $U\to\infty$, and the omission of the Zeeman term for conduction electrons all confine the study to a narrow, largely one-parameter regime. The paper itself concedes (Sec. VI) that a paramagnetic large-mass state is never obtained because a magnetic state always wins — so the model does not faithfully reproduce the actual ground-state competition in Ce/Yb compounds. Most of the "comparison" to UGe₂, YbRh₂Si₂, CeRu₂Si₂ is qualitative analogy, not quantitative.
- **Mean-field only, no fluctuations.** All the second-order character and the Lifshitz classification rest on a static, one-particle self-consistency. In reality Fermi-surface (Lifshitz) transitions are strongly affected by fluctuations and are typically continuous/crossover at finite $T$; the paper itself notes the second-order nature is a $T=0$ artifact and that first-order transitions can occur via $T^3$ corrections. The central "second order" claim is thus tied to the approximation and the $T=0$ limit.
- **The "topological" claim is somewhat overstated.** This is a momentum-space model with no real-space lattice or orbital degrees of freedom, so "Fermi-surface topology change" amounts to a change in the Fermi *momentum* (a level crossing of $\epsilon_{k_F}$ across zero) rather than a true topological change. Comparing it to the "originally proposed Lifshitz transition" and to the Kondo-lattice AFM results requires more care than the paper gives.
- **Unresolved physics left as future work.** The paper explicitly does not compute the Hall effect, the de Haas–van Alphen signal, the $T^2$ resistivity coefficient, the finite-$U$ case, or the multiorbital case — all of which are the quantities an experiment actually measures. This weakens the experimental impact.
- **A few minor issues:** the magnetic field dependence $M(H)$ in Figs. 5–6 being called "anomaly" in YbRh₂Si₂ is asserted rather than demonstrated; the $a_{c_f}, a_W$ pressure parameters are introduced with a degree of freedom (the $a c_f$ term) with no microscopic justification.

### Bottom line
A clean, internally consistent mean-field study that makes one genuinely valuable conceptual point (FS/Lifshitz transitions between ferromagnets without symmetry breaking). It is limited by the U→∞/flat-band/single-orbital approximations, the absence of fluctuations, and only qualitative connections to real materials. Solid but incremental within the Anderson-model literature; the result would need finite-$U$, multiorbital, and finite-$T$ treatments to be quantitatively convincing against experiment.

2. Using the energy per site (Fig. 7) and the free‑energy argument in the Appendix, the author argues FM2→FM1 is first order, FM1→FM0 is *second order* (not a crossover), and — pointedly — that the Lifshitz transition in this model is genuinely **second order rather than the 2.5 order** of the original Lifshitz proposal.

## Is it novel given the work the authors cite?

**What is genuinely new here:**

- **The order‑of‑the‑transition result.** The paper's real contribution is not the phases but showing, with an explicit energy analysis plus a general free‑energy proof (Appendix A), that a second‑order transition without symmetry breaking occurs in this model, and reframing the accompanying Lifshitz transition as second order. This "second order without broken symmetry" is the piece the paper builds its title on and it is not present in the cited mean‑field work, which had obtained the phases but not characterized the order of the boundaries.

## Net judgment

A competent, self‑consistent mean‑field study with a clear heavy‑fermion motivation (CeR₂Si₂, YbRh₂Si₂, UGe₂) and a correct exact‑U treatment. Its novel kernel is narrow and conceptual — the characterization of the FM boundaries as continuous/Lifshitz and the "second order without symmetry breaking" claim — wrapped around a phase diagram whose ingredients were already in the slave‑boson/Kondo‑lattice literature. The method is an incremental extension, the phases are known, and the main new statement (order of transition, 2nd‑order Lifshitz) is a nice but limited insight rather than a field‑moving result.

# Evaluation of `1305.2653v1.pdf`

**Paper:** K. Kubo, *"Ferromagnetism and Fermi-surface transition in the periodic Anderson model: Second-order phase transition without symmetry breaking"* (J. Phys. Soc. Jpn. 82, 063706, 2013; arXiv:1305.2653).

This is a **pure theory** paper. There is no wet-lab "experiment" to reproduce; the "central experiment" is a **numerical/analytical calculation** — sweeping control parameters of the periodic Anderson model (PAM) at T = 0 with the Gutzwiller variational method, locating the ferromagnetic phase boundaries, and reading off their thermodynamic order from the energy derivatives. Below I summarize the method at a reproduction level, then list every assumption the methodology quietly relies on or glosses over.

---

## 1. Methodology in reproduction detail

### 1.1 The model (Eq. 1)
Single-site periodic Anderson model:

$$\mathcal{H}=\sum_{k\sigma}\epsilon_k c_{k\sigma}^\dagger c_{k\sigma}+\sum_{i\sigma}\epsilon_f n_{i\sigma}-V\sum_{k\sigma}\left(f_{k\sigma}^\dagger c_{k\sigma}+c_{k\sigma}^\dagger f_{k\sigma}\right)+U\sum_i n_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow}$$

with the following **fixed simplifications** that must be set before running anything:

- **Infinite U**: $U\to\infty$ (f-orbital is narrow, so the Coulomb term is taken to its strongest form). This is why the Gutzwiller projector $P=\prod_{i\sigma}(1-n_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow})$ removes exact double occupancy.
- **Flat conduction-band density of states**: $\rho(\epsilon)=1/(2W)$ for $|\epsilon|<W$ and $0$ otherwise. This is the single most important modelling choice — the conduction band is a structureless rectangular band of half-width $W$, which normalizes all energies in the plots as $\epsilon/V$ or $\epsilon/W$.
- **Zeeman convention**: under field $H$, $\epsilon_f\to\epsilon_f-\sigma H$ with $\sigma=\pm1$; Bohr magneton $m_B=1$; **$g_f=2$** for f-electrons and **$g_c=0$** for conduction electrons (their Zeeman term is dropped); **magnetic anisotropy dropped** (magnetization assumed along the easy axis).
- **Energy zero**: $\sum_k\epsilon_k=0$ (conduction-electron energy taken as the origin).
- All results at **$T=0$**.
- **Single f-orbital per site, spatially uniform state** (no AFM, no orbital degrees of freedom, no inhomogeneity).

### 1.2 The variational wave function (Eqs. 2–4)
Gutzwiller-projected product of c/f hybridized orbitals:
$$|\psi\rangle=P\,|\varphi_\sigma\rangle\otimes|\varphi_f\rangle,$$
with spin/momentum-dependent variational amplitudes $a_\sigma(\mathbf k)$ and a Fermi momentum $k_{F\sigma}$ per spin. For $n_\sigma<1$ (Eq. 3) only states below $k_{F\sigma}$ are hybrid-filled; for $n_\sigma>1$ (Eq. 4) the lower band is filled plus the upper band is filled above $k_{F\sigma}$.

### 1.3 The self-consistent core (the thing to actually code)
For a fixed total electron number $n=n_\uparrow+n_\downarrow$ and a *trial* polarization $M'=n_\uparrow-n_\downarrow$ (so $n_\sigma=(n\pm M')/2$), solve these **coupled nonlinear equations** for the renormalized f-level $\tilde\epsilon_{f\sigma}$ and the f-number $n_{f\sigma}$:

- Variational amplitude (from $\partial e/\partial a_\sigma=0$, Eq. 19):
$$a_\sigma(\mathbf k)=\frac{2V}{\epsilon_f-\epsilon_k+\sqrt{(\epsilon_f-\epsilon_k)^2+4\tilde V_\sigma^2}},\qquad \tilde V_\sigma=\sqrt U\,\tilde V_\sigma .$$
- Renormalized f-level (Eq. 20): $\epsilon_f-\tilde\epsilon_{f\sigma}=-\sum_{\sigma'}\tilde V_\sigma^2 J_{2\sigma\sigma'}\,\partial q_\sigma^{-1}/\partial n_{f\sigma}$, with the kernel $J_{\sigma\sigma'}$ given by Eq. 21 (a one-loop integral over the conduction DOS).
- f-electron number (Eq. 22): $n_{f\sigma}=(n_\sigma+I_\sigma)/2$, where $I_\sigma$ is the same kind of one-loop integral.
- The Gutzwiller renormalization factors $q_\sigma=(1-n_f)/(1-n_{f\sigma})$ (Eq. 7) tie the projected to the unprojected counts.

These have to be solved **iteratively/numerically** (they are implicit, not closed form) for each $M'$.

### 1.4 Energy minimization and phase selection
For each $M'$, compute the **energy per site** $e=M'/L$… actually $e=\langle\mathcal H\rangle/L=\sum_\sigma e_\sigma$ (Eqs. 16–18). Then:
- Vary the Fermi momenta $k_{F\sigma}$ (equivalently vary $M'$ holding $n$ fixed) and **pick the $M'$ that gives the lowest energy**. That self-consistent minimization over polarization is what produces the equilibrium magnetization and the phase boundaries.

### 1.5 Quantities to evaluate
- f- and c-electron counts (Eqs. 5–6) and momentum-distribution functions with the characteristic jumps $\Delta n_{c\sigma},\Delta n_{f\sigma}$ (Eqs. 8–15).
- **Magnetization** $M=M_f=n_{f\uparrow}-n_{f\downarrow}$ and conduction-band polarization $M_c=n_{c\uparrow}-n_{c\downarrow}$.
- **Effective mass** (Eq. 24): a *newly defined* spin-dependent $m_\sigma^*/m=1/[\Delta n_{c\sigma}(k_F)+\Delta n_{f\sigma}(k_F)]$, i.e. from the jump in the momentum distribution at $k_F$ (NOT the standard hybridized-band curvature).
- Kinetic energy of the conduction electron at $k_F$.

### 1.6 Identifying the transition order (the "central experiment")
The headline computation is **Fig. 7**: at $n=1.75$, $V/W=0.2$, no field, sweep $\epsilon_f/W$ and plot the ground-state energy per site $e(\epsilon_f)$ in and around each boundary. Read the order from its two derivatives:
- a **jump in $de/d\epsilon_f$** → first order (this happens at the **FM2↔FM1** boundary);
- a **continuous slope but a jump in $d^2e/d\epsilon_f^2$** → second order (this happens at **FM1↔FM0** and **FM0↔PM**).

This is the demonstration that FM↔FM transitions, which break no symmetry, are still true second-order transitions at T=0 and are identified as **Lifshitz transitions** (Fermi-surface topology: large FS ↔ small/half-metalllic FS, with the accompanying magnetism of second order rather than Lifshitz's original 2.5 order).

### 1.7 The concrete parameter recipes to reproduce the main figures
- **Reproduce Fig. 3** (the ε₍f₎-sweep that shows all three phases at once): $n=1.75$, $V/W=0.2$, $T=0$, sweep $\epsilon_f/W$; record $M(M_f,M_c)$, $\tilde\epsilon_{f\uparrow}-\tilde\epsilon_{f\downarrow}$, and $m^*/m$.
- **Reproduce Figs. 5–6** (magnetic-field effect): fix $n=1.75$, $V/W=0.2$, $\epsilon_f/W=0.3$ (paramagnetic at $H=0$); sweep $H/W$; expect PM→FM1→FM2 with the kinks at the FS transitions.
- **Reproduce Fig. 2** (phase diagrams): run the above minimization on the $(\epsilon_f/W,\;V/W)$ plane for $n=1.25,1.55,1.75$; observe that FM1→FM2 is second order at $n=1.25$ but first order at $n=1.75$.
- **Pressure** (Eq. 23): model $\epsilon_f=\epsilon_f^{(0)}+a_{\epsilon f}p$ and $W=W^{(0)}+a_W p$ *linear in p*, and — by assumption — hold $V/W$ roughly fixed, then move along the corresponding line in the phase diagram.

A reader with this (flat DOS, $U=\infty$, $g_c=0$, the $a_\sigma/\tilde\epsilon/n_f$ self-consistent solve, and the energy-minim-over-$M'$ rule) can reproduce the phase diagram, the three ferromagnetic phases, and the transition-order result, **within those approximations**.

## 2. Assumptions the methodology papers over

Below are the assumptions it either takes for granted or flags only briefly as "not included for now." Several are load-bearing.

1. **$U\to\infty$ (infinite on-site Coulomb).** The real heavy-fermion Kondo/Joule physics, the finite Kondo temperature, and the *finite* renormalization (and hence the experimentally extracted A-coefficient / effective mass) all require *finite* $U$. The paper itself concedes the $U=\infty$ description must be "revised quantitatively" (e.g. finite $U$, orbital degrees) and notes that in the paramagnetic state $U=\infty$ makes the effective mass explode before the magnetic state sets in — i.e. the *relative stability* of PM vs FM in PM may be an artefact of the limit.
2. **Flat, structureless conduction DOS** $\rho(\epsilon)=1/(2W)\,\Theta(W-|\epsilon|)$. Any real van-Hove singularity, band-edge, or asymmetric DOS can qualitatively change the FS and transition order; the authors merely state "we expect a change in $\rho(\epsilon)$ will affect the results little unless $\rho(\epsilon)$ has some characteristic structures, such as strong peaks." They do not test this.
3. **$g_c=0$ (no Zeeman term on conduction electrons).** Justified only "as long as the magnetic field is not very large," yet Figs. 5–6 go up to $H/W$ where f-polarization approaches saturation — a regime the paper acknowledges it has not checked.
4. **Spatially uniform state only.** The variational ansatz is homogeneous; any striped, inhomogeneous, or spatially modulated competing phase is excluded. Antiferromagnetism (Sec. V) is treated *only schematically* with drawn band structures — never calculated — so the entire AFM/Lifshitz discussion for the A-sublattice/B-sublattice picture is unverified.
5. **Single f-orbital / no orbital degeneracy.** The PAM here is one orbital + spin per site; real Ce/Yb have $4f_{J=5/2}$ multiplet structure, crystal-field splitting, and spin–orbit effects (relevant to the Hall/thermodynamic anomalies they cite) all dropped. The authors admit "by introducing [orbital] degrees of freedom … is an important future problem."
6. **Magnetic anisotropy dropped.** Explicitly "important in f-electron systems" but ignored; the magnetization is assumed pinned to the easy axis, which matters for the field-sweep results.
7. **Mean-field / Gutzwiller variational class only.** All spin, charge, and spin–orbital *dynamical* fluctuations are absent. For $n_\sigma<1$ the electron distribution in the Gutzwiller state is essentially exact-Fermi (renormalization enters only through $\Delta n$), a known intrinsic limitation of the ansatz that the paper notes in passing but does not quantify. The claim of a clean *second-order* (not merely continuous crossover) FM↔FM transition rests entirely on this variational/mean-field description being able to sustain true singularities.
8. **Ad hoc redefinition of effective mass.** Using $m_\sigma^*$ from the $k_F$ jump in Eqs. (8)–(10)/(12)–(15) rather than hybridized-band curvature is a deliberate, somewhat arbitrary choice; the paper itself says "we should better define the effective mass by the renormalization of the hybridized band," and concedes it cannot extract the A-coefficient to compare with experiment. All the "effective-mass peaks/dips" conclusions hinge on this chosen definition.
9. **The "second-order-without-symmetry-breaking" criterion itself (appendix, Eqs. A.1–A.3).** The order is *defined* by which derivative of $F(x,M)$ jumps, assuming $M(x)$ is smooth enough and that $dF/dM=0$ fixes $M(x)$. This quietly assumes the free-energy surface is analytic to the required order and that the correct "control parameter" $x$ and "order-like" $M$ have been chosen; it does not establish a genuine universal critical class and papers over the fact that because no symmetry is broken there is no standard Landau order parameter to validate the classification against.
10. **The transition is a "Lifshitz" one, but the Lifshitz *order* is changed by hand.** The paper asserts its FM↔FM Lifshitz transition is second order, in contrast to Lifshitz's (ref. 35) 2.5-order. That reframing is argued but not independently verified (e.g. no scaling, no correlation-length measurement), so the "2.5 → 2" claim is asserted within the model's own analytic structure.
11. **Linear pressure model with $V/W$ held fixed.** $\epsilon_f(p)$ and $W(p)$ are assumed linear in pressure and $V/W$ is assumed to vary slowly enough to treat it as constant. The "pressure moves the system along a known line" reasoning (Sec. IV.B) then depends on this; the authors themselves note it is "not obvious whether the effect of pressure is opposite to between Ce and Yb compounds" and that the sign of $\tilde a_\epsilon$ is assumed, not measured.
12. **Thermodynamic limit / k-summations treated as integrals** ($1/L\sum_k\to\int$) with the flat DOS, which silently assumes an isotropic, structureless Brillouin-zone integration and no lattice geometry.
13. **$T=0$ everywhere.** The central "second-order" transitions and the Lifshitz character are T=0 statements; the paper notes without calculating that at finite $T$ the FM↔FM boundaries would become *crossovers* (Fermi surface ill-defined), so the very object being classified is not robust under the one perturbation (temperature) that is always present experimentally.
14. **Schematic band arguments assumed, not derived.** The Fig. 1/Fig. 8 "a1/a2/b" band-structure pictures are asserted to correspond to the weakly/fully polarized states; their topology (and hence the identification with a FM↔AFM phase transition) is by construction, not solved.

## 3. Verdict on soundness-within-assumptions

Internally, the treatment is consistent and honest: the self-consistent Gutzwiller equations (19)–(22) are closed and the energy-minimization over $M'$ is a correct way to locate the phases, the order classification via $e'(\epsilon_f)$ vs $e''(\epsilon_f)$ (Fig. 7) is a legitimate T=0 argument, and the author is unusually candid about the main limitations (infinite U, flat DOS, uniform states, the effective-mass definition, and the "future work" items). The conceptual hook — that symmetry-unbroken FM↔FM boundaries are nonetheless true second-order (Lifshitz) transitions whose order can flip with electron count — is a clean and genuinely useful reframing for interpreting de Haas–van Alphen anomalies in CeRh₂Si₂, YbRh₂Si₂, and UCe₂. The main caveats are that the result lives entirely inside the $U=\infty$ + flat-DOS + uniform-state corner of parameter space, and that the "second-order / 2.5-order" claim is argued but not independently validated by scaling.

## Restatement of the paper's logical argument

**Goal.** Use the periodic Anderson model (PAM) in $U\to\infty$ limit, solved by the Gutzwiller (LDA-like) variation for $\sigma$-dependent hybridization amplitudes, to describe ferromagnetic transitions in heavy-fermion compounds (Ce/Yb $R\text{h}_2\text{Si}_2$) where de Haas–van Alphen Fermi-surface data suggest the electronic state changes drastically at a magnetization transition *without* any obvious symmetry-breaking order parameter.

**Chain of reasoning:**

1. **Model setup (Secs II–III).** PAM Hamiltonian; the variational wave function $|\psi\rangle = P|\phi\rangle\otimes|\phi_f\rangle$ with Gutzwiller projection removing double occupancy. The variational parameters $a_\sigma(\mathbf{k})$ are spin-dependent. Solving $\partial e/\partial a=0$ yields Eqs. (19)–(23). The key output objects are spin-dependent, spin-momentum-dependent occupation functions $n_{c\sigma}(\mathbf{k}), n_{f\sigma}(\mathbf{k})$, each of which has a *jump discontinuity* at a spin-dependent Fermi momentum $k_{F\sigma}$, with $\Delta n$ given by Eqs. (9)–(15). This jump is *not* part of one-particle slave-boson MFT but is a genuine Gutzwiller (renormalization) effect — the paper explicitly contrasts the two to establish its internal consistency (a point worth commending).

2. **Phase classification by Fermi-surface topology (Sec IV).** Four states emerge:
   - **PM:** large FS, both spins, $\tilde\epsilon_f\approx\epsilon_f$.
   - **FM0 (weak FM):** FS changes but spin-up spin still has a FS; $\Delta n_{c\uparrow}\neq 0$.
   - **FM1 (half-metallic):** up-spin band completely filled, no up-spin FS; $n_{f\uparrow}=1$.
   - **FM2 (fully polarized):** up-spin band completely empty, $n_{f\uparrow}\approx0$; small FS mostly of conduction character.
   Each is characterized only by the change in $n_{c\sigma}(k_{F\sigma})$ across $k_{F\sigma}$ — i.e., by **topology of the FS**, not by any Landau symmetry-breaking order parameter.

3. **Phase diagrams (Fig. 2).** For $n=1.25,1.55$: FM–FM boundaries are second order (solid lines), with a first-order segment coexisting. For $n=1.75$: the FM1–FM2 boundary is *entirely* first order. Explanation: magnetization jump $\Delta M$ across FMI→FM2 grows with $n$, so the transition must be first-order for $n=1.75$ (an analog of first-order valence transitions in the correlated PAM, ref. 24). On the second-order line, $\chi\to\infty$ and $dn_f/d\epsilon_f\to\infty$, which the paper flags as a possible seed for unconventional superconductivity.

4. **Order of the FM–FM transition without symmetry breaking (Sec IV.D + Appendix).** The core conceptual claim: transitions between two ferromagnetic states (which share the same broken $SU(2)$ symmetry) are usually *crossovers*, not phase transitions, at $T=0$, because there is no distinguishable order parameter. The paper shows that here the transitions **can** be genuine phase transitions because the FS topologies differ; the energy $e(\epsilon_f)$ is continuous, but its first derivative jumps (FM2–FM1) or second derivative jumps (FM1–FM0, FM0–PM), as shown by zoomed figures Fig. 7. The Appendix formalizes this as a **sufficient condition** for a second-order transition: continuity of $M(x)$ plus a jump in $dM/dx$ is a second-order transition, *without assuming* $M(x)=0$ away from the transition. The paper explicitly notes this generalizes a "Lifshitz transition" in the sense that the originally proposed Lifshitz transition has order $2.5$, whereas the present one is second order because magnetization itself is continuous across the boundary.

5. **Magnetic-field dependence (Fig. 5–6).** In a chosen PM region at $n=1.75$, $V/W=0.2$, $\epsilon_f/W=0.3$, $H$ drives PM→FM1→FM2. All three boundaries are second order *even with an external field* at $U=\infty$; the paper notes finite-field second-order FM transitions are possible even for $U=0$, in contrast to the generic finite-field first-order picture. At finite $T$ some become crossovers because the FS is not well defined.

6. **Antiferromagnetic extension (Sec V, brief).** In a two-sublattice AF structure, weak AF corresponds to band-structure (a), strong AF corresponds to (b) — a topologically distinct FS — so the AF transition from PM to AF(a) is a phase transition. Strong AF does *not* change FS topology further, so (a)→(b) is not a phase transition in this picture. The paper argues this parallels Kondo-lattice results and the Ce/Yb$Rh_2$Si$_2$ Hall-coefficient QCP, and offers a possible explanation for mass enhancement around the AF transition.

7. **Caveat on large-$m$ state (Sec IV C, paragraph 2).** The author concedes that a much deeper $f$-level would give a large-effective-mass *paramagnetic* state, but that at $U=\infty$ it is destabilized against magnetism, so the simple model may need finite $U$ or orbital degrees of freedom to be fully realistic.

- **Internal consistency check is well done.** The paper explicitly says Gutzwiller's $\Delta n_{c\sigma}(k_{F\sigma})$ and $\Delta n_{f\sigma}(k_{F\sigma})$ are not one-particle and are not captured by one-particle slave-boson MFT; this is exactly the right thing to emphasize, because a careless reader would otherwise think the FS structure is an artifact of the slave-boson approximation.
- **The Appendix adds a genuinely useful conceptual clarification.** The "sufficient condition for second-order phase transition" (Eq. A.1–A.3) is not a new theorem — it is essentially a rederivation of the standard Ehrenfest/Landau criterion using $M(x)$ as the variable — but the explicit statement "we do not require $M$ to be zero away from the transition, nor that $M$ is the order parameter" is the correct way to frame the problem and it directly addresses the reader's likely confusion about whether FM0→FM1 is "really" a transition.
- **Numerical consistency.** The energy-scaling argument in Fig. 7 (linear in (b, c) difference → first order; quadratic in (a) difference → second order) is the cleanest possible diagnostic; I find the FM1–FM0 quadratic and FM2–FM1 linear scalings convincing.
- **Tie to experiment is specific and testable.** The claim $\alpha\tilde\epsilon_f>0$ for Ce and $\alpha\tilde\epsilon_f<0$ for Yb (so magnetic order is destabilized in Ce, stabilized in Yb, under pressure) is a concrete, falsifiable prediction, not a generic "the model should resemble experiment" statement.
- **Antiferromagnetic application is modest but honest.** It correctly identifies that PM→(a1)/(a2) is topologically distinct and (a1)→(b) is not, and that the strong-AF FS is conduction-electron dominated — this is consistent with the QCP discussion in $YbRh_2Si_2$ without over-claiming.

## Feedback (weaknesses / concerns)

- **One-band square-well conduction band is a substantial assumption.** The paper states explicitly that the results are robust to "characteristic structures, such as strong peaks" in $\rho(\epsilon)$, but does not test even a modestly structured band. The existence of the FM1 half-metallic state, in particular, depends very strongly on the density of states near the Fermi level; a more realistic Ce-like $5d/6s$ band with a $5f$-like van Hove singularity in the up-spin channel could plausibly destabilize FM1 or shift the FM1–FM2 boundary entirely. The robustness claim should be tested, not asserted.
- **$U\to\infty$ limit is a double-edged sword.** The model is internally consistent at $U\to\infty$, but several of the most interesting physical points (mass enhancement, possible superconductivity near the second-order line, the role of the paramagnetic large-$m$ state) all live at *finite* $U$ or involve fluctuations the Gutzwiller variation does not see. The paper flags this in Sec IV.C ("further efforts are necessary") and in the summary ("by using a finite value of $U$ and/or by introducing the orbital degrees of freedom"), but the conclusions should be read as a *limiting case* of a more general model. The FM1 half-metallic state — the most striking prediction — is the one most at risk under this limitation, because a finite $U$ hybridization renormalization could shift the up-spin band edge above the Fermi level and restore the up-spin FS.

The order-2.5 statement is unusual and is not clearly defined in the paper; a reader who has only seen the standard Lifshitz picture (a quadratic band bottoming out at $k=0$ with $\Delta\epsilon\propto\lambda^2$, giving $\langle|n|\rangle\sim\lambda^{2/3}$ and a non-analytic $F\sim\lambda^{5/2}$) might disagree whether the present jump in $\partial^2 e/\partial\epsilon_f^2$ at a second-order point with *no change in $\partial e/\partial\epsilon_f$* is Lifshitz at all — it could equally be called a "quantum critical point without symmetry breaking" in the Hertz–Millisense. A small paragraph distinguishing these would strengthen the claim.
- **Spin-1/2, spin-independent dispersion, no anisotropy.** The paper concedes anisotropy is important in real $f$-electron systems and is dropped. For Yb$Rh_2Si_2$, where the experimental anisotropy is significant, this is a meaningful simplification — but the conclusions about the *order* of the FM–FM transitions should not depend on it, so the limitation is at the level of quantitative comparison.
- **Effective mass definition.** The author defines $m_\sigma^*$ via the jump at $k_{F\sigma}$ (Eq. 24), which is the right Gutzwiller-consistent definition, but in the FM1 half-metallic state the up-spin jump is zero, so *no* effective mass is defined for that spin. The paper simply says "we cannot define the effective mass for the up-spin state in FM1," which is correct but means the commonly quoted "heavy-fermion mass" story breaks down exactly at the transition of most interest. A more careful treatment would discuss what the *band-structure* effective mass $m^*_{\uparrow}/m$ (a separate quantity, defined from the dispersion rather than the jump) does in that phase.
- **No finite-temperature scaling.** The paper says the second-order transitions become crossovers at $T>0$ but does not address the dynamic critical behavior (or the absence of it) that a real Fermi-liquid experiment would see. This is fair for a single-band mean-field treatment, but it limits the direct comparison to dHvA and Hall data taken at finite $T$.
- **Minor: Eq. (17) vs (18) bookkeeping.** The two forms for $e_\sigma$ (for $n_\sigma<1$ and $n_\sigma>1$) are stated separately but they look like they should collapse into a single expression with $q_\sigma$ carrying the sign. Presenting them separately is correct given that the band filling condition is piecewise, but a short remark on why the form is different would help.

## Overall assessment

The paper makes a clear, internally-consistent argument: *in a correlated heavy-fermion model, the topology of the Fermi surface, not a Landau symmetry-breaking order parameter, can carry an order-$1$ or order-$2$ phase transition between magnetically-ordered states, and this naturally explains the experimentally observed dramatic change in the effective mass and the Fermi surface at the magnetic transition field in Ce/Yb$Rh_2Si_2$.* The Gutzwiller framework is used correctly, the order-of-the-transition argument is well supported by the explicit energy-scaling figure, and the Appendix adds a useful framing. The main weaknesses are the untested band-shape assumption, the $U\to\infty$ limit, and the slight over-precise "Lifshitz" identification. The work is a solid single-author theoretical paper aimed at a specific experimental question, with a genuine conceptual point (second-order FM–FM transitions without symmetry breaking, formalized in the Appendix) and concrete falsifiable predictions. It is not a field-shaping paper, but it is a competent and useful contribution to the heavy-fermion phase-transition literature, in line with the broader Gutzwiller/superconductivity program of that era (it is, of course, Kubo's work in the period before his more famous 2011 PRL-accepted PAM superconductivity paper).

## [1305.2653v1-weak-point] Weakest link in the argument


I read the full text of Kubo's 2013 paper, "Ferromagnetism and Fermi-surface transition in the periodic Anderson model: Second-order phase transition without symmetry breaking" (arXiv:1305.2653). Below is the identification of its weakest link.

## The single weakest link

The step where the conclusion stops being entailed by the data is the inference from **Fig. 7's plots of the variational energy** to the headline claim that *"the transitions between the ferromagnetic phases can be second-order phase transitions… i.e. they are Lifshitz transitions."* Concretely:

- The data actually show: the **Gutzwiller variational energy** `e(ε_f)` is continuous (Fig. 7a); its first derivative has a jump at the FM2–FM1 boundary (Fig. 7b) and the second derivative jumps at FM1–FM0 and FM0–PM (Figs. 7c, 7d).
- The conclusion drawn: therefore these are genuine **thermodynamic** first- and second-order (Lifshitz) phase transitions, and the Appendix is invoked to show that a jump in `M′` with continuous `M` is a legitimate definition of "second order without symmetry breaking."

**Why the entailment breaks here** — the energy `e` is *not* the thermodynamic free energy of the periodic Anderson model. It is a **variational upper bound** obtained from a severely restricted trial manifold: Gutzwiller approximation, `T = 0`, the `U → ∞` limit, and a single spin-dependent momentum-space variational parameter `a_σ(k)` per `k` (Eqs. 3, 9, 13, 19–22). The singularity structure of a variational energy — whether its first or second derivative jumps at a point — is a property of the *chosen class of trial states*, not of the true partition function. Mean-field and variational energies are well known to misclassify transition order: they can round a genuinely first-order jump into an apparent cusp, or sharpen a continuous crossover. The paper never demonstrates that the true model's free energy `F(x,M)` inherits this singularity structure; it only shows that the *variational bound* does. The Appendix's argument (Eqs. 4.1–4.3) is internal to whatever `F` you hand it, so it faithfully certifies a property of the variational `F`, not of the model.

This weakness is self-acknowledged and corroborated by two of the paper's own caveats, which together mean the striking conclusion exists only at the tip of a stack of idealizations:

1. **Finite temperature.** "At finite temperatures, the second-order phase transitions between ferromagnetic phases would become crossovers, since the Fermi surface is not well defined at finite temperatures" (end of Sec. IV.D / top of Sec. V). The whole "phase transition" (as opposed to crossover) language is a `T = 0` artifact; experiments are always at finite `T`.
2. **`U → ∞` and single-orbital.** Quantitative agreement with heavy-fermion experiments requires finite `U` and orbital degrees of freedom, which the paper defers to "a future problem" (start of the Appendix to Sec. V). All the quantitative comparison to UGe₂, CeRu₂Si₂, YbRh₂Si₂ is therefore qualitative.

So the chain is: variational energy is smooth with a derivative jump → *therefore* a Lifshitz second-order transition of the model. The final "therefore" is the gap: a derivative jump in a zero-temperature variational upper bound, in a model whose real transitions become crossovers at any finite temperature, does not establish that the physical system exhibits a symmetry-less second-order phase transition.

(Side note, a genuine but secondary flaw: the Appendix's classification rule reads "M continuous ⟹ second order," treating the continuous polarization `M` as if its continuity alone excludes a first-order transition. ## Critique of *KubO, "Ferromagnetism and Fermi-surface transition in the periodic Anderson model," cond-mat/1305.2653* (writing and framing)

The paper is an internally clean single-parameter (ε_f) Gutzwiller/mean-field study at T = 0 and U → ∞ of ferromagnetic states in the periodic Anderson model. All of that is honest and fine. The problem is that the **headline** — a "Second-order phase transition without symmetry breaking" that is "a Lifshitz transition" — is pitched as a stronger statement than any of that demonstrates, and the abstract asserts two things the body itself backs away from.

### Sentences that overclaim beyond the evidence

**1. Title: "Second-order phase transition without symmetry breaking."**
This is the paper's identity and it is misleading. Both phases on either side of these transitions are ferromagnetic — time-reversal and spin-rotation symmetry are *already broken in both*. What is continuous is the crossing from one already-symmetry-broken manifold into another. That does not make the transition "without symmetry breaking" in any standard classification; at most it shows there is no *change* of broken symmetry. A title that reads like a novelty claim about universality ("no symmetry breaking") is doing more rhetorical work than the in-model calculation supports.

**2. Abstract, final sentence: "While we cannot define an order parameter for such transitions in an ordinary way, the topology of the Fermi surface characterizes the transitions, i.e., they are Lifshitz transitions."**
Two overclaims in one breath:

- *"i.e., they are Lifshitz transitions"* — this is contradicted by the author's own § IV.D: *"the originally proposed Lifshitz transition is of 2.5 order [35], while the present Lifshitz transition accompanying magnetism is of second order."* The abstract equates its transition with the Lifshitz one via "i.e." (i.e. = definitionally), and then the body concedes the defining property (order) differs. The abstract therefore states, and the body corrects.

**3. § II, p.2: "the f-electron wave-function is narrow and... we set U → ∞ for simplicity."**
The entire analysis lives in U → ∞, presented as a convenience. But the whole motivation (§ I) is *real* heavy-fermion systems (CeRh₂Si₂, YbRh₂Si₂, UGe₂), which are finite-U, finite-temperature systems. **4. § IV, p.4: "We expect that a change in the form of ρ(ε) will affect the results little unless ρ(ε) has some characteristic structures, such as strong peaks."**
The conduction band is taken as a flat DOS, ρ(ε)=1/(2W), and this sentence — an untested "we expect" — is the *only* justification. "Expect" is not a check; it should be a robustness statement with at least one non-flat band, or be downgraded to an assumption.

**5. § III, p.3: "we can derive also by the slave-boson mean-field theory of the Kotliar-Ruckenstein type, and several physical quantities... are equivalent between the slave-boson mean-field theory and the Gutzwiller method."**
Claimed without the derivation in this paper. It's a useful claim, but as written it's an assertion that a reviewer must take on faith; it should be either shown or scoped ("for the quantities used here, we checked X equals Y").

"We believe... are still captured" is the author's own hedge, but the entire Ce/Yb connection in § I leans on exactly this connection-to-experiments, and it is never justified by a single quantitative match. It belongs in the limitations, not as the bridge to the experiments.

This is honest but it quietly deflates the headline more than the abstract lets on: the "second-order transition" is a strictly T = 0 construct, and at any finite T it is a crossover. # Review

**What the paper does.** Kubo applies a spin-polarized Gutzwiller variational method to the periodic Anderson model (PAM) at $U\to\infty$ on one dimension with a flat-band DOS $\rho(\epsilon)=1/(2W)$. The variational state is a product of up/down Slater determinants of hybridized bands with site-projectors forbidding $f$-double occupancy (Eq. 2). From minimization they obtain self-consistent equations for the renormalized $f$-levels $\tilde\epsilon_{f\sigma}$ and polarizations $n_{f\sigma}$ (Eqs. 20–22). They map out a phase diagram in the $(V/W,\epsilon_f/W)$ plane at $n=1.25,1.55,1.75$ with three FM phases (FM0 weak FM, FM1 half-metal with $n_\uparrow=1$, FM2 saturated FM with small Fermi surface), track the Fermi-surface topology and effective mass, and argue that the FM0–FM1 (and the FM–PM with field) boundaries are genuine *second-order* phase transitions even though no symmetry is broken — the only "order parameter" is the topology of the Fermi surface (Lifshitz type).

## Strengths

1. **Motivation.** The connection to UGe$_2$ (FM1/FM2 labels, pressure) and to YbRh$_2Si_2$ (metamagnetism, FS change near 10 T) is well made and well cited; the pressure discussion in Sec. IV.A, including the sign structure of $\tilde a_{\epsilon_f}$, is a nice and non-trivial point.
2. **Method.** The variational construction is clean; the distinction between $n_\sigma<1$ and $n_\sigma>1$ bands (Eqs. 3 vs. 4) is handled correctly, and the author is upfront about the difference to the Kotliar–Ruckenstein slave-boson limit (the latter can't capture the discontinuity in the momentum distribution). The identification of Gutzwiller vs. slave-boson for the Fermi surface is a legitimate selling point.
3. **Effective-mass definition.** Using the jump in the momentum distribution (Eq. 24) rather than the $f$-quasiparticle residue is the right choice in a magnetically polarized state where the Fermi surface could be $c$-dominated. The author is honest about the limitation that $A$ (resistivity) cannot disentangle the spin branches.
4. **Appendix.** The envelope-theorem argument (Eqs. A.1–A.3) is correct: continuity of $M(x)$ plus a slope discontinuity does imply a jump in $\partial^2 F/\partial x^2$, which is the definition of a second-order transition under the thermodynamic convention. It does not rely on $M$ being an order parameter.
5. **Figures.** Figs. 1, 2, 7 read well; Fig. 7(b)–(d) — the "polynomial subtract and look at the remainder" — is an honest way to demonstrate the analytic class of the free energy.

## Weaknesses / things to fix

1. **The headline claim is under-proven.** "Second-order without symmetry breaking" is the title statement, but the argument in the appendix is essentially a re-labelling: *if* $M(x)$ is continuous and $dM/dx$ has a discontinuity *then* by definition $\partial^2 F/\partial x^2$ jumps. The physics question is whether, in the thermodynamic limit and in the Gutzwiller approximation, the free energy really is non-analytic at the FM0–FM1 boundary, and of what order. The author explicitly notes (correctly) that the original Lifshitz transition is 2.5 order because the singular free energy goes as $k_F^{d+2}$. The claim that the *magnetic* Lifshitz transition is exactly 2 order, rather than 2.5, is not backed by an exponent analysis — it is inferred from a numerical scan at one $n$. I would expect the singular piece to be weaker (or exactly zero) on the FM1 side where the FS has disappeared, and one should at least show the $k_F$-dependence of the free-energy singular part to fix the order.
2. **$U\to\infty$ is a hard limit that changes the universality class.** In the $U\to\infty$ Gutzwiller theory the projection $[1-n_{f\uparrow}n_{f\downarrow}]$ is exact and the mean-field self-consistency has artificial sharp features. Whether a finite $U$ Gutzwiller calculation (or a numerically exact QMC/DMFT calculation) would reproduce the same order classification is not addressed. The model also cannot describe a paramagnet with a large mass enhancement (the author acknowledges this in Sec. VI), which significantly limits the connection to the heavy-fermion ground states the introduction targets.
3. **Flat-band DOS in one dimension.** $\rho(\epsilon)=1/(2W)$ in 1D is a convenient but strong simplification. The order of a Lifshitz transition is, as noted above, dimension-dependent. The 2-vs-2.5 statement is therefore not robust to dimension — it needs to at least be stated explicitly as a property of the toy model, not of the materials.
4. **The FM1–FM2 boundary for $n=1.75$ is called first order (Fig. 2c dash).** At a first-order boundary without symmetry breaking one can have only one jump order (discontinuity of $M$). This is fine — but the discussion in Sec. IV.A about why the order changes with $n$ (larger $n$ → larger jump in FM1→FM2) is heuristic, not derived. It would benefit from a van-der-Waals loop analysis of the energy near the boundary.
5. **Antiferromagnetic section (Sec. V).** Only schematic; no calculations. As written, it is a set of guesses about what a future calculation might find. It should clearly be presented as a discussion / outlook and should either be backed by a reference with comparable schematic structure or shortened.
6. **Minor.** The "preliminary results in Ref. 23" line suggests the present paper is a modest extension of a conference proceedings; it would be worth distinguishing sharper which pieces are new. Eq. 24's definition works for PM and FM0/2 but is undefined for the majority spin of FM1; the author correctly notes this, but it should also be noted that any *quantitative* comparison of $m^*$ with, say, the de-Haas–van-Alphen effective mass in YbRh$_2Si_2$ is then blocked on the same model point.

## Bottom line for the student

Solid, well-motivated, clearly written. The most defensible piece of the paper is **not** the "second-order without symmetry breaking" claim — it is the phase diagram of the $U\to\infty$ Gutzwiller PAM and the demonstration that the Fermi-surface topology changes smoothly and continuously as a function of $H$ or $\epsilon_f$. I'd restructure:

- Move the phase-diagram results and the $H$-scan (Figs. 5, 6) to be the main result.
- Downgrade the appendix argument to what it really is (a consistency identity) and add either an explicit $k_F$-scaling of the free energy to justify "2nd order rather than 2.5-th order," or soften the claim to "Lifshitz-type continuous transition without symmetry breaking, of at least second order."
- Add a one-paragraph honest statement that $U\to\infty$ and a 1D flat DOS may both change the order and that a finite-$U$ QMC study would be the natural next step.
- Cut the AFM section to a paragraph in the discussion.

As written the main result is fine, but the strongest-sounding sentence in the abstract is the one I have the least evidence for.
