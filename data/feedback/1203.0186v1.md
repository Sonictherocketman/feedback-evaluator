## [1203.0186v1-general-review] General Review


# Review of "Limit experiments of GARCH" (Buchmann & Müller, *Bernoulli* 18(1), 2012; arXiv:1203.0186)

## What the paper does

The paper asks a precise question in Le Cam's framework of deficiency: is **COGARCH** (the continuous-time, Lévy-driven GARCH of Klüppelberg–Lindner–Müller) the *statistically* correct limit of the discrete-time GARCH experiments as the sampling mesh goes to zero? This is deliberately stronger than the already-established *in-distribution* convergence (Kallsen–Vesenmayer; Müller–Müller–Szimayer). They restrict the driving process to a **compound Poisson** measure for tractability and distinguish two sampling schemes:

- **Incomplete observations** — only the return process $G$ is observed, not the volatility $h$ (the realistic case).
- **Complete observations** — both $G$ and $h$ are observed (an idealization).

The headline arguments are:

1. **(Thm 2.1–2.2, §2.2)** Under the incomplete-observation scheme, the randomly thinned GARCH experiments do **not** converge in deficiency to COGARCH but to a *new* limiting experiment the authors construct, **MCOGARCH** (eqs. (2.5)–(2.6)). Thm 2.1 obtains this for one specific parametrization with only $Q_n\to Q$ in total variation; Thm 2.2 extends it to a broad class of parametrizations (compact parameter sets, $Q\ll\ell$, uniformity conditions (2.13)–(2.14)).

2. **(Thm 2.3, §2.3)** Under a monotonicity assumption on a Hellinger-type transform $g_{f,\zeta}$, COGARCH and MCOGARCH are **not equivalent in deficiency** except on a low‑dimensional set of parameters (conditions (i)–(v)), so the statistically correct limit for unobserved-volatility data is genuinely different from COGARCH.

3. **(Props 2.1–2.3, §2.4)** Under the complete-observation scheme, the GARCH, COGARCH and MCOGARCH experiments are all generically equivalent to a simple reference experiment, so COGARCH *is* then a legitimate continuous-time equivalent of GARCH. The dividing line is observability of the volatility.

## Strengths

- **Rigorous, self-contained and well-argued.** The Le Cam/deficiency apparatus (Helinger integrals, Markov-kernel construction, the monotonicity theorem for Markov kernels) is applied correctly, and the Appendix makes the paper legible to someone who has not read Strasser in detail.
- **The central insight is genuinely new and, in a sense, surprising:** the statistically relevant limit under the *natural* (unobserved-volatility) regime is *not* the COGARCH that already appears as the in-distribution limit, but a distinct object (MCOGARCH) whose jump times carry "more randomness." Identifying the **complete-vs-incomplete observation dichotomy** as the factor that decides whether COGARCH is or is not a valid limit is the paper's real conceptual contribution.
- **Honest about scope.** The conclusion explicitly concedes that extending beyond compound Poisson to general Lévy drivers "seems out of reach" and that the complete-observation result is "mainly of theoretical interest." That candour is to its credit.
- The simulation (Wilcoxon rank test, Table 3) is a sensible pragmatic complement that probes parameter points not covered by the monotonicity assumption of Thm 2.3.

## Main critique

1. **The positive, usable model is the one that never arises in practice, and the "correct" model is not fit-able.** The result that GARCH→MCOGARCH is the main theorem, but MCOGARCH is defined only through the abstract time change $T_\kappa$ in (2.6), embedded in an infinite-dimensional Poisson point process — nobody is going to estimate it. The paper's *constructive* message is therefore essentially negative ("COGARCH is not the right statistical limit for raw data"). Meanwhile the clean "COGARCH *is* the limit" result requires observing the unobservable volatility path. So the **applied takeaway is murky**, and the paper does not give practitioners a crisp decision rule. A short interpretive paragraph ("what should you therefore do with COGARCH?") would go a long way.

2. **The compound-Poisson restriction is a real and under-acknowledged scope limitation.** Dropping the Brownian component means the limit paths are pure jump and the model loses the diffusion behaviour that real high-frequency data display. Since the whole motivation is financial, it matters that the result does not extend to the more realistic Brownian-plus-jump setting, and the paper leaves that entirely open (no partial results, no conjecture). One would like at least a statement of what breaks (the "three sources of randomness") in the general Lévy case.

3. **The Thm 2.3 hypothesis and its equivalence set are hard to translate into economics.** The condition that $g_{f,\zeta}(h)$ be strictly increasing on $(0,1]$ is a measure-analytic property, not a financial one, and is only *verified* for the symmetric-gamma and centered-Cauchy families. Equivalence then collapses to the low-dimensional relations (i)–(v) (e.g. $\beta_1 h_0=\beta_2 h_0$, $\alpha_2/\alpha_1$ tied to $h_0/\beta$), which have no clear financial meaning and are measure-zero. The result is a clean *qualitative* non-equivalence but gives no quantitative statement ("how different is COGARCH?").

4. **Density and presentation.** The results are stated at the level of decision spaces, $D_1/\ell_\infty$-equivalence and Hellinger integrals throughout, with the "message" buried. The notation (the $Q_\theta$, $(2.22)$ piecewise construction, the $\widehat\Theta_e$, the $\Psi_{d,w,\theta}$ recursion in (6.1)) is heavy. A reader who wants the *conclusion* — "in deficiency, unobserved-vol GARCH→MCOGARCH≠COGARCH; observed-vol GARCH→COGARCH" — must wade through pages of measure-theory to find it. This is acceptable for *Bernoulli* but does limit the audience and hence the reach.

5. **No real-data illustration.** Given the financial motivation (GARCH, stylized facts, option pricing), the total absence of empirical work — even a single high-frequency panel to which the convergence claim could be sanity-checked — is a notable omission for an audience that is substantially applied. The simulation substitutes for, but does not replace, it.

## Minor technical points

- **§2.3, "median of the likelihood ratio."** The objects are path-valued experiments with no literal likelihood ratio; what is actually being tested is the Hellinger-transform analogue (as the text hints with (2.20)). Calling it a "likelihood ratio" is loose and could mislead a reader trying to reproduce it. The software, seeds and exact statistic should also be documented for reproducibility.
- **Symmetric-gamma "straightforward" monotonicity.** The closed form $g_{f,\zeta}(h)=(h^{bc}/(h^c\zeta+1-\zeta))^{c/b}$ is claimed strictly increasing on $(0,1]$ *for all* $0<\zeta<1$. But differentiating the base gives sign controlled by $(b-1)\zeta u+b(1-\zeta)$, which evaluates to $b-\zeta$ at $u=1$. So for small $b$ (e.g. $b=0.5$, $\zeta=0.9$) the function is *decreasing* near $h=1$, and the stated monotonicity fails. It holds for $b\ge1$, which covers the named special cases (normal $b=2$, Laplace $b=1$), so the *examples* are fine, but the blanket claim over the whole $(a,b,c>0)$ family is an overclaim that should be qualified (e.g. "for $b\ge1$, or more generally whenever $b\ge\zeta$").
- **Normal/Laplace membership.** This is fine upon inspection (normal ↔ $c=1,b=2$; Laplace ↔ $c=1,b=1$), but the formula as written has an $|z|^{c-1}$ factor that makes the $c\to0$ regime singular; a one-line note that the named members correspond to $c=1$ would pre-empt doubt.

## Verdict

A competent, rigorous, and in places genuinely insightful contribution to the *statistical* (deficiency) limit theory of GARCH. Its durable value is the construction of MCOGARCH and the demonstration that **observability of the volatility — not the choice of approximation — determines whether COGARCH is a valid continuous-time limit.** That is a real and somewhat counterintuitive correction to the literature. However, its reach is bounded by (i) the compound-Poisson restriction, (ii) the practical unreachability of both the MCOGARCH limit and the complete-observation regime, and (iii) a level of abstraction that keeps the message distant from the (largely applied) readership the model is for. Solid but specialized; useful reference in the subfield rather than a field-shaping work.

Theorem 2.1 shows convergence of the thinned-GARCH experiment to MCOGARCH under total-variation convergence of the jump measures; Theorem 2.3 then gives necessary conditions for COGARCH and MCOGARCH to be equivalent, showing equivalence is restricted to lower-dimensional, non-open parameter sets.
2. **Complete observations** (volatility observable): the discrete and continuous (M)COGARCH experiments are generically equivalent to each other and to COGARCH (Section 2.4, Propositions 2.1–2.3).

**Strengths.**

## The paper's stated contribution (in my own words)

Buchmann & Müller (Bernoulli 2012) take a question that the continuous-time GARCH literature had already answered in a weaker sense — *does the discrete GARCH model converge to the continuous-time COGARCH process* — and answer it in the stronger, subtler sense of **Le Cam's theory of statistical experiments (convergence in deficiency / asymptotic equivalence)**. They restrict attention to GARCH driven by **compound Poisson innovations** (a single driving Lévy dimension) and, as the sampling grid is refined, ask whether the *experiments* generated by GARCH and by its candidate continuous-time limits become statistically indistinguishable. The headline results are:

- **Complete observations** (the volatility process h observed in full, the idealized case): the GARCH experiment converges **strongly** to the COGARCH experiment. COGARCH is thus a genuine continuous-time statistical equivalent of GARCH (Prop. 2.1 / Thm. 2.2 region).
- **Incomplete observations** (the realistic case — volatility unobserved): GARCH does **not** converge to COGARCH. Instead it converges in deficiency to a **new limiting experiment** that the authors call **MC-GARCH** ("modified COGARCH") (Thm. 2.1), and they prove (Thm. 2.3) that MC-GARCH is in general *not* statistically equivalent to COGARCH — equivalence holds only on parameter sets of strictly lower dimension. The practical gloss: in COGARCH the jump *times* can be "more random" than in MC-GARCH.

## Is it novel, given the cited prior work?

**What is not new** (and the paper is honest about this):
- **COGARCH as the continuous-time limit of GARCH** is not their idea. That machinery is Klüppelberg–Lindner–Maller (2004) [17], Kallsen–Vesenmayer (2009) [16], and Maller–Müller–Szymayer (2008) [20], who already obtained convergence **in law / in distribution / in probability** with a *general* Lévy driving process. This paper is, in that respect, *narrower* (it specialises to compound Poisson) and the limit object itself is pre-existing.
- The **entire Le Cam framework** — deficiency, asymptotic equivalence, the Markov-kernel characterisation — is Strasser (1985) [27] and Le Cam [18]; they are importing a ready-made tool.
- **Random thinning** as the discretisation/limiting device is standard and explicitly attributed ([16], [20], and cited elsewhere).
- That **Nelson's diffusion is not a valid deficiency-limit of GARCH** is already established by Wang (2002) [28] and Brown–Wang–Zhao (2003) [6]; the present paper is essentially the *successor question* for COGARCH, not a fresh discovery of non-equivalence per se.

**What genuinely is new:**
1. **The first formulation of the GARCH→COGARCH limit problem in the deficiency / statistical-equivalence language.** [16] and [20] proved convergence "in law/distribution/probability"; the authors explicitly note (Intro, Sec. 3) that such passage is only statistically justified if the corresponding *experiments* converge in deficiency, and they do not. Elevating a law-convergence result to a statement about the recoverability of model parameters from the data is a real and non-trivial step.
2. **The MC-GARCH object and its non-equivalence to COGARCH (Thm. 2.1 + Thm. 2.3).** This is the paper's real content. The observation that, once the volatility is unobserved, the true limit experiment is *not* COGARCH but a modified cousin with jump times that cannot be more random than the compound-Poisson clock, had not been identified in [16], [20] or the Nelson-diffusion line. Theorem 2.3's characterisation that equivalence collapses to lower-dimensional parameter slices is a sharp, original negative result.
3. **A strong convergence-in-deficiency theorem for the *thinned* GARCH with essentially arbitrary limiting jump law Q** (only total-variation convergence Q_n→Q required), going beyond the fixed-Q, compound-Poisson set-up of [16]/[20]. Remark 2.2(i)–(ii) also extends the discussion to the peak-over-threshold/conditional-tail regimes where the limit is still COGARCH-in-law but generically MC-GARCH-in-deficiency — a nuance not in the cited work.

**Judgement on novelty:** The work sits in a narrow but active corner (asymptotic statistical theory of GARCH). It is **not** a new model or a new limit theorem, and it *builds directly and openly* on the COGARCH-limit literature, which drags its originality down. But the decisive move — recognising that under the *realistic* unobserved-volatility condition the deficiency-limit is a distinct process (MC-GARCH) and proving its non-equivalence to COGARCH — is genuinely new and was not contained in [16], [20], or [28]/[6]. So it is an **incremental-on-the-machinery but substantive-on-the-statistical-conclusion** contribution: a good step forward inside the niche rather than a paradigm change.

**Caveats that temper the impact:** the realistic case (Thm. 2.1/2.3) is exactly the case the authors concede has little direct predictive content because volatility is unobservable; the equivalence result of real interest (complete observations) is "mainly of theoretical interest." The general-Lévy extension is left as future work. The simulation only *suggests* the generality of Thm. 2.3.

**Scores:** Originality 4 (new MC-GARCH non-equivalence result + first deficiency framing, but heavily parasitic on prior COGARCH-limit and Le Cam machinery), Overall 4 (self-contained, rigorous, proofs included and readable within the niche; technically dense and specialised), Impact 3 (valuable to the small community doing asymptotic theory of stochastic-volatility models; limited practical reach because its strongest statement needs unattainable full-observation).

# Evaluation of arXiv:1203.0186v1 — "Limit experiments of GARCH" (Buchmann & Müller, Bernoulli 18(1), 2012, 64–99)

## 1. Reproducibility-level summary of the methodology

The paper's object of study is **the family of Le Cam statistical experiments** associated with a discretely-sampled **randomly thinned GARCH(1,1)** model, and its **deficiency-limit** as the number of observations n→∞. The central experiment is the discrete-time GARCH experiment $E_{n,H_n}$ and its claimed continuous-time limit. Three candidate limits are constructed and mutually compared in Le Cam's deficiency distance: the classical **COGARCH** experiment $E$, a newly introduced **MCOGARCH** (modified COGARCH) experiment $\bar E$, and the fully-observed versions $E^h, \bar E^{\,h}$.

### 1.1 The discrete-time GARCH model being approximated

The starting point (eq. 2.1–2.3) is an $n$-sample GARCH(1,1)-type recursion driven by a *randomly thinned* innovation:

- Innovation vector $Z_n = (Z_{n,k})_{1\le k\le n}$ with i.i.d. marginal law $\mathcal L(Z_{n,k}) = (1-p_n)\varepsilon_0 + p_n Q_n$, where $\varepsilon_0$ is the Dirac mass at 0, $p_n\in(0,1)$ tends to 0, and $Q_n$ is a probability measure on $\mathbb R$ (jump distribution at level $n$).
- **Rare-events constraint**: $\gamma := \lim_{n\to\infty} n p_n \in (0,\infty)$ (eq. 2.2).
- GARCH recursion (2.3), with the "parameterization" $H_n(\theta)=(h_{0,n},\beta_n,\alpha_n,\lambda_n)$:
  $$G_n(k) = G_n(k-1) + \sqrt{h_n(k-1)}\,Z_{n,k},\qquad
  h_n(k) = \beta_n + \alpha_n h_n(k-1) + \lambda_n h_n(k-1)\,Z_{n,k}^{2},\quad h_n(0)=h_{0,n},$$
  $G_n(0)=0$. The parameter $\lambda$ multiplies the squared innovation (the usual GARCH $\beta$ here is their $\alpha_n h_{n(k-1)}$ and is absorbed into the product).
- Parameters are indexed by $\theta=(h_0,\beta,\alpha,\lambda)\in [0,\infty)^4$ with $h_0$ (initial volatility) treated as an **additional unknown** (convention (2.3), stated at the start of §2).
- **Experiment**: $E_{n,H_n}(\Theta) = (\mathbb R^n, \mathcal B(\mathbb R^n), (\mathcal L_\theta(G_n))_{\theta\in\Theta})$ (eq. 2.10).

Two sampling schemes are treated separately:
- **Incomplete observation**: only $(G_n)$ (resp. $(G)$) is visible; $h_n$ (resp. $h$) is latent.
- **Complete observation**: both processes are visible; the experiment is on $\mathbb R^{2n}$ (resp. $\mathcal D^2$).

### 1.2 Continuous-time limits: COGARCH vs MCOGARCH

The limit processes $(G,h)$ are the pathwise solutions (on time horizon $[0,1]$) of integral equations driven by a Poisson random measure $N$ with intensity $\gamma\,\ell\otimes Q$:

- **COGARCH** (eq. 2.4): 
  $G(t)=\int_{[0,t]\times\mathbb R}h^{1/2}(s_-)z\,N(ds,dz)$,
  $h(t)=h_0+\int_0^t(\beta-\alpha h(s_-))ds+\int_{[0,t]\times\mathbb R}\lambda h(s_-)z^2 N(ds,dz)$.
- **MCOGARCH** $(\tilde G,\tilde h)$ (eq. 2.5): identical to (2.4) **except** that the mean-reversion time integral $\int(\beta-\alpha\tilde h(s_-))ds$ is replaced by $\int(\beta-\alpha\tilde h(s_-))\,dT_N(s)$, where $T_N$ is the **cumulative jump-location operator** defined at (2.6): for a point measure $\sigma=\sum_k\varepsilon_{(t_k,x_k)}$ with $m$ points $(0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_m<1)$,
  $T_\sigma(t)=\tfrac{t-t_k}{m(t_k-t_{k-1})}+\tfrac{k}{m}$ for $t\in[t_{k-1},t_k)$ and $T_\sigma(t)=\tfrac{t-t_m}{m(t_m-t_{m-1})}+1$ for $t\in[t_m,1]$.
  Concretely, on the jump grid $T_N$ is piecewise linear from 0 to 1 passing through each jump time; it is the **regularized time scale** that makes the "arrival times" behave as equally-spaced order statistics within each count $m$, not as exponential spacings. MCOGARCH is driven by **two sources** of randomness (jump count, jump sizes) whereas COGARCH is driven by **three** (jump count, jump sizes, jump location).

The corresponding experiments are $E=(\mathcal D^1,\mathcal D^1,(\mathcal L_\theta(G))_{\theta\in[0,\infty)^4})$ and $\bar E=(\mathcal D^1,\mathcal D^1,(\mathcal L_\theta(\tilde G))_{\theta\in[0,\infty)^4})$, with $\mathcal D^1$ the Skorokhod space equipped with the point-evaluation sigma-algebra.

### 1.3 The GARCH parameterizations compared

Two families:

- **Paper's "reference" parameterization** ($H^{(0)}_n$, eqs. 2.11–2.12), for $\alpha>0$:
  $$h_{0,n}(\theta)=h_0 e^{-\alpha/n}+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}(1-e^{-\alpha/n}),\quad
  \beta_n(\theta)=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}(1-e^{-\alpha/n}),\quad
  \alpha_n(\theta)=e^{-\alpha/n},\quad
  \lambda_n(\theta)=\lambda e^{-\alpha/n},$$
  and the $\alpha=0$ analogue at (2.12). This is the unique parametrization under which Theorem 2.1 is proved with no restriction on $Q$.
- **Existing literature** ($H^{(KV)}_n$ by Kallsen–Vesenmayer 2012, $H^{(M)}_n$ by Maller et al. 2012), eq. (2.16): $h_{0,n}=h_0$, $\beta_n=\beta/n$, $\alpha_n=e^{-\alpha/n}$, and either $\lambda_n=\lambda$ or $\lambda_n=e^{-\alpha/n}\lambda$. The paper's **Theorem 2.2** shows these two parameterizations also converge to MCOGARCH (not COGARCH), provided the jump distribution $Q$ admits a Lebesgue density.

For **complete observation** a further experiment $E^{h,n}_{H_n}=([\mathbb R^{n+1}]^2,\mathcal B((\mathbb R^{n+1})^2),(\mathcal L_\theta(G_n,h_n)))$ is defined (eq. 2.23).

### 1.4 The main theorems

- **Theorem 2.1** (strong deficiency convergence, incomplete obs). Under $Q_n\to Q$ in **total variation** and $np_n\to\gamma>0$, $E_{n,H^{(0)}_n}\xrightarrow{\text{deficiency}}\bar E$ (strongly).
- **Theorem 2.2** (uniform parameter, incomplete obs). If additionally $Q\ll\ell$ and the parameterization satisfies the uniform rate bounds
  $\sup_\theta\max\{|h_{0,n}-h_0|,|\lambda_n-\lambda|\}\le C/n$ and
  $\sup_\theta\max\{|n\beta_n-\beta|,|n(\alpha_n-1)+\alpha|\}\le C$,
  then the convergence holds uniformly for any $\Theta$ with compact closure in $(0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)^3$, and $E_{n,H_n}\to \bar E^{(\Theta)}$ strongly in deficiency. In particular $H^{(KV)}_n$ and $H^{(M)}_n$ fall in this scope, so the prior "GARCH→COGARCH-in-law" conclusions must be refined to "GARCH→MCOGARCH-in-deficiency".
- **Theorem 2.3** (non-equivalence, incomplete obs). For any jump distribution $Q$ with strictly positive Lebesgue density $f$ that satisfies a Hellinger-monotonicity condition $g_{f,\zeta_0}$ strictly increasing on $(0,1]$ for some $\zeta_0$ (verified for the generalized symmetric gamma family — which contains $N(0,\sigma^2)$ and Laplace — and for centered Cauchy), any two-parameter equivalence between $E_{\gamma,Q}$ and $\bar E_{\gamma,Q}$ forces the parameter set to have considerably lower dimension (conditions (i)–(v) listed in the statement). **Generically**, COGARCH and MCOGARCH are *not* equivalent in deficiency.
- **Propositions 2.1–2.3** (complete obs). With volatilities observable, both $E_h$ (COGARCH) and $\bar E^h$ (MCOGARCH) are **equivalent** to a simple reference experiment $F=([0,\infty]^4,\mathcal B,(\mathcal Q_\theta))$ where $\mathcal Q_\theta$ is a Bernoulli mixture on $\{(h_0,\beta,\alpha,\lambda),(h_0,\infty,\infty,\infty)\}$ driven by the event "no Poisson arrivals on $[0,1]$" (mass $e^{-\gamma}$) or a jump (mass $1-e^{-\gamma}$). Similarly the discrete experiments $E^{h,n}_{H_n}$ converge to $F_n$ with a discrete exceptional set $\Theta^e_n=\{h_0 n(1-e^{-\alpha/n})=\beta\}$, and $F_n\to F$ in deficiency (weakly). Consequence: under **complete observation** GARCH, COGARCH, MCOGARCH are all asymptotically equivalent in deficiency **generically** — i.e., except on the fixed-point set $\Theta^e=\{h_0\alpha=\beta\}$, where only $h_0$ can be recovered from the observation path before the first jump.

### 1.5 Proof machinery (Sections 4–7)

- **Theorem 2.1** (§4): three-step sandwich.
  (i) Build an intermediate experiment $\mathsf E^\star_{1,n}$: the GARCH process embedded in an integral-equation form (2.4) but with the deterministic grid measure $N_{1,n}=\sum_k 1_{\{Z_{n,k}\ne 0\}}\varepsilon_{(k/n,Z_{n,k})}$. Show this is **deficiency-equivalent** to $E_{n,H^{(0)}_n}$ by an explicit identity $h_n(k)=h^{1,n}(((k+1)/n)-)$ (eq. after 4.3).
  (ii) Build $\mathsf E^\star_{2,n}$: same construction but with the grid randomized to $V_{n,k}=((k-1)+U_k)/n$ via i.i.d. $U_k\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$, producing the measure $N_{2,n}=\sum_k 1_{\{Z_{n,k}\ne0\}}\varepsilon_{(V_{n,k},Z_{n,k})}$.
  (iii) **Lemma 4.1**: $\mathcal L(N_{2,n})\to\mathcal L(N)$ in total variation (a rare-events / law-of-small-numbers argument for thinning Bernoulli point measures against a Poisson limit, plus total variation on $Q_n\to Q$). A monotonicity + coupling argument then gives $\mathsf E^\star_{2,n}\to\bar E$ strongly, and $\mathsf E^\star_{1,n}\sim\mathsf E^\star_{2,n}$ in deficiency (the grid perturbation is "invisible" in deficiency because the count of non-zero $Z_{n,k}$ and their $Z$ values are the same, and the experiments are dominated).
- **Theorem 2.2** (§5): reduce to total variation on the jump distribution plus a discretization error; use the fact that for two **dominated** experiments with bounded likelihood ratios, deficiency is controlled by the total-variation distance of the laws (Strasser's theorem), and pass the $\Theta$-sup bound via an exponential-Lipschitz estimate on $h$ along the recursion.
- **Theorem 2.3** (§6): the non-equivalence proof. (a) Both experiments are reduced (via change of measure, eq. (A.2)) to a "count–arrival-sizes" experiment $F_n=(\mathbb R^e,\mathcal B^e,(\mathcal L_\theta(\nu_n,\Delta U_{\nu_n},\Delta G_{\nu_n})))$ since all jump times are observed (they coincide with the jumps of $G$). (b) The equivalence test reduces to equality of **Hellinger transformations** $H^{(n)}_{\theta_1,\theta_2}(\zeta)$; these are computed explicitly via the diffeomorphism
  $\Psi_{d,w,\theta}(z)=(h_{d,w,\theta,k}(z)\,z_k)_{1\le k\le d}$, with $h$ given by the recursion (6.1):
  $$h_{d,w,\theta,k}(z)=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}(1-e^{-\alpha w_k})+e^{-\alpha w_k}(1+\lambda z_{k-1}^2)h_{d,w,\theta,k-1}(z),\qquad
  h_{d,w,\theta,1}\equiv\frac{\beta}{\alpha}(1-e^{-\alpha w_1})+e^{-\alpha w_1}h_0.$$
  (c) The infinite series identity (below (A.5) on p.28) equates the Hellinger integrals over $S_d$ (the simplex $\{\sum w_i\le 1\}$) for COGARCH with the degenerate point $w^*=(1/d,\dots,1/d)$ for MCOGARCH. The monotonicity assumption $g_{f,\zeta}$ strictly increasing then shows these integrals are not equal for generic parameters, yielding non-equivalence. The five conditions (i)–(v) of the theorem are the only parameter configurations in which the equality could hold.
- **Proposition 2.1–2.3** (§7): change-of-measure argument. Under complete observation the full path of $(G,h)$ determines the Poisson count $\nu(1)$ with mass $e^{-\gamma}$ (no jumps), and with probability $1-e^{-\gamma}$ exposes the full parameter $\theta$ (via differentiable recovery of $\beta,\alpha,\lambda,h_0$ from the ODE $h'=\beta-\alpha h$ on $[0,T)$). For MCOGARCH the same holds because $\tilde h$ is also observable; the only difference between COGARCH and MCOGARCH is the law of the jump times, which is **irrelevant in deficiency** once $h$ is visible. This gives the reference experiment $\mathcal F$ in (2.22). The discrete-time version $F_n$ has the fixed-point set $\Theta^e_n=\{h_0 n(1-e^{-\alpha_n/n})=\beta_n\}$, which converges to $\Theta^e$ as $n\to\infty$; the resulting deficiency distance $\delta(F,F^{(n)})\to 0$ follows from a stability of the Bernoulli-mixture experiments.

### 1.6 The numerical experiment (Section 2.3, reproducibility checklist)

Purpose: **empirical support for Theorem 2.3**, including the mixed-normal case not covered by the theorem.

Setup (fixed for all 8×3 configurations):
- Base parameter $\theta_0=(2,1,1,0.1)\in(h_0,\beta,\alpha,\lambda)$ (Table 1).
- Eight alternatives $\theta_{ij}$ that differ from $\theta_0$ in exactly one coordinate: $(0.4,1,1,0.1),(10,1,1,0.1),(2,0.2,1,0.1),(2,5,1,0.1),(2,1,0.2,0.1),(2,1,5,0.1),(2,1,1,0.02),(2,1,1,0.5)$.
- Intensity $\gamma=4$ throughout.
- Three jump distributions: $N(0,1)$, standard Cauchy $\mathrm{Cauchy}(0,1)$, and the symmetric two-component normal mixture $\tfrac12 N(-0.5,0.75)+\tfrac12 N(0.5,0.75)$ (mean 0, variance 1). The first two are covered by the monotonicity condition used in Theorem 2.3; the mixture is **not** — that is what the simulation is probing.

Sampling (recursion 6.1 / 6.2):
- For each configuration, draw $N\in\{10^4,10^5,10^6\}$ i.i.d. copies of the "reduced" observation: Poisson($\gamma$) count $d=\nu(1)$; if $d=0$ record the zero; otherwise draw i.i.d. $w=(w_1,\dots,w_d)\in S_d$ — **uniform under COGARCH** (this is the induced uniform-density on $S_d$ from the conditional density of the order statistics of $d$ exponential spacings summing to 1) and **the single point $w^*=(1/d,\dots,1/d)$ under MCOGARCH** — together with i.i.d. $z_k\sim Q$.
- Compute the **likelihood ratio** $\mathcal L_\theta(G)/\mathcal L_{\theta_0}(G)$ at each parameter $\theta$ — for COGARCH, via the density of the Poisson-point measure with respect to the jump-arrival-times density on $S_d$; for MCOGARCH, via the density at the fixed $w^*$. The recursion (6.1) for $h_{d,w,\theta,k}$ lets each likelihood be evaluated per sample in $O(d)$ arithmetic.
- The paper reports (Table 2) the **25 / 50 / 75 percentiles** of the two likelihood-ratio samples (COGARCH row and MCOGARCH row for each $\theta_{ij}$ and each $Q$).
- Apply a **Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann–Whitney U) test** for the null hypothesis "median of COGARCH LR = median of MCOGARCH LR"; report the standardized stat $W$ and two-sided p-value (Table 3).

Outcome: for every (jump dist, $\theta_{ij}$), the p-value collapses to 0 at $N=10^6$; the smallest signal among the 24 combinations is around $p\approx 10^{-5}$, all consistent with the null being false. The authors **conjecture** from this evidence that Theorem 2.3 holds for "quite arbitrary jump distributions", not only those satisfying the monotonicity assumption.

### 1.7 Reproducing the central experiment (checklist)

1. Choose $n$ (sample size), $p_n=\gamma/n$ (or any sequence with $np_n\to\gamma$), and $Q_n$ (e.g. $Q_n\equiv Q$).
2. Draw $Z_{n,k}$ i.i.d. as $(1-p_n)\varepsilon_0+p_n Q_n$ for $k=1,\dots,n$.
3. Fix $\theta=(h_0,\beta,\alpha,\lambda)$; set $(h_{0,n},\beta_n,\alpha_n,\lambda_n)$ via (2.11) or (2.12).
4. Run the recursion (2.3); record $G_n=(G_n(1),\dots,G_n(n))$ (incomplete obs) or $(G_n,h_n)$ (complete obs).
5. For **complete obs**, apply Propositions 2.1–2.3: the experiment is asymptotically equivalent to $F$ (an experiment in $[0,\infty]^4$ with a Bernoulli mixture $e^{-\gamma}\varepsilon_{\text{fixed point}}+(1-e^{-\gamma})\varepsilon_\theta$). Verify $\delta(F,F_n)\to 0$ directly (Bernoulli mixture experiments, total variation of the two mixing weights).
6. For **incomplete obs**, apply Theorems 2.1–2.3: verify the strong convergence to $\bar E$ (via the Lemma 4.1 construction in §4.1–4.3, or via the $Q_n\to Q$-in-TV path of §5). For non-equivalence, check the Hellinger-transformation identity in §6 and verify the monotonicity of $g_{f,\zeta_0}$ for your chosen $Q$ (explicit closed form for gamma-family: $g_{f,\zeta}(h)=\big(\frac{h^\zeta}{h^{\zeta}+(1-\zeta)}\big)^{c/b}$, eq. after (2.19)).
7. To reproduce **Table 2/3**: sample (d, $w$, $z$) as described above with $N=10^6$; compute LR for $\theta=\theta_{ij}$ against $\theta_0$; apply the Wilcoxon two-sample test to the two LR samples; tabulate 25/50/75 quantiles and p-values.

This is a self-contained, executable procedure — no proprietary code, no data outside the jump distributions, no fitting of parameters to real data.

## 2. Assumptions the methodology papers over

(Each item is a restriction under which a result is proved or that the simulation silently relies on; the reader reproducing the central claim in any realistic setting must check all of these.)

1. **Driving Lévy process restricted to compound Poisson.** The whole framework assumes the innovation sequence converges to a **compound Poisson process** via random thinning. The authors state explicitly (§3 Conclusion) that extending to general Lévy processes "seems out of reach at the present stage". Thus results do **not** cover, e.g., the stable or variance-gamma-driven COGARCH that appears in the prior KL–M / Maller–Vesenmayer papers, and the "GARCH→COGARCH" statement is only for that special slice of the COGARCH family. This is the single largest gap between the paper's title (which promises a general answer for COGARCH as a continuous-time GARCH limit) and what is actually shown.

2. **Random thinning, not aggregation.** The rare-events limit ($np_n\to\gamma$) is essential. The classical **aggregated-innovations** regime — $Z_{n,k}=\sum_i X_{n,i}$ with $nX_{n,i}\to$ Gaussian/Brownian — gives **Nelson's diffusion**, not COGARCH, and Wang / Brown already showed that diffusion limit is *not* equivalent to GARCH. The paper's results do not apply in that regime. So "GARCH converges to COGARCH" is true only under the *specific* sampling scheme of rare, non-aggregated events; this is a real modelling choice, not a mathematical necessity.

3. **Total variation convergence $Q_n\to Q$.** Both Theorem 2.1 and Theorem 2.2 require $\|Q_n-Q\|_{TV}\to 0$. This is *stronger* than weak convergence. In the peak-over-threshold / extreme-value application the authors themselves raise (Remark 2.1(ii)), $Q_n$ converges *weakly* to a generalized Pareto law; the paper only argues *informally* that the weak conclusion (COGARCH limit in law) may still hold in that setting, and explicitly leaves a deficiency statement in that setting to future work. A reader applying this to tail data has no deficiency guarantee.

4. **Strictly positive Lebesgue density $f$ for $Q$ (Theorem 2.3 only).** The non-equivalence result requires $\operatorname{ess inf} f>0$. Discrete or mixed $Q$ are not covered by the theorem. For the **simulation**, the mixed normal is still absolutely continuous so this is fine, but the jump distribution of a heavy-tailed empirical application may have mass at 0 (e.g., micro-ticks) and then the theorem is silent.

5. **The Hellinger monotonicity condition $g_{f,\zeta_0}$ strictly increasing on $(0,1]$ for some $\zeta_0$.** Only three families are verified: generalized symmetric gamma (containing normal, Laplace, generalized gamma) and centered Cauchy. Nothing structural proves that the monotonicity condition holds for, say, skewed or multimodal heavy-tailed $Q$. The authors *conjecture* from the simulation that the theorem's conclusion holds more generally, but do not prove it. This is a real "papered over" assumption: Theorem 2.3 is a structural non-equivalence theorem only under a density-shape condition that the reader must **check independently** for each application.

6. **$\gamma_n\ne\gamma$ for all $n$ (Theorem 2.3 hypothesis).** This is an artificial restriction; if the convergence is eventually constant ($\gamma_n\equiv\gamma$) the statement as written is not applicable. The proof (via an infinite series identity) presumably extends, but it is not stated.

7. **$Q(\{0\})=0$ (Propositions 2.1–2.3).** Incomplete: the equivalence-to-$F$ results are stated under $Q(\{0\})=0$. The paper's own Remark 2.2 says the fix is "easy" (replace $\gamma$ by $\gamma Q(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\})$), but leaves "details to the reader". In practice $Q(\{0\})>0$ is common (e.g., a GARCH model with intermittent volatility), and the formal proof is missing.

8. **Parametrization restrictions.** Theorem 2.2 requires $\overline\Theta$ compact in $(0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)^3$, i.e., **$h_0>0$ uniformly**. The boundary $h_0=0$ (initial volatility zero) is excluded by the rate bounds (2.13)–(2.14). The α=0 (no mean reversion) case is handled separately by the $H_n^{(0)}(2.12)$ formula and not absorbed into the main theorem.

9. **The "generically equivalent" phrasing in complete observation is not uniform in the parameter.** Propositions 2.1–2.3 produce equivalence on the complement of $\Theta^e=\{h_0\alpha=\beta\}$ (or its $n$-th analogue $\Theta^e_n$), a **codimension-1 set**. The statement "GARCH converges generically to COGARCH in deficiency" is therefore: the parameter sets for which convergence/equivalence holds are open and dense, and equivalence fails exactly at the fixed-point set. A reader who fits a GARCH to data with $\hat h_0\hat\alpha\approx\hat\beta$ (a real-data possibility — e.g., initial volatility equal to the long-run level) is **outside the genericity statement** — the equivalence claim does not apply, and $F$-equivalence degenerates to $e^{-\gamma}\varepsilon_{(h_0,\infty,\infty,\infty)}+(1-e^{-\gamma})\varepsilon_{(h_0,\infty,\infty,0/1)}$, i.e., one loses $\alpha,\beta$ identification at the single-jump event.

10. **Observation scheme is binary: full $(G,h)$ or $G$ only.** There is no treatment of **partial** observation (e.g., $G$ plus occasional observations of $h$), **noisy** volatility (realized-variance or Aït-Sahalia–Mykland–Zhang type estimators that converge to $h$ with an error term), or **traded-volatility substitution** (the Hubalek–Posedel idea of replacing $h$ by observable trading volume), all of which the paper **mentions** in two paragraphs of §2.4 but does **not** incorporate. A practitioner cannot directly import the main theorems to a realized-variance-estimated COGARCH; the "complete observation" equivalence is a statement about a mathematically idealized data set that does not exist in practice.

11. **Initial volatility $h_0$ treated as an unknown parameter.** Classical GARCH(1,1) fixes $h_0$ (often at a long-run level or estimated as an intercept nuisance). Here $h_0\in[0,\infty)$ is part of the 4-dim parameter with its own deficiency theory. The equivalence statements in Proposition 2.1 are therefore about a problem with an extra scalar; the fixed-point set $\Theta^e=\{h_0\alpha=\beta\}$ that arises is **specific to the $h_0$-as-parameter convention** — a different convention would produce a different exceptional set, and the "genericity" statement would be different too.

12. **Time horizon $[0,1]$ fixed.** All theorems are stated on $[0,1]$. There is no discussion of behaviour on $[0,T]$ for $T\ne 1$ (a rescaling would absorb $T$ into $\gamma$ and $\alpha$, which is likely harmless but is not stated).

13. **i.i.d. innovations with constant (or $n$-indexed) $Q_n$.** No dependence between $Z_{n,k}$, no regime-switching, no conditional heteroskedasticity beyond the GARCH recursion itself, no time-varying thinning rate. Real financial data have all of these to varying degrees.

14. **Pathwise uniqueness of the (M)COGARCH SDEs.** Taken for granted; the integral-equation formulation is presented as the "unique pathwise solution" without proof of existence/uniqueness. The paper builds on Kallsen–Vesenmayer / Maller for this, but the statement is repeated as if trivial. For a self-contained reproduction this is a non-trivial piece to verify.

15. **Wilcoxon test is a test of medians, not of deficiency directly.** The paper's numerical claim "GARCH and COGARCH are not equivalent" is supported by: (a) a theoretical non-equivalence theorem under extra assumptions, (b) a simulation of the likelihood-ratio distributions under both models, and (c) a two-sample Wilcoxon rank-sum test on the **medians** of those two LR distributions. A rank-sum test of medians at $N=10^6$ is **powerless to distinguish distributions that are very close but not equal**, and **overpowered to distinguish distributions that differ only in the extreme tails** — which is not exactly the deficiency question. A more direct diagnostic (e.g., Hellinger-affinity estimator between the two samples, or a two-sample KS/QQ) would have been more on-point. The result "p→0 as $N\to\infty$" is also expected under **any** fixed alternative, however small — so it confirms non-equivalence *in principle*, not the **magnitude** of the difference.

16. **The simulation restricts to one fixed $\gamma=4$ and specific parameter values.** It does not explore the boundary of the genericity (i.e., $\theta$ near $\Theta^e$), and the conclusion "Theorem 2.3 holds for quite arbitrary jump distributions" is an extrapolation to distributions not even tested. Three distributions and eight parameter pairs is suggestive, not a verification.

17. **The equivalence of $\mathsf E^\star_{1,n}$ and $\mathsf E^\star_{2,n}$ in Theorem 2.1** rests on the "randomization of the time grid is deficiency-invisible" argument, which is correct in the *incomplete observation* case because the jump *sizes* $\{Z_{n,k}\}$ and the *count* $\sum 1_{Z_{n,k}\ne0}$ are the same in both experiments and the jump *location* is not observed in the incomplete case. This argument **would not** work in the complete observation case (where $h$ is observable and carries information about the time location), and indeed in §2.4 the complete-observation proof takes a completely different route (via the reference experiment $F$). The fact that the "deficiency-invisibility" of the time grid is **specifically tied to the observation scheme** is a subtle piece of the argument that the reader must not conflate with a general invariance principle.

18. **Hellinger-transformation equality is a necessary and sufficient condition for equivalence only for dominated experiments** (Strasser's characterization). This is the standard result the paper invokes (Strasser 2000, Corollary 53.8). For the **incomplete observation** case, the reduction to $F_n$ via (A.2) — which uses the fact that the jump times of $G$ equal the jump times of $N$ — is the key step that **forces domination** and hence allows the Hellinger calculation. A reader applying the technique to a setting where the jump times are not observable (e.g., a censored jump model) cannot directly reuse this reduction.

19. **No error bars or variance estimates on the Monte Carlo simulation.** Tables 2 and 3 report quantiles and p-values but no standard errors of the Wilcoxon test at $N=10^4, 10^5, 10^6$ (the p-value is a continuous test statistic so SE is implicit, but no confidence interval on the median difference is given). The monotone decline of the p-value with $N$ looks consistent with a real effect, but a 95% CI on the median difference would have strengthened the empirical claim.

20. **No sensitivity to numerical artifacts.** The recursion (6.1) for $h_{d,w,\theta,k}$ is a **product of $d$ terms** $(1+\lambda z_{k-1}^2)$; for $d$ in the hundreds and $\lambda>0$, this can overflow in double precision for Cauchy innovations (unbounded $z$). The paper does not mention any rescaling, log-space, or arbitrary-precision handling, nor the precision of the Wilcoxon test statistic for $10^6$ paired samples (the test statistic's integer count $U$ may hit the limits of double-precision exactly at the reported magnitudes).

21. **Definition of "convergence in deficiency" for non-dominated experiments** relies on Strasser's (2000) formal definition rather than Le Cam's original. In particular, the $\delta$-distance is only a **pseudo-metric** for non-dominated experiments and its zero-set is exactly statistical equivalence — so Theorem 2.3's conclusion ("not equivalent in deficiency") is actually a **deficiency-distance $>0$** claim plus a structural characterization of when the distance is zero. The paper's phrasing "we do not have equivalence in deficiency" is the strong claim, but the **quantitative** content (the actual $\delta$-distance as a function of $\Theta$) is not computed; the reader gets the dichotomy but not the magnitude.

## 3. Critical assessment

**Strengths.**
- The identification of **MCOGARCH** as the correct deficiency-limit under incomplete observation is the paper's main contribution and is genuinely novel: it corrects the prior "GARCH→COGARCH" statements of Kallsen–Vesenmayer and Maller et al., which are in law only, and shows that the in-law limit need not be the in-deficiency limit. This is the kind of subtle phenomenon that justifies an entire Le-Cam-theoretic treatment.
- The complete-observation positive result (Propositions 2.1–2.3) is clean: the reduction to a **Bernoulli mixture on the 4-dim parameter space** driven by the no-jump event mass $e^{-\gamma}$ is elegant and gives an explicit, checkable equivalence.
- The simulation, while not a substitute for the theorem, is well-designed: the choice to use **three** jump distributions (two within the theorem, one outside it) is a reasonable sensitivity test, and the Wilcoxon trend across $N$ is informative.
- The proof structure (Theorem 2.1 as a three-step sandwich through $\mathsf E^\star_{1,n}$ and $\mathsf E^\star_{2,n}$) is modular and reproducible.

**Weaknesses.**
- The **big-picture claim in the abstract** ("COGARCH can indeed be considered as a continuous-time equivalent to GARCH") is conditional on **complete observation**, which the authors themselves concedes is "mainly of theoretical interest". The *practically relevant* incomplete-observation case is one in which COGARCH and GARCH converge to **different** limit experiments, so the abstract overstates the practical takeaway.
- The **restriction to compound Poisson** is large and is stated as such only in the §3 Conclusion. A reader who comes to the paper expecting "GARCH ↔ COGARCH" in general will be surprised to learn that the results are only for one special subclass of COGARCH.
- The **monotonicity condition of Theorem 2.3** is verified for three families only, and there is no structural insight (e.g., a necessary-and-sufficient condition on $f$) — the theorem is "if $f$ has property $P$, then non-equivalence" with $P$ neither necessary nor characterized. The paper's conjecture from the simulation that the result is much more general is plausible but unproved.
- The **simulation is a test of medians of likelihood ratios, not of the deficiency distance** — it confirms non-equivalence *in principle* under the specific alternatives tested but does not quantify the size of the non-equivalence (e.g., the actual Hellinger distance between the two experiments).
- The **Q({0})=0 assumption** left "to the reader" (Remark 2.2) is a real gap in a finance paper, since micro-structure data almost always have mass at zero in the per-tick return distribution.
- No **error bars, no confidence intervals on the median difference, no precision analysis** of the simulation. The paper reports p-values but not effect sizes in a principled way.

**Overall.** The technical contribution is real and interesting: identifying MCOGARCH as the correct limit experiment, proving non-equivalence to COGARCH under a (partially vacuous) assumption, and proving equivalence in the fully-observed case are all genuine mathematical results of the kind that Le Cam-theoretic papers in statistics are supposed to produce. ## Understanding of the logical argument

**Problem.** GARCH is the dominant discrete-time heteroskedastic time-series model. Two continuous-time limits are known in distribution as n → ∞ under a "law of rare events" (random thinning) scheme: Nelson's bivariate diffusion (from *aggregated* innovations) and COGARCH (from *thinned* innovations). Wang showed that Nelson's diffusion limit is **not** valid in Le Cam's stronger sense of statistical deficiency: GARCH is driven by 1-d innovations, whereas the diffusion is driven by 2-d Brownian motion. So "converges in distribution" does not justify the statistical passage to the limit.

**Question the paper answers.** Does COGARCH, driven by a 1-d compound Poisson, *also* fail to be the statistical limit of GARCH? More precisely: in Le Cam's deficiency framework do the GARCH experiments En and the candidate continuous-time experiments (COGARCH E or MCOGARCH Ê) converge to the same limit?

**Method and structure.**

1. **Set-up (Section 2.1).** Discrete experiments En(Θ) = (Rⁿ, B, Lθ(Gn)θ∈Θ) and continuous experiments E (COGARCH, driven by a Poisson point measure N with intensity γℓ⊗Q) and Ê (MCOGARCH, where the *drift* integral in the volatility equation is integrated against a **randomized time-changed** Poisson measure T_N rather than the Lebesgue measure; jump arrivals are no longer uniformly distributed because they inherit the random thinning grid structure). Two sampling schemes: incomplete observations (only (G, G_n)) and complete observations (both G and h).

2. **Theorem 2.1 (convergence to MCOGARCH).** Under the canonical parametrization H^(0), the discrete GARCH experiments converge **strongly in deficiency** to MCOGARCH. Proof strategy: En ≜ E★₁,n ≜ E★₂,n, and E★₂,n → Ê in deficiency (via Lemma 4.1, a Hellinger bound on randomized grid-thinned Poisson measures).

3. **Theorem 2.2 (uniformity of parametrizations).** For a broad class of parametrizations Hn satisfying natural rate conditions — including the Kallsen–Vesenmayer and Maller–et al. parametrizations that they show converge to *COGARCH* in distribution — the discrete experiments are still (strongly) convergent to *MCOGARCH*, not COGARCH, in deficiency. This is the central counterintuitive result: the in-distribution continuous-time limit (COGARCH) is **not** the in-deficiency limit (MCOGARCH); what is "seen" depends on the kind of limit being asked for.

4. **Theorem 2.3 (non-equivalence of E and Ê in deficiency).** When observations are incomplete, for a broad class of innovations (positive Lebesgue density f with a monotonicity property on the Hellinger scaling transform g_{f,ζ}), the experiments E_{γn,Q}(Θ) and Ê_{γn,Q}(Θ) are **not** equivalent in deficiency unless Θ is severely restricted (essentially to lower-dimensional slivers of the parameter space — pinned (h₀, β) pairs, or pinned α, or restricted h₀). # Peer review: "Limit experiments of GARCH" (Buchmann & Müller, Bernoulli 18(1):64–99, 2012; arXiv:1203.0186v1)

## Scope and motivation

The paper addresses a precise and important theoretical question: when GARCH(1,1) with randomly thinned innovations is viewed as a sequence of statistical experiments, which continuous-time experiment is it asymptotically equivalent to in the sense of Le Cam deficiency? Prior work (Kallsen–Vesenmayer; Maller, Müller–Szimayer) showed convergence *in distribution* to COGARCH, and Wang (2002) had shown that Nelson's diffusion limit of GARCH is *not* equivalent in deficiency. The gap this paper fills is to establish the correct limiting experiment statistically, and to compare it to COGARCH.

## Contributions

1. **MCOGARCH (Theorem 2.1).** The authors discover that, under incomplete observations (volatilities unobserved), the actual limit experiment in deficiency is not COGARCH itself but a "modified" process (G̃, ĥ) in which the mean-reversion clock is a stretched-exponential time change T of the jump counting process (eqs. 2.5–2.6). This is the genuinely new mathematical object in the paper: the discrete-time grid "remembers itself" through the geometrically distributed (per cell) jump locations, and the limit preserves this structure. The theorem is strong: convergence holds for a general limit jump law Q with Qn → Q in total variation, for one clean parametrization.

2. **Robustness to parametrization (Theorem 2.2).** Under compactness of the parameter set, Q ≪ ℓ, and mild regularity (Lipschitz density with compact support in the proof; uniform O(1/n) closeness to the reference parametrization), the Kallsen–Vesenmayer and Maller et al. parametrizations also converge in deficiency to MCOGARCH. The proof machinery (Lemmas 5.1–5.2, tracking the volatility recursion jump-to-jump via the σ-index sets M_{n,k} and change-of-variables densities f̃) is delicate and, to my reading, competently carried out: the induction bounds (5.15)–(5.17), the C^k/n error accumulation in Lemma 5.2, and the final summation over kN_n·k = k all cohere.

3. **Non-equivalence of COGARCH and MCOGARCH (Theorem 2.3 + simulation).** This is the substantive negative result: equivalence can only hold on low-dimensional parameter sets (the Hellinger-ζ monotonicity condition on the jump density restricts (i)–(v) severely), so generically the two experiments are *not* equivalent for incomplete observations — a contrast with the diffusion case where the failure came from the driving-noise dimensionality; here the failure comes from the localization of jump times. The authors honestly flag that the theorem is conditional (requires monotonicity of g_{f,ζ}) and supplement it with simulations: 8 one-coordinate parameter contrasts × 3 jump laws (N(0,1), Cauchy(0,1), a symmetric normal mixture with mean 0), 10^6 samples per cell, comparing the two likelihood-ratio distributions (2.20) via Wilcoxon tests with p → 0 as sample size grows. For the mixture case — outside the theorem's coverage — this is the only evidence for non-equivalence, which is a limitation of the generality of the claim.

4. **Complete observations (Section 2.4).** When (G, h) are fully observed, everything reduces to a trivial finite-point experiment F (Proposition 2.1), because the deterministic affine flow between jumps encodes the parameters in derivatives (with the fixed-point set Θe = {h0α = β} as the exception). The careful handling of the exceptional set Θe and the discrete analogues Θe,n, culminating in the exact δ-zero / positive-deficiency characterizations of Proposition 2.3, gives the "generic asymptotic equivalence" headline result: for a large class of parameter sets, GARCH is asymptotically equivalent in deficiency to (M)COGARCH. The proof in 7.2 (construction of the explicit statistics Xn and kernels K1,n, K2,n with q1,n, q2,n error bookkeeping) is intricate but appears correct.

## Strengths

- The paper is squarely on a live theoretical question and completes the picture left by Kallsen–Vesenmayer and Maller et al. at the level of statistical equivalence, not just in-distribution convergence. The conceptual finding that the "right" limit experiment is MCOGARCH rather than COGARCH under realistic incomplete observations is a real (if negative-leaning) contribution, and the authors state the practical reading honestly: the price difference is the randomness/precision of jump-time localization.
- Full proofs are included for all main statements; the argument chains (in particular the three-experiment sandwich E → E*_{1,n} ≍ E*_{2,n} → Ê in the proof of Theorem 2.1, and the Hellinger-distance decomposition in Lemma 4.1) are well-organized and verifiable.
- Good situating in the literature (Le Cam, Strasser; Wang; Brown–Low, Nussbaum, Millstein–Nussbaum for the aggregated-innovations contrast; the peak-over-threshold connection in Remark 2.1(ii) is a useful aside).
- The discussion of practical paths around volatility unobservability (realized-vol-type estimators, Hubalek–Posedel's trading-intensity reinterpretation) gives the theory a handle on the real world, even if the authors themselves concede the complete-observation result is "mainly of theoretical interest."

## Weaknesses

- **Generality is bounded, and honestly so.** Only compound-Poisson driving processes are treated; the general Lévy case is declared out of reach. Q ≪ ℓ is required for the parametrization-robustness result (Theorem 2.2), and Theorem 2.3 needs the monotonicity-of-g condition; together, the *unconditional* non-equivalence claim rests on simulation in general cases. A rigorous non-equivalence theorem without the monotonicity assumption (e.g., via Hellinger bounds uniform in the parameter for a broader class of two-point subfamilies) would have strengthened the paper materially.
- **The positive (equivalence) headline requires volatility observability**, which the authors themselves note is largely of theoretical interest; in practice MCOGARCH ≠ COGARCH, so the practical message leans negative: plugging GARCH estimates into COGARCH is not justified in deficiency for partial observations. This is an interesting but not empowering conclusion, and the paper does little (no quantitative error rates, no finite-sample guidance, no estimation procedure) with it beyond suggesting that "practitioners may see" more random jump times as an advantage.
- **The Monte Carlo section is thin as statistics.** The tests are median-comparison Wilcoxon tests on likelihood-ratio samples; p-values reported as 0.0000 are uninformative (they round), no effect sizes beyond quantile tables (Table 2 is hard to read in print layout), and the choice of 8 one-coordinate contrasts is arbitrary. It is suggestive, not persuasive, for the mixture case — appropriate as an appendix-style illustration, but it carries the burden of evidence where the theory does not reach.
- **Minor technical/typographical issues** (none affecting validity, but typical of the genre): line ~37/34 "times series"; reference [24] "Asymtotic"; Table 3 caption "corresonding"; in Section 4.3 (around eq. 4.11) the parameter vector is written θ = (h0, β, α, γ) where λ is meant; Lemma 5.1's constant C is named C(S, θ) in the proof but C(S, Θ) in the statement. The notation E_θ(X) / L_θ(X) is standard but the paper's heavy use of θ-ambiguity ("θ = (h0, β, α, γ)") is a source of confusion.

A solid, rigorous theoretical paper in the Le Cam-equivalence theory of time-series limits at a top probability journal — which it deservedly appeared in. Its core insight (MCOGARCH as the correct limit experiment, with generic non-equivalence to COGARCH under realistic partial observations) is a real and novel result, and the complete-observation equivalence characterization is cleanly executed. Its limitations — compound-Poisson-only scope, conditional non-equivalence theorem, thin simulation backup, limited practical pay-off — keep it firmly in the "important for the subfield" rather than "field-shaping" category.

Theorem 2.1 shows the randomly‑thinned GARCH experiment converges in deficiency to MCOGARCH, provided $Q_n\to Q$ in total variation.
