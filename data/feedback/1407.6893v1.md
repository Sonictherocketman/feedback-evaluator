## [1407.6893v1-general-review] General Review


# Review: "Spectral self-action of THz emission from ionizing two-color laser pulses in gases" (Cabrera-Granado et al., arXiv:1407.6893v1)

## Summary of the argument

The paper studies terahertz (THz) generation by tunneling photoionization with two-color (ω + 2ω) femtosecond pulses in gases. Starting from a semi-analytic model built on the local-current approximation — stepwise tunneling ionization (SRK) giving a piecewise-constant plasma density, and a drift equation for the plasma current — the authors derive a closed-form expression for the current as a sum over ionization events. Their central thesis: the THz field already emitted in the channel co-propagates with and drives the free electrons born later, thereby feeding back on the very process that generates the THz radiation — a "spectral self-action" that makes THz generation inherently nonlocal along the propagation direction and produces strong low-frequency broadbanding. They support this with (i) a simple "perturb the pump with a 2% 50-THz component" semi-analytic demonstration, (ii) an A(ω)/B(ω) decomposition of the emitted spectrum isolating the trajectory-feedback channel, and (iii) 1D and 2D FDTD simulations. In 2D they further argue that diffraction of the low-frequency components out of the plasma channel truncates the low end of the spectrum and thereby sets the achievable bandwidth.

## Strengths

1. **A genuinely interesting physical mechanism.** The idea that a 2% (of the fundamental) low-frequency field, simply because it is long-lived in time and co-propagates with the electrons, dominates the low-frequency secondary spectrum (Figs. 2, 3) is a clean and persuasive demonstration of nonlinearity/nonlocality in the process. The phase scan (Fig. 2d,f) showing the feedback works at almost all two-color phases — not only at the optimal φ = π/2 — is a nice generalizing result.

2. **The A/B decomposition is the conceptual core and it is well chosen.** B(ω) contains only the initial velocities at ionization, so the low-frequency spectrum of B fixes the "standard" low-frequency content; the appearance of A(ω) — containing the full free-electron velocity history — as the carrier of the feedback (Figs. 3, 4c,f,i) gives a transparent mechanistic explanation rather than a black-box FDTD result. The phase-space plot (Fig. 3c) is excellent visual evidence.

3. **The control experiments in the FDTD are clever.** Recording the propagating spectrum and comparing it with the "local" secondary emission computed from a pump whose <200 THz part is removed (Figs. 4b,h; 5b,d,f) cleanly separates local from nonlocal contributions and shows the propagating spectrum exceeding the local one only after substantial propagation — exactly the signature of a nonlocal feedforward mechanism.

4. **The 2D diffraction result is a useful and somewhat novel point.** That the low-frequency field diffracts away from the plasma channel (u(ω) ∝ 1/ω; Fig. 6) and thereby self-limits the interaction length of the feedback is a physically sensible, quantitatively useful insight for bandwidth engineering.

## Weaknesses and specific critique

**Reproducibility / numerical method (most serious gap).**
- The FDTD section (Sec. 4) is underspecified to the point of non-reproducibility. No grid resolution, timestep, Courant number, spatial extent, PML boundary parameters, or verification against the continuum are given. For fields up to ~1 PHz (λ ≈ 0.3 µm in argon), grid dispersion is a real error source and must be discussed.
- More fundamentally, **it is never stated which kinetic/fluid model the FDTD solves for the plasma.** The semi-analytic model tracks one mean electron velocity; does the FDTD solve the same fluid drift equation (Eq. 3) at each grid point, or something else? What is the "nonlinear generalization of FDTD" ([19], [20] cited here are an odd combination — Taflove is the standard linear-FDTD text, and ref. [20] is about dissipative solitons)? A reader cannot reproduce these numbers from the paper. This should be an entire paragraph plus a table of parameters.

**Model validity.**
- The single-ionization (SRK) premise is used in 2D runs with 47 GV/m on-axis peaks; the authors themselves flag >50 GV/m as the single-ionization limit. That's borderline enough that a brief multiphoton-check or sensitivity statement is owed.
- Several free parameters have no sensitivity analysis: the phenomenological collision rate γ = 7.7 ps⁻¹, the ionization-event width τ = 0.2 fs, and the gas density. Since the *magnitude* of the broadening (and even whether self-action is visible at all, cf. the 37 GV/m negative control in 2D) plausibly depends on these, a small parameter-study figure would materially strengthen the central claim.
- Eq. (6), E_J(ω) = gωJ(ω), rests on "g is a constant" — this is an effective local-field prescription whose derivation (dipole/near-field scaling of the current field on axis) should be stated, because the whole "secondary radiation" quantity ν|J(ν)| is interpreted physically throughout the paper.
- The statement that ρ(t) ≈ ρ̃(t) and J(t) ≈ J̃(t) hold "throughout the whole analysis ... [which] were always close to equality" is asserted but never quantified; the Fig. 1 overlays do not cover the feedback configurations, where the approximation matters most (a stronger co-propagating field makes the local approximation less safe).

**Scope of the claims.**
- In 1D, they themselves note the broadening/shift of the low-frequency maximum was reported before in PRL 105, 053903 (ref. [16]) and Babushkin et al. OPA (ref. [22]). The novelty is the attribution to trajectory feedback, which is well argued — but the abstract/lead should be more careful to distinguish "reporting a new effect" from "identifying the mechanism of a known effect," since that distinction is what makes or breaks the contribution.
- The closing speculation that generated THz radiation may alter electron trajectories on HHG timescales (via Fig. 3c) is, at 2% field amplitude, not a serious dynamic effect in actual HHG conditions (where the THz is many orders below the driving field) and risks overclaiming. It would be better stated as an observational note.

**Experiment.**
- Everything is simulation. For a mechanism paper this is acceptable, but the mechanism (THz feedback on drift) is in principle testable — e.g., by measuring the low-frequency spectral width vs. gas pressure/path length, or with gated two-color pulses that suppress co-propagation. A short discussion of a discriminating experiment would considerably raise the paper's standing.

**Minor.**
- Fig. 4's cutoff at "200 THz" for the pump suppression test is arbitrary and its insensitivity to the cutoff value should be checked or stated.
- The 2% 50-THz/15 fs pump component is a proof-of-principle perturbation; a sentence noting that realistic THz seed levels are well below this (which is why the 37 GV/m 2D case is "negative") would help readers gauge the effect's practical reach.
- Some figures conflate the *effective* secondary-field quantity gωJ(ω) with a physical field; consistent caption language would help.
- Ref. [21] (a 1960 infrared index measurement) as the basis for the THz dispersion of argon is dated; the relevant THz refractive index of argon should be discussed.

## Verdict (summary)

The paper puts forward a well-motivated and largely convincing identification of a specific feedback mechanism — co-propagating THz radiation driving the free electrons that subsequently carry the current — for the broadband low-frequency content of two-color ionization THz emission, with a clean analytic A/B decomposition backed by FDTD in two geometries and a useful diffraction-limited-bandwidth result for 2D. Its main deficiencies are methodological rather than conceptual: the FDTD setup and plasma-kinetics model are insufficiently documented for independent reproduction, key phenomenological parameters are unvaried, the effective-field prescription of Eq. (6) is underived, and there is no experimental comparison. As a simulation/mechanism contribution submitted to a major journal, it is solid but not yet fully rigorous.

**Recommendation (as framed by this question):** accept the physical argument as plausible and well-evidenced by the internal controls of the paper, with revisions required on FDTD documentation, parameter sensitivity, and restraint in the experimental/HHG claims.

## Summary

The work studies how the low‑frequency (THz) part of a two‑color (ω + 2ω) ionizing femtosecond pulse broadens as it propagates through a gas. The authors (i) build a semi‑analytic model from the local‑current picture, summing over discrete ionization events (Eqs. 2–5), and (ii) run 1D and 2D FDTD simulations. The central claim is a **THz spectral self‑action**: the THz field already radiated co‑propagates with the remaining pump, is felt by electrons born at *later* ionization events, and thereby reshapes their trajectories and the subsequently emitted low‑frequency spectrum. The key analytic device is the decomposition of the secondary field into two terms, A(ω) (driven by the instantaneous velocity v_f(t), Eqs. 9) and B(ω) (driven only by the birth velocities v_f(t_n), Eqs. 10). B is essentially the local‑current model of the authors' 2011 NJP paper [15]; A is the "feedback" contribution, which only activates in the THz range once a low‑frequency component is present in the total field. They demonstrate the effect by adding an external THz component (2 % of the fundamental, centered at 50 THz) and showing it relocates the low‑frequency spectral peak across almost all pump phases, then confirm it with 1D propagation (400 μm, Ar; the <100 THz peak shifts upward with distance) and with 2D focused beams, where beyond focus strong defocusing of the THz relative to the IR lets the low‑frequency field "leave the plasma channel" and arrest the effect. They conclude that **diffraction, not self‑action, ultimately sets the achievable bandwidth.**

Overall: a clean, internally consistent, well‑executed theory paper with a genuinely useful analytical lens (the A/B split), validated against FDTD. My reservations are mostly about quantification, the tension between the abstract's framing and the 2D result, and the lack of observational/experimental grounding.

## What I think works well

- **The A(ω)/B(ω) decomposition is the best part of the paper.** It gives a physically transparent way to separate "standard local current" (birth‑velocity) emission from the part in which the electric field — including the self‑generated THz — modifies the plasma current at later times. Fig. 3 (especially the A/B traces and the electron phase‑space portrait in 3c) makes the mechanism legible. This is a nice incremental advance over [15] and it does real work explaining the results rather than just fitting them.
- **The model is simple and self‑consistent.** Stepwise sigmoid plasma (Eq. 2) → closed form for J(t) (Eq. 4) matches the numerically integrated Eqs. 1,3 very well (Fig. 1 red dashed lines). For the intended diagnostic purpose this approximation is well justified.
- **Three levels of evidence (semi‑analytic, 1D FDTD, 2D FDTD)** is proportionate to the scope. Using a suppressed‑pump control (<200 THz removed) and an external THz kicker to isolate the effect (Fig. 4, Fig. 2) is a reasonable experimental design for a numerical study.
- The 2D threshold observation — self‑action visible at 47 GV/m at focus but absent at ~37 GV/m — is interesting and is the most physically suggestive result in the paper, even though it's under‑developed.

## Main concerns

1. **The headline is somewhat undermined by the authors' own 2D result, and the paper doesn't fully reconcile the two.** The abstract says self‑action "plays a key role in the observed strong spectral broadening," but the realistic, focused 2D case (the geometry that actually matches experiments) shows the effect is *weaker* and gets arrested past focus because the THz delocalizes relative to the IR, and the bandwidth is ultimately limited by **diffraction**. So there's real tension between the "key player" framing and the finding that, in the regime that matters, self‑action is intensity‑gated (disappears near ~37 GV/m) and geometry‑arrested. The conclusion should state plainly: in a focused beam, self‑action is a secondary effect that operates only over part of the trajectory and above an intensity threshold, while diffraction sets the terminal bandwidth. Right now the abstract over‑claims relative to §4.

2. **Almost everything is qualitative.** "Significantly," "strong," "considerably," "more pronounced" appear repeatedly, but I get very few hard numbers. How much does self‑action actually broaden the spectrum — a 3 dB or 10 dB bandwidth in THz, before/after, as a function of propagation distance, plasma density, and intensity? What is the magnitude of the peak shift in Fig. 4? A table or a couple of explicit statements (e.g., "self‑action shifts the 50 THz peak by X THz over Y μm; the low‑frequency bandwidth increases by Z %") would substantially strengthen the paper and make the claimed importance testable. As written, a reader cannot gauge whether this effect is *small but real* or *dramatic* until they trust the word "strong."

3. **The effect is isolated only artificially, and it's unclear how it would be observed for real.** The 1D/2D "self‑action" is established by (a) adding an external THz kicker, or (b) suppressing the THz band in a post‑processed local calculation. In actual propagation, self‑action coexists with other propagation effects (pump self‑steepening/broadening, ionization‑induced changes — which the authors themselves flag, citing [7,16]) that also reshape the low‑frequency spectrum. The paper never proposes a way to *distinguish* self‑action from those in real data (a gas‑path‑length or pressure or focus scan, say, with a predicted diagnostic signature). The kicker is a great internal proof of existence, but it's not something you'd do experimentally. Without an observability story, the practical relevance stays open. A short "how one would confirm this in an experiment" paragraph is missing and would be cheap to add.

4. **Model assumptions deserve more honest bounding.** The model drops recollisions, ponderomotive forces, and quiver motion, fixes a single phenomenological drag γ = 7.7 ps⁻¹ (not tied to the gas pressure/density actually used), fixes the ionization‑event sigmoid width τ = 0.2 fs, and uses a single‑ionization picture. Some of this is fine at the low densities in play (ρ/ρ_nat ~ 0.08 in Fig. 1d), but the authors should at least state the validity range: at what γ/ρ and what intensity does the single‑collision, zero‑birth‑velocity picture break down? The 2D note that "at >50 GV/m… the self‑action is more pronounced, however our model only becomes questionable in this high‑intensity regime" is the right instinct — but it means the model can't be trusted exactly where the effect would be *strongest*, which is a notable limitation that should be foregrounded, not footnoted.

5. **The "inherently nonlocal" framing is loose.** What's actually being shown is a *temporal/causal* feedback (earlier‑radiated THz acts on later‑born electrons), and A vs B is a bookkeeping partition of E(t)-driven vs birth‑velocity‑driven response. Calling the process "nonlocal" invites the (standard) spatial‑nonlocality reading, but the split is partly definitional. One sentence clarifying that A(ω) is "the part of the field‑driven velocity response beyond the birth‑velocity (local‑current) term" would prevent misreading.

6. **The 1D propagation length (400 μm) is short** relative to cm‑scale air filaments that dominate experiments. The paper shows the shift growing out to 100 μm and gestures at "larger propagation distances," but doesn't actually push the 1D case to the point where saturation/competition with the other effects is clear. A longer 1D run with the full un‑suppressed pump would help show where self‑action stops being the dominant low‑frequency shaper.

## Minor points

- The phase‑scan in Fig. 2c says the THz kicker "increases THz yield considerably for almost all φ" and attributes the kink at φ = 3π/2 to "exceptionally low THz yield" there — that's asserted, not explained. A mechanistic sentence (which term, A or B, responds how as φ varies) would close it.
- The constant g in Eq. 6 is left as "a constant [18]." Fine, but a one‑line note on the geometry (near‑field/short‑wave radiation factor it encapsulates) would help readers reproduce it.
- The electron‑initial‑velocity = 0 assumption and its interaction with the drag term are worth one more sentence on why the error is small for these multi‑cycle pumps (they cite the multi‑cycle footnote, which is good; make it a bit more explicit).
- Figures 1 and 2 are clean; Fig. 4's three‑column layout is busy — the <200 THz comparison is the crucial content and arguably deserves being front‑and‑center with the suppressed‑control.
- No experimental data of any kind; for New J. Phys. this is acceptable, but it's what keeps the paper on the theoretical side of the ledger.

## Verdict

This is a solid, publishable physics paper: the A/B decomposition is a genuinely useful analytical tool, the model is simple and validated, and the 1D evidence for THz feedback on later‑born electrons is convincing. Its limitations are: qualitative treatment (few numbers), a headline that the authors' own realistic 2D case softens (diffraction and intensity thresholds actually dominate), isolation of the effect only via artificial diagnostics with no experimental observability plan, and model assumptions that weaken exactly where the effect would be strongest. I'd support publication in New J. Phys. **with revisions** that (1) quantify the bandwidth/peak‑shift effects, (2) reconcile the "key role" language of the abstract with the 2D diffraction result, and (3) add a concrete proposal for how the effect could be distinguished experimentally from the other propagation effects it coexists with. As it stands it reads more as "a real but secondary and regime‑restricted effect, identified with a nice model" than as "the dominant mechanism for broadband THz generation."

The intellectual architecture is the paper's strongest asset. Cross-referencing is generous and well placed, and each section's purpose is signposted. The introduction builds motivation without overreaching, and the conclusion lands with a nice forward link to high-harmonic generation. ## [1407.6893v1-critique] Critique


# Critique: "Spectral self-action of THz emission from ionizing two-color laser pulses in gases" (Cabrera-Granado, Chen, Babushkin, Bergé, Skupin; arXiv:1407.6893, submitted to NJP)

## Summary of the work

The paper argues that in ionizing two-color (800 nm + 400 nm, ~30 GV/m multi-cycle) femtosecond pulses in gases, the THz radiation that is already generated acts back on free electrons born later in the plasma channel, coherently modifying their acceleration. This "spectral self-action" makes THz generation nonlocal along the propagation direction. The authors support the claim with (i) a semi-analytic local-current extension of Babushkin et al. (2011, ref. [15]) in which the secondary spectrum E_J(ω) ≈ gω[A(ω) − B(ω)] is decomposed into A(ω) — depending on the *current* free-electron velocity v_f(t), and therefore sensitive to any low-frequency field present — and B(ω), which depends only on v_f at the ionization times (hence "frozen"); (ii) an FDTD benchmark in which adding a mere 2%-amplitude, 50-THz component to the pump strongly reshapes the low-frequency secondary spectrum; and (iii) 1D and 2D propagation simulations showing low-frequency spectral broadening, an upshift of the sub-100-THz peak with propagation distance, and — in the focused 2D case — a bandwidth cap set by differential diffraction of the THz versus IR beams.

## Strengths

1. **Genuine mechanistic insight.** The attribution of the previously observed red→blue shift of the THz quasi-DC component with propagation distance (refs. [16, 22]) to a *specific* feedback channel (the ωA(ω) term, i.e., E_low acting on v_f(t)) is the paper's real contribution. It is more concrete than the vague "propagation effects modify the spectra" that accompanied earlier observations.
2. **Clean diagnostic decomposition.** Splitting the local secondary emission into A(ω) and B(ω) (Fig. 3, Figs. 4c,f,i) is a genuinely useful tool: it separates the "live-trajectory" (field-sensitive) part from the "frozen ionization-history" part, and the suppression test (deleting the <200 THz part of the pump) directly demonstrates which term carries the feedback. The agreement between the dashed (model) and solid (FDTD) curves throughout Figs. 3–4 is convincing at the qualitative-and-semi-quantitative level the paper claims.
3. **Consistent 1D/2D comparison.** Showing that the effect weakens in focused 2D geometry and that diffraction, not just ionization, depletes the on-axis low-frequency field (Fig. 6) is the physically important negative result: it explains why the strong self-action seen in 1D does not translate unlimitedly into broader spectra in real beams.
4. **Clear, honest limitations.** The authors explicitly state where their single-ionization model breaks down (>50 GV/m at focus), where other effects (pump spectral broadening, plasma oscillations) may take over at long distances, and that the 2D effect is weaker than 1D.

## Weaknesses

### Scientific / methodological

1. **The central claim rests on an external-field proxy.** The striking Fig. 2 result is produced by *injecting* a 50 THz wave (2% of the fundamental, 15 fs) into the pump. The paper then argues the "real" self-action is the same because in the FDTD runs the THz builds up spontaneously. This inference is reasonable but a step weaker than it appears: a 2% *coherent, phase-correlated, externally driven* field is not quantitatively identical to the self-generated broadband, phase-diffusing THz field. A more direct test would be, e.g., a comparison of the sub-100 THz local spectrum at fixed z with vs. without the back-action of the generated THz field on v_f(t) in the FDTD itself (i.e., a "turn off the THz→electron coupling" counterfactual run), rather than relying on the A/B decomposition computed from the *pump* field only. As presented, the self-consistent feedback strength is never quantified (e.g., as a percentage shift of the sub-100 THz peak attributable specifically to self-action versus to pump reshaping).
2. **The "hence nonlocal" framing overstates the model.** Eq. (6), E_J(ω) = gωJ(ω), is still a *local* current-to-field relation; the nonlocality enters only insofar as the v_f(t) used in A(ω) is itself modified by a previously generated field. Calling the process "inherently nonlocal" in the abstract is defensible rhetorically but should be qualified: what is nonlocal-in-propagation is the *source term*, which is standard in any self-consistent source-field simulation.
3. **Single parameter set, no robustness study.** Every result uses one pump (E₀=31 GV/m IR, r=0.44, τ_p=24 fs, argon, one density), one collision rate γ = 7.7 ps⁻¹ (introduced without justification or sensitivity analysis), and (for 2D, Fig. 5) one focus length. The qualitative claim that self-action is generic for "relatively narrow IR pump spectra" would be much stronger with even a short scan over r, τ_p, gas density, or γ. The phase scan (Fig. 2d,f) is the only parameter study, and it concerns the auxiliary added field.
4. **The regime where the effect would matter most is excluded.** The authors note that at larger distances the pump's own IR/visible spectral broadening "may become dominant…(not shown)," and that at >50 GV/m focus the model is questionable because of multi-ionization. So the effect is demonstrated only in a moderate-intensity, moderate-length corner of parameter space, and the interesting regimes (high intensity, long gas cells, multi-ionization) are waved away. A single multi-ionization or ADK-rate variant, or even a discussion of how tunneling-rate dependence (Eq. 1 uses W_ST(E)) already partially accounts for field-history, would have tightened the claim.
5. **No comparison with experiment.** The paper is entirely theoretical, yet it explicitly aims to "shed light on the interpretation of recent experiments on HHG with two-color pulses [23]" and prior broadband-THz observations [16,22]. At minimum, the predicted spectral broadening/upshift should be plotted against (or compared with) the spectra in [16,22,11]; as it stands the "implication" for HHG (ref. [23]) is speculative: "the similar pump configuration" is, honestly, a thin connection, since HHG is dominated by early-cycle rescattering physics that the single-ionization, ponderomotive-free model used here does not capture. The abstract would be safer without promising that.
6. **Model assumptions unexamined.** Newly born electrons get zero initial velocity and ponderomotive forces are neglected — both defensible at ~30 GV/m, but the free-electron-velocity ODE (Eq. 3) has no drag from gas collision except the phenomenological γ; whether the *shape* of v_f(t), not just its amplitude, is robust to this is untested. Recombination is neglected, which is fine for 100 fs pulses but should be stated as a validity bound on gas density/pressure (the simulations never specify a pressure — only the W_SR rate is fixed).

### Presentation

1. **Fig. 3(c) caption and notation:** "phase-space representation of the motion of a free electron born at t = −∞" is not a physical electron in the model (electrons are born at t_n); it's the v_f(x_f)-type reference trajectory, and that should be said, since the spiral in the red curve is otherwise hard to interpret.
2. The argument that "A(ν) yields a similar spectrum as the pump field E(ν) but broader" (p. 6) is asserted from "the mathematical structure of Eq. (9)"; a single line showing explicitly that the FT of v_f(t)·H(t) inherits the pump's low-frequency content (e.g., via the low-frequency part of v_f(t) itself, cf. Fig. 3c) would make it a demonstration rather than an assertion.
3. The "yield ∝ ∫₀¹⁰⁰ ν²|E(ν)|²dν" in Fig. 2(c) is a proxy metric; stating that it is the *low-frequency* photon number (and hence why the 50 THz component is in-band) would pre-empt reader confusion.
4. Minor: "1D-FDTD" is first introduced in section 4 without citation placement for the specific code; the argon density is never stated even though W_ST(E) in Eq. (1) requires it; "b.u." in Fig. 2(c) should be spelled out.

## Verdict

A solid, honest piece of theoretical work that identifies and decomposes a specific feedback mechanism (generated THz → free-electron acceleration → modified low-frequency emission) and adds a useful 2D diffraction-limited bandwidth argument to the field. Its main limitations — no experiment, a single parameter set, an external-field proxy for the self-action, and an excluded high-intensity regime — keep it at a respectable but incremental step within the established Babushkin–Skupin local-current program rather than a field-shaping demonstration.

## [1407.6893v1-novelty] Novelty of the contribution


## Stated contribution (in my own words)

The paper argues that THz generation in gas plasmas by ionizing two-color (fundamental + second-harmonic) femtosecond pulses is **self-acting in the frequency domain**. Their picture: once a THz field begins to form (a few femtoseconds into the pump), it co-propagates through the ionized gas and acts on the free electrons created *after* that moment. The low-frequency (THz) component of the field adds its impulse to each electron's drift velocity, so the current — and hence the subsequently emitted THz spectrum — depends on the field that was already generated. In short, the emitted THz feeds back on the process that produces it, making the generation intrinsically nonlocal along the propagation direction.

To demonstrate this they (1) build a semi-analytic "local-current" model in which the secondary field splits as $E_J(\omega)\approx g\{A(\omega)-B(\omega)\}$, with $A(\omega)$ encoding the free-electron velocity trajectory (and thus the self-action) and $B(\omega)$ a memory kernel; (2) run 1D and 2D FDTD simulations; and (3) additionally show that in focused (2D) geometries **diffraction depletes the low-frequency component faster than the IR/visible light**, which caps the achievable THz bandwidth. They also speculate the effect matters for interpreting two-color high-harmonic generation.

## Novelty verdict

**What is genuinely new:**
- The specific reframing as *spectral self-action* — the self-generated THz acting on later-born electrons to broaden and shift the *THz* spectrum. The electron phase-space plot (Fig. 3c) and the $A$/$B$ decomposition are new analytical tools for isolating the mechanism.
- The 2D claim that diffraction, not ionization physics, is the bandwidth-limiting factor via preferential depletion of low frequencies.

**What is not new (and the authors themselves flag it):**
- Broadband THz from two-color ionizing gases, the plasma/quasi-DC-current origin, and the strong *nonlocality of propagation* on THz spectra are all established by the same group (Babushkin 2009/2010; Bergé 2013; Babushkin 2011) — ref. [15] is essentially the parent model this work extends.
- The physical substrate — a co-propagating low-frequency field modulating electron drift in ionized gas — is the same physics exploited for THz *remote sensing* (Oh et al. 2010, ref. [17]); here it is turned around to affect generation rather than being detected.
- The FDTD machinery and the LC approximation are inherited, not invented.

So the novelty is a **new interpretation of known physics**, which is a legitimate but modest step: rather than a new phenomenon, it is an explanation for already-observed spectral broadening.

## Critical limitations (affecting confidence)

- **The model breaks where it matters most.** The single-ionization, single-pulse LC model is, by the authors' own admission, not valid above ~50 GV/cm peak field — precisely the regime where self-action would be strongest. Their conclusions are therefore anchored in the moderate-intensity window.
- **The "yield increase" figure uses an *externally added* 2% THz pump component** (Fig. 2), which is an artificial perturbation; the *real* self-generated feedback (no THz at $z=0$) is shown separately and is less dramatic.
- **The physically realistic 2D case weakens the claim:** self-action is more pronounced in the 1D diffraction-free geometry, and in 2D diffraction *arrests* the mechanism past focus. So the effect is strongest under an idealized condition.
- **No experimental confirmation** — it is purely computational.

## Bottom line

A competent, internally consistent theoretical paper that extends a known model with a useful spectral decomposition and an FDTD confirmation. Its central idea (THz self-action) is a fresh lens on well-established plasma–field physics rather than a new mechanism, its predictive power sits outside the model's validity in the highest-field regime, and it is unverified experimentally.

### A. The semi-analytic (local-current) model

## My understanding of the paper's argument

**Context.** THz radiation can be generated by focusing intense two-color femtosecond pulses (fundamental + second harmonic) into a gas. After an initial (incorrect) attribution to optical rectification, the dominant mechanism was shown to be a quasi-DC plasma current produced by tunnel-photioized free electrons driven by the asymmetric two-color field, plus plasma oscillations. Broad spectral control of this emission remains hard because the field undergoes strong spatiotemporal, nonlocal propagation dynamics.

**Central claim (the "spectral self-action").** The already-generated THz field co-propagates with the ionizing pump and, because the produced THz is low-frequency and strong, it itself drives the free electrons in the plasma channel. This reshapes the free-electron velocity `v_f(t)`, which in turn alters the *subsequent* THz generation. The process is therefore nonlinear and nonlocal in the propagation direction: the emitted field feeds back on its own source.

**Analytical toolkit.** Starting from a semi-analytic model built on the *local-current (LC) approximation* (small gas volume, no propagation), they model (i) stepwise tunneling ionization into a sum of well-separated error-function "events" `ρ̂(t) ≈ Σ δρ_n H(t−t_n)`, (ii) the electron velocity via a damped first-order ODE, and (iii) the current `J(t)` → secondary radiation `E_J(ω)=gωJ(ω)`. They validate these closed forms (Eqs. 2, 4) against the numerics (Fig. 1, red dashed ≈ solid).

**The key decomposition.** They rewrite `gωJ(ω) ≈ g[A(ω) − B(ω)]`. `B(ω)` depends only on the velocity *at the ionization times* `v_f(t_n)`; `A(ω)` depends on the full `v_f(t)` after the electron is born. A known result: for a pure two-color pump at the optimum phase (φ=π/2), the sub-100 THz radiation is set entirely by `B(ν)` (Fig. 3a). When a low-frequency field is present, `A(ν)` contributes — because the THz field changes `v_f(t)`. `A(ν)` is a broadened version of the pump spectrum (convolution of `FT[v_f(t)]` with `FT[H(t)]`), so the feedback preferentially acts at low frequency and produces **spectral broadening** (Fig. 2, 3b, 4).

**Verification and role of diffraction.** The hypothesis is confirmed with 1D-FDTD (400 µm in Ar, nonlinear FDTD, argon dispersion, stepwise ionization, self-consistent `J`): the low-frequency part broadens past 100 THz and its peak shifts to higher frequency with distance — matching the semi-analytic model (Fig. 4). In 2D-FDTD (focused f=330 µm beam), the effect is *weaker and self-limiting*: beyond focus the THz field diffracts out of the plasma channel faster than the IR, so it can no longer drive the electrons; the emission spectrum peak stays pinned near ~50 THz (Fig. 5, 6). Thus **diffraction sets the final achievable bandwidth** by shortening the interaction length of the low-frequency field with the plasma.

**Implications suggested.** Beyond broadband THz generation, they argue the same feedback may matter for high-harmonic generation with two-color pulses, because the similar pump can alter electron trajectories on HHG-relevant timescales (Fig. 3c).

## Feedback

**Strengths.**
1. **Clear and genuinely useful mechanism.** The A(ν)/B(ν) decomposition is the real intellectual contribution: it localizes *where* the nonlinearity lives in the model. It explains, with a simple analytical picture, why the sub-100 THz spectrum is frozen for the pure two-color case but opens up once any low-frequency field is present. This is a satisfying, testable statement rather than a post-hoc fit.
2. **Consistent validation ladder.** Semi-analytic → 1D-FDTD → 2D-FDTD, with the same decomposition carried through each, and explicit "low-frequency-suppressed-pump" controls (Figs. 4, 5 dashed curves) that isolate the feedback from unrelated broadening. That control is well done and is the strongest part of the evidence.
3. **Physical honesty about limits.** They candidly state the effect is weaker in 2D, that a 37 GV/m case shows *no* self-action (THz field an order of magnitude too low), and that their model assumes single ionization and neglects ponderomotive forces. Good calibration of claim to evidence.

**Weaknesses / logical issues.**
1. **The illustrative seed is slightly contrived.** Section 3 opens by *adding* a 50 THz / 2 % amplitude component to the pump to "probe" the response (Fig. 2). This convincingly shows the *susceptibility* of the model, but it isn't the actual self-action, where the seed grows from zero. The genuine bootstrap — how sub-100 THz radiation, emitted with effectively zero low-frequency initial content, builds enough low-frequency amplitude to feed back — is only addressed by the FDTD (footnote ‡: "no THz component at z=0"). The analytical narrative therefore quietly leans on the numerics for the bootstrap, and the paper should say so more explicitly; as written it risks looking like the seed is doing the work.
2. **Overstated generality.** The model is a 1D, local, single-collision, phenomenological-`γ` approximation. The "strong impact" language in the 1D regime gets diluted in 2D, where the central effect (broadening/shifting) largely stops at focus. Since realistic two-color THz sources are focused 2D/3D beams, the most interesting regime is precisely where the paper finds the effect weak. The conclusion that "diffraction is the key player" is well supported, but it is a somewhat negative result: the self-action is real but is naturally throttled by the very geometry real experiments use.
3. **The HHHG implication (ref. It's fine to include as a closing remark, but it slightly overreaches the demonstrated scope.
4. **Minor.** The claim that A(ν) is "broader due to the convolution with FT[H(t)]" is stated in words (p. 5–6) but not derived; a reader who doubts the "broader" assertion (convolution broadening is standard, but the relative weights matter) would benefit from even a sentence on scale. The 50 THz / "15 fs duration" seed choice and the 0.2 fs ionization width τ are parameter choices whose sensitivity is not shown.

**Net.** A coherent, internally consistent mechanistic paper whose A/B decomposition is a nice analytical tool and whose 1D/2D-FDTD controls are rigorous. The novelty is a real but bounded reframing of already-reported broadening; the biggest effect appears in the (unphysical 1D) limit, and in realistic focused geometry the effect is self-limiting. Solid "good" work, not a paradigm shift.

# Critique: "Spectral self-action of THz emission from ionizing two-color laser pulses in gases" (arXiv:1407.6893v1, 2014)

## Strongest quantitative claim

The paper's strongest quantitative claim, combining the abstract and §3–§4, is: **the generated THz field interacts back with the newly produced free electrons and thereby reshapes its own spectrum** — quantitatively, adding a mere **2%-amplitude 50 THz component** to the driving field boosts the measured THz yield (∫ν²|E(ν)|²dν) by roughly an order of magnitude for nearly all relative phases (Fig. 2c, from ~10¹⁰ to ~5–10×10¹⁰–10¹¹ a.u.), and full 1D-FDTD propagation of the *unseeded* pump broadens the low-frequency secondary emission "well above 100 THz" (Fig. 4) with propagation-distance-dependent spectral shifts. The abstract elevates this to the strong statement that self-action "plays a key role in the observed strong spectral broadening of the generated THz field."

## Audit of the evidence

**1. Is the comparison fair?**

- The *controlled ablations* are well designed: Figs. 4 and 5 use identical simulations with only the low-frequency pump part (<200 THz) suppressed (dashed vs. solid), which is a legitimate A/B test of the feedback channel, and the A(ν)/B(ν) decomposition (Eqs. 8–10) cleanly attributes the effect to the free-electron-velocity term A(ν). The semi-analytic local-current model (Eqs. 2, 4) against the numerical solution of Eqs. (1), (3) (Fig. 1) is a fair internal consistency check with visibly excellent agreement.
- However, the headline yield claim (Fig. 2c) is a **seeded vs. unseeded comparison, and it conflates seed amplification with the spontaneous self-action mechanism**. A 2% seed yielding a ~10× yield increase is a strong nonlinearity, but it is also exactly what any stimulated/parametric amplification of a resonant seed would produce. No seed-amplitude convergence study is shown (e.g., yield vs. 0.1%, 1%, 2%, 5% seed) that would demonstrate the effect is dominated by self-generated THz rather than seed amplification. The unseeded FDTD runs partially compensate for this, but they do not isolate the magnitude of the seeded result.
- The claim of a "key role in the **observed** broadening" is not quantitatively supported against any experiment. The only experimental anchor is Babushkin et al., PRL 105, 053903 (2010) [Ref. 16], and the comparison is qualitative (broad low-frequency spectra at long propagation distances, as in their Fig. 4). There is no fit, no quantitative metric (bandwidth in THz, on-axis field, total energy) reconciling model and measurement.

**2. Are baselines current?**

- **As of 2014, mostly yes**: citations run through 2013 and cover the main competing lines (Roskos group: Cook 2000, Löffler 2004, Kim 2008/2012; Wen–Lindenberg PRL 2009; Bergé/Skupin HFM simulations PRL 2013). The authors correctly acknowledge that the propagation-induced low-frequency shift was already reported in [16, 22]; their genuinely new content is the mechanistic attribution (the A(ν) feedback through v_f, Fig. 3c), which is reasonable.
- **The model baseline is the weak point, and the authors flag it themselves**: the entire semi-analytic and FDTD framework is single-ionization with a phenomenological collision rate γ = 7.7 ps⁻¹, no recombination, and local plasma treatment. They state that simulations above ~50 GV/m peak become "questionable" in this model, while the 37 GV/m control shows *no* self-action at all. The positive result therefore sits in the 37–47 GV/m window exactly at the **edge of the model's stated validity** — precisely where multi-ionization and space-charge effects should become important. No independent model (higher-fidelity ab-initio treatment, multi-ionization FDTD, or experiment) is used to bracket the claim. From today's vantage the work was never experimentally verified, and the single-ionization assumption is known to be crude for these intensities.

**3. Error bars or statistical significance?**

- **None at all.** The paper contains no experimental data of its own, no error bars, no confidence intervals, no significance tests, no seed or parameter sensitivity analysis, and no FDTD grid-convergence study (cell size, timestep, Courant number, plasma-density sampling are not reported). Statements like "excellent agreement" (Figs. 1, 2) are visual judgments on log-scale plots without a quantitative agreement metric. The phenomenological parameters (γ, τ = 0.2 fs, r = 0.44, φ = π/2) are fixed by choice or by prior work and never perturbed, so no uncertainty is propagated into any of the quantitative numbers (the ~10× yield, the "well above 100 THz" bandwidth, the >10 THz shift in 2D).

## Summary judgment

A competent and clearly written computational study with a genuinely interesting mechanistic decomposition (A(ν)/B(ν)) and well-constructed internal A/B controls. But its strongest quantitative claim rests on (i) a seeded experiment that does not rule out seed amplification, (ii) a model that the authors themselves place at the boundary of validity for the very amplitude that produces the effect, and (iii) qualitative — not quantitative — agreement with a 2010 experiment. The absence of any uncertainty quantification (error bars, significance, convergence or parameter studies) means the headline numbers should be read as order-of-magnitude model outputs, not validated measurements.

## [1407.6893v1-weak-point] Weakest link in the argument


## Identifying the argument chain

The paper's structure is:

1. **Mechanism (Sec. 3):** In a local-current model, a co-propagating low-frequency (THz) field modifies the free-electron velocity $v_f(t)$, which enters the secondary emission through the $A(\nu)$ term (Eq. 9). Demonstrated by *injecting* a 2%-amplitude 50 THz component into the pump: the low-frequency secondary spectrum changes dramatically (Figs. 2–3).
2. **Corroboration (Sec. 4):** 1D/2D FDTD show the generated THz accumulates while propagating and the low-frequency spectrum broadens/shifts. The "causal test" is a *diagnostic*: recompute the local secondary emission $\nu J(\nu)$ from an artificially modified pump (low-frequency part below 200 THz suppressed), keeping all else from the full run (Figs. 4, 5 — dashed vs. solid).
3. **Conclusion:** THz self-action "plays a key role in the observed strong spectral broadening," and (via 2D diffraction of the THz) "diffraction limits the achievable THz bandwidth," with implications for interpreting two-color HHG experiments.

## The single weakest link

The gap is the step from the dashed-vs-solid local-emission comparison to the claim that **self-action is "a key player" in the *recorded* spectral broadening** — i.e., the causal attribution in Section 4 (and the conclusion), which rests on a diagnostic that is not a counterfactual simulation.

**Exactly why it is not entailed by the data:**

1. **The counterfactual only changes the local source, not the run.** In Figs. 4–5 the authors compute the local emission from a field with its sub-200 THz part removed, using the ionization times $t_n$ and density increments $\delta\rho_n$ *taken from the with-feedback run*. This measures "how broad is the local source term if the co-propagating THz weren't present to drive electrons, at fixed plasma." But the quantity the paper actually explains — the *recorded, propagated* spectrum $E(\nu)$ — is the coherent accumulation of THz radiated by the plasma at *every* point of the channel. Nothing in the paper quantifies how much of that accumulated low-frequency content would remain if the feedback loop were genuinely turned off: the pump alone still drives asymmetric THz emission at every $z$, and that emission still adds coherently to the co-propagating field. The baseline (no-feedback) *recorded* spectrum is never computed.
2. **Freezing the plasma hides the feedback's other paths.** The injected THz alters the full electron trajectory (Fig. 3c shows the phase-space orbits moving); in a real no-feedback run the ionization dynamics, drift velocities, and densities would differ everywhere downstream. The suppressed-field diagnostic, by construction with $\delta\rho_n, t_n$ held fixed, excludes that entire class of back-action, so the observed local-spectrum difference neither over- nor under-shoots the true no-feedback recorded spectrum in a controlled way — it simply isn't the right observable.
3. **Because the source model is nearly linear** ($J(t)$ is a linear-response functional of the local total field, Eq. 3–4), the difference between solid and dashed curves is largely guaranteed by the presence of a strong local low-frequency field: electrons in a plasma with ~10–100 MV/m of THz present will radiate low frequency content whether that THz is "self-generated" or not. The demonstrated effect — "a co-propagating THz field reshapes the local source term" — is necessary for the claimed mechanism, but a local property. The *nonlocal* claim (that history-dependent self-action, not plain accumulation of pump-driven emission and the already-known red-shifting reported in refs. 16, 22, is responsible for the observed broadband shape) is the inferential leap, and no run with the feedback selectively switched off in the electron dynamics (i.e., the $A(\nu)\to B(\nu)$ limit) is ever performed to measure it.
4. **The 2D claim compounds the gap.** "Diffraction limits the achievable bandwidth / efficiently depletes the low-frequency amplitudes" is inferred from a single transverse profile 30 µm beyond focus (Fig. 6) plus the observation that the spectral maximum pins at ~50 THz. That is a static geometric observation; it is never shown that the low-frequency *amplitude depletion* is caused by defocusing rather than by the reduced on-axis plasma density or reduced local gain (the plasma channel itself decays, Fig. 5g), and no 2D counterfactual run is done either.

**What would close the gap:** one FDTD run in which the electron–current coupling to the co-propagating THz component is selectively removed (so the $A(\nu)$ term collapses to the $B(\nu)$-only form) with the full recorded 1D/2D spectra compared, plus a quantitative decomposition of the recorded low-frequency spectrum into a baseline (pump-driven, passively accumulated) part and a feedback-dependent part. Until then, the data entail "the local source term is broadened by a co-propagating THz field" — a much weaker statement than "self-action plays a key role in the observed spectral broadening and determines the attainable bandwidth."

## Critique of writing and framing

**What the paper actually shows, in one breath.** A semi‑analytic local‑current model, confirmed against 1D and 2D FDTD, demonstrates that (a) an injected ~50 THz, 2%‑amplitude field reshapes the THz spectrum of two‑color ionization, and (b) in self‑consistent propagation the emitted low‑frequency THz back‑acts on newly born electrons and broadens the spectrum; in 2D, diffraction then depletes the low‑frequency field and pins the spectral peak at ~50 THz. That is a coherent, mostly defensible result. The problem is a persistent gap between the *modest, geometry‑specific, single‑ionization* evidence and the *universal, causal, "key‑role"* language the authors wrap it in — concentrated in the abstract and conclusions, where the body is frequently careful.

### Sentences that overclaim beyond the evidence

1. **Abstract: "…make the process inherently nonlocal."**
   The mechanism is a *causal, retarded* feedback: `v_f(t)` is an integral of `E(τ)` from −∞ to t (Eq. 5), so the THz field emitted earlier back‑acts on electrons born later. That is standard causality of any propagating emission, not a fundamental spatial nonlocality. A solid, self-contained theoretical paper presenting a plausible and well-supported propagation mechanism (THz spectral self-action) with a clean semi-analytic handle and good model–simulation agreement, plus a practically insightful finding on diffraction-limited bandwidth in 2D. It is a competent contribution to an active subfield but is not a headline result: the effect, while mechanistically clean, is bounded by the single-ionization regime, lacks experimental confirmation, and is somewhat weaker in the more realistic 2D geometry. ## What the paper is doing

It asks a clean, well‑motivated question: if the THz you are generating is co‑propagating with the ionizing two‑color pump, does that already‑emitted THz feed back on the free‑electron dynamics of electrons born later, and thereby reshape its own spectrum? You build a semi‑analytic model on the local‑current (LC) approximation, validate it against a full numerical solution of the rate + current equations (Fig. 1–2), decompose the radiated field into `A(ω)` (contains the full trajectory `v_f(t)`) and `B(ω)` (contains only the ionization‑time values `v_f(t_n)`), argue `A` is the self‑action channel, and then confirm in 1D‑ and 2D‑FDTD that (i) the sub‑100 THz spectrum broadens and shifts up during propagation, and (ii) diffraction limits the usable bandwidth because it depletes the co‑propagating low‑frequency field inside the plasma channel.

## What is good

- **The physical framing is solid.** Identifying a *nonlocal‑in‑propagation* self‑action mechanism for THz spectral shaping is a legitimate and understudied point, and it's genuinely the right thing to ask for broadband THz sources.
- **The decomposition is the real contribution.** Splitting `νJ(ν)` into `A(ν)` and `B(ν)` (Eqs. 8–10) turns a numerical "the spectrum changed" observation into a mechanistic statement, and Fig. 4 uses it to isolate which channel is active. That is better than most purely‑simulator papers do.
- **The controlled "suppress the low‑frequency part of the pump" comparison** (dashed curves in Fig. 4 and Fig. 5) is the strongest piece of evidence you have: it directly tests whether it is *self‑generated* low‑frequency field (not the pump's own IR content) driving the broadening. Keep and highlight this.
- **Model validation is done properly** — you don't just throw the semi‑analytic reduction at FDTD, you first show it reproduces the full solution of Eqs. (1),(3) (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).
- Good figures, clean writing, honest about the LC approximations reproducing the numerics.

## What I would push you to fix before publication

These are in roughly descending order of importance.

1. **Your quantitative claim is qualitative.** The headline is "strong spectral broadening," but you never *measure* it. Give me bandwidth‑above‑100 THz (or a spectral centroid) vs. propagation distance, full pump vs. suppressed‑pump, with the delta stated as a number. Right now a reader can't tell whether the self‑action gives a 10% or a 100% relative bandwidth increase. The noisy curves in Fig. 4(b,d,h) and (c,f,i) worry me — are those wiggles physics or time‑sampling/FFT padding artifacts? Say how you window/zero‑pad and that the broadened *envelope* is robust to that.

2. **The 2% seed is a proxy, and you don't close the loop.** You inject a 2%-amplitude, 50 THz component to *demonstrate* sensitivity, then argue the *naturally generated* field does the same for free. You never state the amplitude ratio of self‑generated THz to the pump. Report it. If the coherent on‑axis self‑field is, say, ~1–3% of the fundamental over the generation length, your argument is airtight; if it's smaller, the 2% test isn't representative. This is the single gap that most undermines the central mechanistic claim.

3. **The local‑current radiation law (Eq. 6) is the crux and is under‑justified.** `E_J(ω) = g ω J(ω)` is a *local* relation (cite: Jackson, for the constant) — it says the field at a point comes from the current at that point. But your effect is fundamentally *nonlocal*: the field from upstream generation drives downstream electrons. You handle that nonlocality only implicitly through FDTD. The whole semi‑analytic insight rests on LC, so I want a paragraph on its limits — at what wavelength/curvature does local radiation become bad, and does Fig. 6 (THz diffracting much more strongly than IR) already tell you it's strained for the high‑frequency part you care about? Cite [18] for the prefactor, yes, but justify the local form.

4. **Your model and your best results disagree about regime.** You say the model is "questionable in the high‑intensity regime" (>~50 GV/m) and accounts for single ionization only — yet your 2D case focuses to 47 GV/m, right at that boundary. The 37 GV/m reduced case shows *no* self‑action at all, which is a clean result, but it also means your *only* clean 2D demonstration is at the weakest field, and your interesting high‑field 2D case sits where your semi‑analytic justification is weakest. State where, quantitatively, the semi‑analytic `ρ̂, Ĵ` begin to deviate from the full solution as intensity rises, rather than hand‑waving the boundary.

5. **Sharpens the novelty claim.** The up‑shift of the low‑frequency maximum with propagation distance is explicitly "already reported in [16],[22]" — two of which are your own group's papers. So the *observables* are not new; only the *attribution* to THz self‑feedback vs. pump spectral broadening is. The abstract as written ("plays a *key role*") over‑claims relative to the body. Reword the abstract so a reader knows the new thing is the *mechanism*, and put the Babushkin 2010/2011 context up front where you discuss the shift.

6. **Cheaper things to mention/cut:**
   - Damping: γ = 7.7 ps⁻¹ gives a time constant ≈130 ps vs. ~100 fs dynamics, so `e^{γ(t-t_n)}≃1` throughout. The term does essentially nothing; say so and drop it as a "free parameter," or justify keeping it.
   - Zero birth velocity + neglecting the ponderomotive force inside a strong THz field are assumptions; for a *self‑action* paper where the THz field is the actor, at least discuss whether ponderomotive quiver of the newborn electron could masquerade as the `A(ω)` effect.
   - The 2D diffraction conclusion (your last claim) is drawn from one f‑number (f=330 µm, w₀=30.2 µm). You've made the right physical argument (Fig. 6), but a sentence on the scaling (Rayleigh length vs. THz wavelength) would make it general rather than "for this beam."
   - The HHG implication (conclusion, [23]) is the most exciting thing in the paper and gets four sentences at the end with a single phase‑space figure. Either develop it a half‑paragraph more or cut it — as is, it reads as an unearned teaser.

## The overall picture

This is competent, coherent, and physically intelligent work — much better organized than most simulator papers because of the `A/B` decomposition and the suppressed‑pump control. But it is *moderately* original (the observables predate you; the mechanism attribution is the genuinely new part), and the central result is asserted more than measured. The two structural weaknesses are (a) the gap between the 2% demonstration seed and the actual self‑field amplitude, and (b) the reliance on the local‑current law and single‑ionization limit precisely in the geometry/intensity where you want the strongest claim. Fix the quantification (points 1–2) and the novelty sentence in the abstract, and this is a good, publishable NJP‑level paper.
