## [0905.4540v1-general-review] General Review


## Review: Nardetto et al., "High resolution spectroscopy for Cepheids distance determination V. Impact of the cross-correlation method on the p-factor and the γ-velocities"

### Summary of the argument

The paper addresses a practical but underappreciated problem: the cross-correlation (CC) radial-velocity pipelines (e.g. HARPS, CCF-based) are the workhorse for low S/N Cepheid spectra, but the Baade–Wesselink (BW) distance method was built on first-moment radial velocities. The gap between the two is absorbed by the projection factor *p*, so using a *p* derived from the wrong velocity definition propagates into the distance. The authors:

1. Define a "CC projection factor" $p_{cc}=p\,f_{cc}$ where $f_{cc}$ corrects the first-moment amplitude to the CC amplitude.
2. Derive, from 8 HARPS Cepheids (P = 3.4–41.5 d), a dedicated relation $p_{cc}=p\,f_{cc}=(-0.08\pm0.05)\log P+[1.31\pm0.06]$.
3. Show a wavelength dependence of the CC amplitude.
4. Demonstrate that CC-derived γ-velocities are systematically lower than literature γ-velocities by ~1–1.8 km/s, arguing that ~half of the "K-term" is a methodological artefact of the CC method.

The motivation is sound: BW distances of M31/LMC/SMC Cepheids do in practice feed on CC radial velocities, so a CC-appropriate *p* relation is a genuinely useful calibration artifact.

### Strengths

- **Well-motivated and internally consistent** with the series (Papers I–III); the logic that $f_{cc}$ must be period-independent is clean, and the check (Fig. 2a, mean $f_{cc}=0.93\pm0.02$) supports it.
- **High-quality data**: well-sampled HARPS curves, careful cubic-spline interpolation, and per-order uncertainties give the amplitude estimates credibility that older, low-resolution determinations lacked.
- **The γ-velocity comparison** (Fig. 5) is a nice diagnostic: putting $V_\gamma^{[CC]}$ against both the Galactic Cepheid Database value and the paper-III center-of-mass value in one panel is a genuinely informative test, and attributing part of the K-term to the CC method's velocity-gradient sensitivity is a reasonable, physically-motivated interpretation.
- **Honest about uncertainty**: the authors explicitly state that the wavelength corrections are negligible given the error bars on Eq. 5, rather than overselling them.

### Criticisms

1. **The sample is the central weakness.** A log-linear *Pp* relation fitted to **8 stars** with reduced $\chi^2=1.2$ is statistically fragile. The derived slope $-0.08\pm0.05$ is consistent with zero, and the difference from Paper II (slope $-0.064\to-0.08$; zero-point $1.376\to1.31$, "5%") is small and within the overlap of error bars between the two relations. With only eight points spanning $\log P\in[0.53,1.62]$, the fit is easily dominated by a couple of long-period outliers (RZ Vel, RS Pup at the top of the $f_{cc}$ dispersion). The statistical claim of a 5% zero-point shift is not clearly significant. A larger sample would either confirm or dissolve the headline result.

2. **The wavelength-dependence result is self-defeating.** Section 5 goes to the effort of deriving $f_{400nm}$ and $f_{700nm}$, only to conclude they are "currently irrelevant given our statistical uncertainties." Presenting a measured effect immediately discounted by its own uncertainties weakens the paper; these relations should either be framed as an outlook with a clear power analysis of what sample size would make them significant, or dropped.

3. **The *p* used in the $f_{cc}$ derivation is circular in a subtle way.** $f_{cc}$ is computed against the Paper-II $p$ for the Fe I 4896 Å line — the same single low-depth line (D≈8%) that anchored the original *Pp* relation. Because $f_{rad}$ (the ratio of the 4896 amplitude to the line-forming-region amplitude) is the *only* quantity in the *Pp* relation sensitive to line depth, and because the CC mask uses an average depth ~25%, the entire CC correction is inherited from the single-line calibration. The robustness of $p_{cc}$ to that one anchor line is not tested; a sensitivity check across a few diagnostic lines (e.g. Fe I vs Ca, different D) is missing.

4. **K-term attribution is hedged and speculative.** The claim that "≈50% of the K-term" is a CC artefact rests on a 1–2 km/s systematic in γ-velocity, with the remaining 1–2 km/s attributed to an *unidentified* "another contribution." The paper does not separate genuine rotation asymmetry from the depth/gradient effect in the CC measurement; the 1 km/s blue-shift the abstract notes as needed to explain the K-term is asserted, not derived. This is the paper's most physically interesting claim and its least firmly supported.

5. **Scope of the correction is understated.** The mask is built from a G2 synthetic model and the correction is derived for a specific wavelength window in a G3–F-type dominated sample. Generalization to LMC/SMC Cepheids (the actual application) to different metallicities, to cooler/hotter subtypes, and to lower-resolution pipelines is hand-waved in the conclusions ("large sample with metallicities"). The practical distance gain from switching to $p_{cc}$ is not quantified end-to-end (i.e. the resulting shift in a BW distance, in magnitudes) — that is what would make the paper compelling to a distance-calibrator.

### Verdict

A competent, careful, and well-motivated incremental contribution to an ongoing series. It delivers a practical tool (a CC-specific *Pp* relation) and a plausible re-interpretation of the K-term, but it is limited by a very small calibration sample, an inherited single-line assumption, a self-discounted wavelength result, and a K-term claim that remains only partly explained. The paper would be substantially stronger with a larger sample, a per-line sensitivity study, and a quantified end-to-end distance impact.

## Summary of the work

This is Paper V of Nardetto's "High resolution spectroscopy for Cepheids distance determination" series. It tackles a concrete, well-motivated calibration problem: the cross-correlation (CC) method — the workhorse technique for Cepheid radial velocities in most large surveys — is applied to distance work via a projection factor (*p*) that was derived for the first-moment (line-center) velocity definition. The authors ask whether using the "wrong" *p* biases the Baade–Wesselink (BW) distance, and whether the CC method explains the long-standing Cepheid "K-term" (~2 km s⁻¹ blue shift). Using eight Galactic Cepheids observed with HARPS, they (i) fit a CC-specific *Pp* relation, (ii) measure a systematic CC blue shift, and (iii) tentatively look at wavelength dependence.

## What the paper does well

- **The motivation is real and important.** If nearly every Cepheid RV survey uses CC, then any systematic in how CC maps to the photometric (photospheric) velocity amplitude propagates directly into every derived distance and every Galactic γ-velocity. This is a first-order calibration question, not a curiosity, and the framing isolates it cleanly from the intrinsic atmospheric velocity-gradient correction handled in Papers II/III.
- **The experimental design is sound.** Comparing the HARPS-native CC curves to the first-moment *RV_c* curves on the *same* data and stars, then reading off the amplitude ratio *f_cc*, is the right way to disentangle the CC-specific bias from the gradient correction. Deriving the *Pp_cc* zero point by differencing from the Paper-II relation is clean.
- **The headline results are coherent and internally consistent.** The slope on *p* (−0.08 ± 0.05) matches the rough Paper-II CC estimate (−0.075), the *f_cc* ≈ 0.93 (i.e., CC slightly under-amplifies the curve relative to the photosphere) is physically sensible given the mask spans depths ~25% vs. the *Pp* line at 8%, and the ~1.0 km s⁻¹ systematic blue shift plausibly explains a fraction of the K-term. The comparison table (Table 2) of Vᵧ[CC] vs. Vᵧ[GCD] vs. Vᵧ[NOS8] is exactly the right object to scrutinize, and Figure 5 is clear.
- **Good reproducibility scaffolding** for that era: interpolated CC curves published per star (Fig. 1), and the full RV_CC tables (Tables 4–5) with phases and uncertainties.
- **Intellectual honesty** about what they *cannot* claim: they explicitly say the wavelength dependence is within noise (≲5%), and that ~50% of the K-term remains unexplained by CC.

## Where it falls short

- **The sample is dangerously small for what it's being used for.** *Pp_cc* is fit to **eight** Galactic, roughly solar-metallicity, G-type Cepheids, and the stated purpose is calibrating *LMC*/SMC Cepheids for the surface-brightness distance scale. There is no metallic- or M-type anchor, no LMC anchor, and the extrapolation in period (8–42 d) is uncontrolled. Eight points, with slopes/intercepts of ±0.05/±0.06, is a plausible relation but a weak *calibration*. The conclusions themselves flag metallicity, S/N, and resolution as open — i.e., the very variables that matter for the LMC.
- **The error treatment is loose in places.** The *f_cc* values in Table 2 scatter from 0.90 to 0.99 while the reported mean is 0.93 ± 0.02. For eight points with individual bar errors of ~0.01, the standard error of the mean should be ~0.004; a ±0.02 bar must be hiding systematic/scatter that isn't clearly justified. The wavelength-dependence fit gives reduced χ² = 0.3 (Fig. 4, blue), which is suspiciously small and may point to underestimated per-order errors rather than a genuinely tight fit. These should be spelled out.
- **The K-term result is under-powered and partly narrative.** Claiming the CC bias "explains ~50% of the K-term" rests on a 1.0 ± 0.2 km s⁻¹ mean over eight stars. The Gaussian-profile CC estimate is, as the authors note, sensitive to temperature, abundance, rotation, and line width *independently* of period — exactly the degeneracies that could masquerade as (or confound) a 1 km s⁻¹ systematic. The remaining ~1 km s⁻¹ is hand-wave-dispatched to "quality of 1939 observations" or "different methods." This is the most interesting thread in the paper (Galactic-structure γ-velocities) but it's the least quantitatively secure.
- **It lags current p-factor practice.** By the point this was being finalized, much of the field had converged on *p* ≈ 1.4–1.5 via hydrodynamics/model atmospheres and on different RV/line-selection strategies; a purely empirical CC-vs-first-moment correction on eight stars, while valid for its era, is more of a targeted patch than a durable calibration. That limits its longevity.

## Bottom line

This is a competent, clearly written, and physically motivated contribution that delivers on its stated aims and hands the community a directly usable *Pp_cc* relation plus a reasonable partial account of the K-term. It is not, however, a strong *calibration*: eight stars, mild effects (a few percent in *p*, 1 km s⁻¹ of 2), modest error handling, and an incomplete K-term story cap its weight. It is a solid incremental step in a credible series rather than a result that moves the distance scale or the field.

# Critique: Nardetto et al., "High resolution spectroscopy for Cepheids distance determination V. Impact of the cross-correlation method on the p-factor and the γ-velocities" (arXiv:0905.4540)

## Summary of the claim

Applying the HARPS cross-correlation (CC) method to 8 Galactic Cepheids, the authors (i) show that the CC pipeline overestimates the radial-velocity amplitude by a factor $f_{cc}\simeq 0.93\pm0.02$, (ii) use that to rescale the semi-theoretical period–projection-factor relation of Paper II into one "dedicated" to CC data ($P_{p_{cc}}$), (iii) argue the wavelength dependence of $P_{p_{cc}}$ is negligible over the optical, and (iv) use the CC γ-velocities to explain roughly half of the historical Cepheid "K-term."

## What the paper does well

**Clear, useful motivation.** The p-factor is the key conversion from radial to pulsation velocity in the Baade–Wesselink method, and if it is "wrong" it biases the radius linearly and the distance linearly. A ~5% zero-point shift (1.376 → 1.31) is genuinely meaningful for the local distance-scale calibration, so the question is well posed.

**Cleanly defined method.** Defining $p_{cc}$ as a ratio to a single well-characterized first-moment line (Fe I 4896, Eq. 4) cleanly isolates the CC-specific bias from the velocity-gradient physics already handled in Paper II. The "we know the answer a priori" structure makes it a well-posed comparison.

**Internal consistency.** The new slope ($-0.08$) agrees with the rough Paper II gradient-only estimate ($-0.075$), and $P_{p_{cc}}$ reproduces Mérand et al.'s $p=1.27$ for δ Cep. These are honest cross-checks.

**Appropriate intellectual honesty.** The authors explicitly flag that SNR, resolution, metallicity and the remaining ~50% of the K-term are unaddressed.

## Weaknesses and concerns

**1. Sample size vs. the number of free parameters.** Eight Cepheids are being used simultaneously to (a) fit a two-parameter $P_{p}$ line, (b) fit six per-star λ-slopes (Table 3, 16 parameters for 69 usable orders), and (c) fit two γ-velocity correlations. This is a lot to extract from $N=8$. In particular the claimed $P_{p}$ slope of $-0.08\pm0.05$ is significant only at ~1.6σ, so the "the slope changes" message is much weaker than the paper's framing. It is the zero-point correction ($\times0.93$) that really carries the result, and that is a single effective number.

**2. The γ-velocity/K-term fits are statistically poor.** The reduced χ² of the two linear relations (11) and (12) are 3.0 and 3.8 — far from unity. The authors draw quantitative conclusions (the 1.0 km/s offset, the "50% of the K-term" split) from intercepts of clearly underfitting lines, and the $V_\gamma\text{[GCD]}$ points themselves carry ±2.0 km/s errors. This section is suggestive, not demonstrative, and the paper slightly overstates it. The "K-term" decomposition into 50% CC + 50% "mystery" should be presented more cautiously as a hypothesis.

**3. Hidden dependence on Paper II.** The whole calibration is an empirical rescaling of Paper II's semi-theoretical p. It inherits *all* of that paper's systematic uncertainties (hydrodynamic scaling, the single-line assumption, the $f_{\rm grad}$ estimate) and provides no independent external validation. A genuinely robust result would anchor $P_{p_{cc}}$ against an independent p (e.g. full hydrodynamics or a direct interferometric distance), which here is not available.

**4. Mask/line-mix heterogeneity is unmodeled.** The CC mask is built from a G2 synthetic star, yet the sample spans a range of $T_{\rm eff}$ and metallicity, so the *actual* mix of line-forming depths differs per star. Since the amplitude bias partly comes from line-depth-dependent velocity gradients, a per-star mismatch between mask and true line mix could masquerade as $f_{cc}$ scatter. With only 8 stars the authors cannot disentangle this from the period trend — they flag metallicity as future work, but $T_{\rm eff}$ masking effects are a nearer-term limitation.

**5. Gaussian profile assumption.** The CC velocity comes from fitting the average profile with a Gaussian. For a broad mixture of Cepheid lines with a non-trivial velocity gradient the profile is not exactly Gaussian; the authors note this (bias #1 in Sect. 4) but do not quantify the induced error on either the amplitude or the γ-velocity. Given that the entire result hinges on amplitude ratios accurate to a few km/s, this deserves a sensitivity estimate.

**6. "Negligible wavelength dependence" conclusion.** With only 69 usable orders and 8 stars, and per-star λ-slopes of order $-0.005$ to $-0.011$ km/s/nm, the claim that red corrections (Eq. 10, slope $+0.02$) are negligible is defensible *today* — and the authors are careful to say it "might become significant in the near future." But it is a conclusion at the edge of the data's sensitivity, not a clear null result.

**7. Minor presentation issues.** Typos ("Edouart Belin," "the whole profil," "A reason might be that," "reduce χ²"); the fcc statement "clearly statistically dispersed around a mean" slightly over-reads points that are largely consistent at their quoted errors; the K-term attribution to "quality of observations in the past" (Joy 1939) is speculative.

## Verdict

A coherent, competently executed continuation of a well-structured series that produces a practically useful calibration relation for a standard technique. Its science is sound but incremental and internally referenced rather than independently validated, and the two most interesting quantitative claims — the period-slope change and the K-term decomposition — rest on fits that are statistically fragile given the eight-star sample. The core amplitude-rescaling result is trustworthy; the finer conclusions should be read as provisional. This is solid, mid-tier observational spectroscopy rather than a breakthrough.

## The paper's stated contribution, in my own words

The project has already (in Papers I–III) established that the first-moment of a single, close-to-photosphere spectral line (Fe I 4896.439 Å) is the cleanest spectroscopic proxy for the pulsation of a Cepheid, and derived from it a semi-theoretical "Pp" linear-period–projection-factor relation, plus a set of calibrated center-of-mass γ-velocities. This fifth paper does three things:

1. **Quantifies how the cross-correlation (CC) method — the standard tool for low-S/N radial velocities, and the one the whole Cepheid distance / Galactic-structure literature relies on — distorts the projection factor.** From a dedicated "correction factor" f_cc = ΔRV_cc/ΔRV_c, measured on 8 galactic Cepheids with HARPS, it derives a **Pp relation tuned specifically to CC-derived velocities**: p_cc = [−0.08 ± 0.05] log P + [1.31 ± 0.06].

2. **Characterizes the wavelength dependence** of the CC amplitude (a_λ relation, and 400nm/700nm correcting factors).

3. **Uses the CC γ-velocities to re-examine the "K-term."** It finds the CC method systematically underestimates the γ-velocity by ~1.0–2 km/s, so that CC alone accounts for only ~50% of the historically quoted, apparently-intrinsic K-term (~2 km/s) — arguing the K-term is likely largely an artifact of the velocity-measurement method (the rest attributed to dynamical atmospheric structure / other measurement effects, per Paper III).

## Is it novel, given the cited prior work?

The contribution is **incremental but genuinely useful** rather than conceptually new. Concretely:

**What is new (genuinely):**
- The **CC-specific Pp relation** (Eq. 5) is a new, practical deliverable. Prior Pp relations (Paper II, and Mérand et al. 2005) were not tied to a specific velocity-measurement method; here the authors explicitly separate the method-dependent projection factor from the intrinsic one. This is a real, citable advance for anyone calibrating the Cepheid distance scale using the ubiquitous CC pipeline (e.g., the LMC/SMC distance work by Gieren et al.).
- The **quantitative link between the CC method and the K-term** (Sects. 6, 7) is the most interesting scientific point: showing that only ~50% of the K-term traceable to the measurement method, reframing Joy's (1939) "2 km/s" intrinsic term. This is a reasonable and somewhat new interpretation, though it is partly carried by Paper III.
- The f_cc ≈ 0.93 ± 0.02 correction, the a_λ wavelength-slope relation (Eq. 8), and the 400/700nm correcting factors (Eqs. 9–10) are new quantitative relations, if at the margin of statistical significance.

**What is not new (built on the series' own prior work / cited methods):**
- The core conceptual machinery is from **Paper II** (the semi-theoretical Pp relation, the single-line f_grad correction, the ΔRV_c amplitude method) and **Paper III** (the γ-velocity / center-of-mass / K-term framework). This paper applies and extends rather than invents.
- The **CC method itself** (Baranne et al. 1996; Pepe et al. 2002; HARPS pipeline) is a standard, well-established technique — not new.
- The **BW method, Pp relations, and γ-velocity concepts** are long-standing (Kervella et al. 2004; Davis et al. 2008; Gieren et al. 1998; Joy 1939; Fernie et al. 1995). The "first moment is independent of velocity gradient" idea is from Paper I (2006), itself following Burki et al. 1982.

**Verdict on novelty:** This is the 5th in a tightly-coupled series that builds on the same instrument (HARPS), the same 8-star sample, and the same internal framework. The CC-specific Pp relation is a legitimate, useful new tool, but it is a method-application refinement rather than a conceptual breakthrough. A reviewer should note that the statistical power is thin — 8 stars (7 for the wavelength trends), with correction factors (f_cc, the 400/700nm factors) that the authors themselves concede are "marginal" given the uncertainties, and χ²_reduced well above unity (3.0, 3.8, 0.3/1.3). The "new" Pp relation is close to the Paper II one, differing mainly in the zero-point (−0.084 vs −0.064 slope). The strongest and most defensible novelty is the K-term accounting (~50% attributable to the CC method), which is a new quantification rather than a new phenomenon.

**Caveats a critic would raise:** the sample is small (8 stars, periods ~3.4–41.5 d) and biased toward bright, well-covered galactic Cepheids; the CC relations extrapolate to the LMC/SMC (different metallicities, higher S/N regimes, different lines in the mask) without a dedicated test; the physical explanation of the a_λ decrease with wavelength is hand-waved ("consistent with" hydrodynamical models rather than verified); and several claims hinge on the internal consistency of the five-paper framework rather than independent confirmation.

## [0905.4540v1-methodology] Methodology reproducibility


## Paper: Nardetto et al. (2009), "High resolution spectroscopy for Cepheids distance determination V. Impact of the cross-correlation method on the p-factor and the γ-velocities"

### Methodology (reproducible summary)

**Goal.** The semi-theoretical period–projection-factor (Pp) relation of Paper II (Nardetto et al. 2007) was derived from the *first moment* (centroid) of a single Fe I 4896.439 Å line. Most distance programs (Baade–Wesselink with the infrared surface-brightness method) instead use cross-correlation (CC) RVs, which the authors suspect underestimates the RV amplitude and biases the γ-velocity. The experiment measures both biases and re-derives a Pp relation valid for CC RVs.

**Step-by-step procedure:**

1. **Sample and data.** Eight Galactic Cepheids already observed with HARPS (R ≈ 120,000, 72 orders) at La Silla (programs 072.D-0419, 073.D-0136): R TrA (P = 3.39 d), S Cru (4.69), Y Sgr (5.77), β Dor (9.84), ζ Gem (10.15), RZ Vel (20.40), ℓ Car (35.55), RS Pup (41.52). Observing details (epoch T₀, measurement count) are in Paper I; the measured (phase, RVcc) pairs are fully tabulated (Tabs. 4–5).
2. **CC RVs.** The standard HARPS pipeline (Baranne et al. 1996; Pepe et al. 2002): a G2-mask of thousands of metallic lines (line weights from a G2 star) is cross-correlated; the mean profile is fitted by a Gaussian over the *whole* profile via χ² minimization, giving one RV per order and one RV averaged over all 72 orders, used here with their pipeline uncertainties (~0.5 km s⁻¹).
3. **Curve interpolation.** Each (phase, RVcc) set is interpolated with a periodic cubic spline, either directly through the data or through "pivot points" (max/min/zero-crossings) optimized by least squares (Mérand et al. 2005). Pivot points are used for Y Sgr and RS Pup, which have incomplete phase coverage. The amplitude is ΔRVcc = max − min of the interpolated curve; its error is taken as the mean of the per-point RV errors over a cycle.
4. **Transfer from the fiducial line to CC.** For each star, the reference amplitude ΔRVc[4896] (first-moment curve of the low-depth, photosphere-proximate Fe I 4896 Å line, from Paper II; Tab. 1) is divided by ΔRVcc to obtain a per-star correction factor fcc = ΔRVc[4896]/ΔRVcc (Tab. 2). A linear fit of fcc vs. log P shows no reliable trend, but a mean 0.93 ± 0.02 (scatter ±~0.02–0.03).
5. **New CC Pp relation.** Multiplying the Paper II p values by fcc and refitting gives **pcc = [−0.08 ± 0.05] log P + [1.31 ± 0.06]** (reduced χ² = 1.2), versus Paper II's p = [−0.064 ± 0.020] log P + [1.376 ± 0.023]. The effect is mainly a 5% zero-point decrease; the slope change (−0.064 → −0.08) is partly attributed to the CC line mix forming higher in the atmosphere (mean depth ≈ 25% vs 8%).

### Assumptions / gaps the methodology papers over (or papers only thinly over)

1. **n = 8 for two free parameters.** The Pp fit has 6 d.o.f.; the γ fits have reduced χ² of 3.0 and 3.8, i.e. the scatter is far larger than quoted errors. The 0.93 ± 0.02 mean fcc and the −0.99 + 0.78 km s⁻¹ intercepts are then statistically fragile.
2. **Instrument/technique transferability.** The fcc = 0.93 correction is measured only on HARPS at R ≈ 120,000 and S/N ≈ 100–200, yet is presented as applicable to *any* CC Pp use (lower S/N, different R, other masks, LMC/SMC targets). The authors explicitly defer S/N, spectral-resolution, and mask tests — the correction's dependence on them is unknown, and the Gaussian-fit bias should scale with spectral quality.
3. **Mask–star mismatch.** The HARPS mask is G2-weighted; the sample spans F- and K-type Cepheids with different line depths, abundances and rotation. The paper lists Teff/metallicity/rotation as *hypothesized* biases (Sec. 4, item 1) but never quantifies or tests them; the resulting "no trend" of fcc vs. period may simply be these effects washing out.
4. **Fiducial reference validity.** The whole construction inherits Paper II's chain: ΔV_p^o comes from 1-D hydrodynamic models, and Fe I 4896 is assumed to form "close to the photosphere" with a linear ΔRVc(D) gradient correction. Any model error in the velocity gradient propagates 1:1 into pcc.
5. **Stability of the pulsation.** Periodic-spline fitting presumes constant amplitude and waveform during each ~1-year campaign. RZ Vel is a Blazhko Cepheid and several others show amplitude modulation; no period-derivative or cycle-to-cycle amplitude check is done despite the long baselines.
6. **Uncertainty underestimation.** The error on ΔRVcc is just the mean point-to-point RV error; it ignores phase-sampling, interpolation choice (pivot vs. direct), and systematic RV errors (instrumental zero point, telluric correction, calibration drift). The 2σ-looking 95–96%/0.96/0.97 phases near R TrA's maximum illustrate sparse sampling near extrema, which directly bias the max–min amplitude.
7. **Spline / pivot-point choices.** Amplitudes from a cubic spline depend on the knot strategy; two "equivalent" methods are claimed equal without a quantified difference, and the pivot-point solutions for Y Sgr/RS Pup rest on hand optimization.
8. **Wavelength study.** Orders are quietly dropped (59, 68, 72, "instrumental characteristics and/or unrealistic results") and the per-order amplitudes are velocity-shifted to match the global amplitude before fitting (Eq. 7), which forces the conclusion that only amplitudes change; order-to-order *profile* differences (line blends, fringes) are ignored.
9. **K-term attribution.** The claim "CC explains 50% of the K-term" assumes (a) Vγ[N08]'s center-of-mass calibration is correct (itself model-dependent, built on the γ-asymmetry ≈ 0 physical reference), (b) an axisymmetric Galactic rotation model, and (c) that old, heterogeneous GCD values (mixed methods/instruments over decades) are unbiased. With σ(intercept) ≈ 0.8 km s⁻¹ and reduced χ² ≈ 4, the 1.0 km s⁻¹ CC offset is only marginally significant.
14. **No error propagation beyond the fit.** Systematics (mask, interpolation, model) are never folded into the quoted ±0.05/±0.06 Pp coefficients; the relation is recommended for extragalactic distance work with effectively optimistic uncertainties.
11. **Extrapolation.** Applying a 3.4–41.5 d Galactic relation to LMC Cepheids (and by extension any program) assumes the linear log P form persists and that LMC metallicities (~1/3 Solar) shift nothing — metallicity is acknowledged as the biggest unknown and left untested.

### Assessment

This is a well-motivated "instrument-method correction" paper in a coherent series, with fully tabulated raw RVs (genuinely reproducible) and a clean experimental design. The physics logic — Gaussian-fit + line-mix biases on CC amplitudes, per-order wavelength test — is sound and the pcc relation is practically useful for BW work. Its weaknesses are statistical (8 stars, poor reduced χ², underestimated error bars) and generalization: the correction is demonstrated on one instrument, one mask, one S/N band, one metallicity regime, yet is proposed for general use, and the K-term result is a suggestive ~1σ-per-point offset rather than a firm effect.

## What the paper does

This is paper V of a series. Its argument chain is:

1. The **cross-correlation (CC) method** builds thousands of theoretical spectral lines and fits a single average profile, so its radial-velocity amplitude measures a *different* atmospheric layer than a single near-photospheric Fe I line. Therefore the "projection factor" suitable to CC differs from the semi-theoretical one in Paper II.
2. They measure, for 8 HARPS Cepheids, the amplitude of the CC curve (`ΔRV_cc`) and the near-photospheric amplitude at 4896 Å (`ΔRV_cc[4896]`), define a correction `f_cc`, and fit a **new period–projection relation for the CC method**: `p_cc = p·f_cc = [−0.08±0.05]log P + [1.31±0.06]` (reduced χ² = 1.2, 8 stars).
3. They check the **wavelength dependence** of `p_cc` (at 400 and 700 nm) and call it *negligible*.
4. They check the CC **γ-velocity** and conclude it is systematically ~1.0 km/s below the (Paper III) center-of-mass γ, and — the headline interpretation — that **the CC method accounts for ≈50% of the Cepheid "K-term"** (~2 km/s).

## The single weakest link

**Step 4 — the claim that the cross-correlation method is responsible for ≈50% of the K-term.** This is the step where the conclusion stops being entailed by the data.

The arithmetic behind it is: the *true* reference is the Paper III center-of-mass velocity `V_[No8]`; their CC γ is ~1.0 km/s below it (Eq. 12: `V_γ[CC] = 1.02·V_[No8] − 0.99`); the literature CC-compilation γ (`V_γ[GCD]`) is ~1.8 km/s below it; the historical K-term is ~2 km/s; therefore "1 out of ~2 km/s is due to the CC method." Several things break:

- **The baseline has no independent grounding.** `V_[No8]` is not a ground-truth γ-velocity — it is itself a *derived* center-of-mass velocity, obtained by correcting first-moment line velocities with γ-asymmetry linear relations fitted on a handful of stars, and it is defined *relative to an axisymmetric rotation model of the Galaxy*. The 50% figure is only valid if the true (asymmetry-corrected) Cepheid γ **equals** the rotation-model velocity, so that the entire 2 km/s K-term is a pure measurement artifact. That equality is an assumption borrowed from Paper III, not a result demonstrated in this paper, and Paper III's own correction relations rest on the same 8-star, low-SNR sample. There is no independent γ-velocity against which either the K-term or the CC bias can be checked.

- **Numerator and denominator share the same reference frame (circularity).** The K-term is a *population-level* discrepancy of Cepheids against a dynamical rotation model; the "CC bias" of ~1 km/s is a *per-star, zero-velocity* offset measured *against that same rotation-model-based calibration*. You cannot use a reference frame to measure an offset against, and then report that offset as a fraction of a discrepancy that is defined against the very same frame. If the true γ is offset from the rotation model by some (unknown) ε, the correct attribution is ~1.0/(2 − ε), not 1.0/2 — the data contain no handle on ε, so "50%" is not entailed.

- **The 50% is an arithmetic leftover, not a measured quantity.** It is `(total K-term) − (CC offset)`, with the residual 1 km/s labelled "K-term / other effects" by subtraction. Nothing in the sample identifies what that residual physically is, nor whether the K-term is really a constant 2 km/s across this period/Galactic-position range. The result is book-keeping, presented as a physical attribution.

In short: the Pp-relation result (steps 1–2) is actually cleanly supported by the fits, but the paper's most consequential interpretive conclusion hangs entirely on an unverified identity between the (uncalibrated-by-themselves) center-of-mass γ and the Galaxy's rotation model — a circular, self-referential step where the conclusion outpaces the data.

(Secondary, not the main crack: the "wavelength dependence is negligible" claim rests on two wavelength endpoints whose own zero-points, 0.99 and 1.02, sit a few percentages from unity — comparable to the ±6% zero-point uncertainty of the final Pp relation — so "negligible over the entire optical range" is not justified by the small evidence shown.)

## Scoring

- **Originality:** an incremental, well-executed methodological correction within an existing series (a method-specific Pp relation and a K-term reinterpretation). No new formalism.
- **Overall:** the core Pp fit is sound and clearly presented, the pipeline is careful, and uncertainties are reported — but the sample is 8 stars, the K-term interpretation is circular, and the wavelength claim is over-stated. A solid B-/low-B paper.
- **Impact:** a genuinely useful, if modest, correction for anyone applying the CC method to Cepheid distance scales (f_cc ≈ 0.93); the K-term story is contested and unlikely to redirect the field.

# Peer Review

**Paper:** Nardetto et al., "High resolution spectroscopy for Cepheids distance determination V. Impact of the cross-correlation method on the p-factor and the γ-velocities" (arXiv:0905.4540)

## 1. Summary

This paper (the fifth in a series on spectroscopy of Galactic Cepheids with HARPS) addresses a practical but consequential bias in the Baade–Wesselink distance method. The cross-correlation (CC) method, standard for deriving radial-velocity curves at low signal-to-noise, produces velocity amplitudes that differ from the line-centroid (first-moment) amplitudes on which the semi-theoretical period–projection-factor relation of Paper II was built. The authors define a "CC projection factor," apply the HARPS pipeline to 8 Galactic Cepheids, derive a new period–p relation suited to the CC method, check the wavelength dependence of the CC amplitude, and quantify the bias of CC γ-velocities relative to calibrated centre-of-mass velocities, partly disentangling the ~2 km/s Cepheid "K-term."

Main results: (i) a correcting factor f_cc = 0.93 ± 0.02 yielding p_cc = [−0.08 ± 0.05] log P + [1.31 ± 0.06]; (ii) a negligible wavelength dependence of CC amplitudes over the optical range (linear fit, slopes ≈ −0.005 to −0.011 km s⁻¹ nm⁻¹); (iii) CC γ-velocities are systematically blue-shifted by ≈1 km/s relative to calibrated centre-of-mass velocities, i.e. the CC method accounts for roughly half of the K-term, with the other half intrinsic (line asymmetries, Paper III).

## 2. Strengths

1. **Well-motivated and timely problem.** The projection factor is a known weak link in the infrared surface-brightness Baade–Wesselink technique, and most Cepheid RVs in the literature (including the Galactic Cepheid Database of Fernie et al. 1995) were obtained via cross-correlation. Deriving the p-factor *for the method actually used in the distance pipelines* closes a genuine consistency gap.

2. **Internal consistency.** The construction is clean: the p defined in Eq. (2) is explicitly γ-independent; the per-star f_cc values (0.90–0.96, Tab. 2) are consistent with a constant correction; the resulting p_cc for the 5-day period (≈1.25) agrees with Mérand et al. (2005)'s interferometrically based p = 1.27 for δ Cep, an important independent check. The paper also notes agreement with the rough CC estimate (slope −0.075) anticipated in Paper II.

3. **Novel, quantitatively useful by-products.** The wavelength-dependence study (Sect. 5) is a new observation and a sensible precaution before applying optical p-relations to NIR photometry in IRSB work; the γ-velocity dissection (Sect. 6) is the first quantitative split of the K-term into a method (CC) component and an intrinsic (atmospheric) component, which matters for Galactic structure work using Cepheid γ-velocities.

4. **Honest framing of limitations.** The authors explicitly defer S/N, resolution and metallicity effects to future work, and do not claim to fully explain the K-term.

## 3. Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Small, homogeneous sample vs. the main motivation.** Eight stars, one instrument (HARPS, R ≈ 120,000), one G2 mask, comparable metallicities and high S/N. The stated purpose of the new P–p relation is calibrating *LMC/SMC* Cepheid distances — i.e. metal-poor stars observed at much lower S/N and with different instruments/masks. Extrapolation from a solar-metallicity, high-quality sample to that regime is asserted rather than tested, and the conclusion that the relation "is a solid tool … especially to derive the distance of LMC Cepheids" is not supported by the data presented. At minimum, the discussion should quantify how plausible this extrapolation is given the known metallicity dependence of line depths and of f_grad.

2. **Statistics are thinner than the "relation" suggests.** With 8 points and a fitted slope of −0.08 ± 0.05, the slope is statistically consistent with zero; the result is essentially a *constant* p_cc ≈ 1.31 ± 0.06. Similarly, the claim (Sect. 4) that the zero-point shift from 1.376 ± 0.023 to 1.31 ± 0.06 is "significant" is marginal: the difference is 0.066 with a combined error ≈ 0.064, i.e. ~1σ of the *fitted* relations. The more defensible statement is that the eight individual f_cc ratios are consistently < 1 (~6% lower), which is what actually carries the signal. The paper should lean on that.

3. **Fit quality of the γ-velocity relations is poor and undisclosed.** The two linear fits in Sect. 6 have reduced χ² = 3.0 and 3.8 for 8 points — a clear mismatch that is never discussed. The derived offset errors also disagree across the paper: the abstract quotes "1.0 ± 0.2 km s⁻¹," Sect. 6 quotes "(0.9 ± 0.8) km s⁻¹," and the two independent offsets (−0.99 ± 0.17 and +0.86 ± 0.78) are combined in the text as "0.86 + 0.99 ≃ 1.8 km s⁻¹" with no error propagation and despite the internally inconsistent reduced χ². The K-term decomposition ("CC explains ~50%") therefore rests on fits of questionable adequacy and somewhat arbitrary bookkeeping of two inconsistent offsets.

### Minor

4. **Uncertainty treatment of f_cc.** The per-star f_cc scatter (0.90–0.96) is comparable to the quoted ±0.02–0.03 uncertainties, and these uncertainties are derived as *amplitude-ratio* errors; systematic contributions (interpolation method, mask, Gaussian profile fit, line-mixing effects) are plausibly of similar size but are not estimated. The ±0.02 on the mean f_cc is likely optimistic.

5. **Mask-related systematic.** All RVs come from the standard HARPS G2 mask; the dependence of f_cc and Vγ[CC] on mask choice (e.g. the HARPS "Cepheid-specific" or Geneva masks) is not examined, although the authors themselves list mask composition as a source of bias in the introduction.

6. **Interpolation and coverage.** Phase coverage is sparse for several stars (e.g. ℓ Car is observed only over phases ~0.01–0.70, RZ Vel is gappy; Y Sgr and RS Pup require pivot points). Given the whole analysis rests on amplitudes and means of the interpolated curves, the paper should show that the spline interpolation does not bias ∆RV_cc (e.g. by comparing with a different interpolation or with a sinusoidal fit). Fig. 1 helps, but amplitude-level checks would be more direct.

7. **References.** "Sabbey et al. 1995, ApJ, 446, 250" does not correspond to the cited work (Sabbey et al. 1996, ApJ, 460, 365); "Davis et al. 2009, MNRAS, tmp" is an in-press reference; Sect. 6 cites "Joy et al. 1939" (the reference is Joy 1939). "Kervella P., Nardetto N." has inconsistent comma usage. These should be fixed for publication.

8. **Wavelength dependence.** The linear fits ∆RV_cc(λ) = a_λλ + b_λ are reasonable at the ~100 nm scale probed, but the per-order RV curves have not been re-interpolated consistently (amplitudes are taken per order, then the *mean* curve is used as reference); the conclusion that the effect is "negligible" is fine for today's uncertainties, yet the effect is largest for long-period stars that will be the NIR photometry targets — the authors should say how many km/s this translates to in p for, e.g., a 20-day Cepheid in the K band.

## 4. Questions for the authors

1. Can you provide, even roughly, an estimate of how the f_cc correction depends on metallicity, or at least bound it (e.g. via the line-depth scaling of f_grad in Paper II), to justify applying Eq. (5) to LMC Cepheids?
2. Why are the reduced χ² values of the fits in Eqs. (11)–(12) so high, and how does this affect the error bars on the 1 kmm s⁻¹ CC γ-velocity offset?
3. Would a different RV mask, or a non-Gaussian line-profile fit, change f_cc measurably in your data?
4. For the sparsely covered stars, how much does ∆RV_cc change if one uses a simpler (sinusoidal) fit instead of the pivot-based spline?

## 5. Overall assessment

This is a careful, well-motivated calibration paper from a series that has substantially advanced the spectroscopic side of the Baade–Wesselink method. The core finding — that CC-based amplitudes are ~6–7% too large for the Paper II P–p relation, and that CC γ-velocities carry a ~1 km/s blue bias accounting for half of the K-term — is the kind of small, method-specific correction that quietly matters for the extragalactic distance scale. The execution is sound but the sample is small and homogeneous, the statistical significance of the "period dependence" is weak, and the K-term bookkeeping in Sect. 6 is the softest part. I would accept the paper after the major issues above (especially an honest restatement of the LMC extrapolation and the K-term error treatment) are addressed.

**Recommendation (for a prestigious journal):** Accept with moderate revision — the science is solid, well within the field's needs, and the corrections required are of presentation and scope-of-claim rather than methodology.

Overall quality is sound with a weak N=8 sample and some statistical inconsistencies → 3. Impact is high: the p_cc ≈ 1.3 relation for cross-correlated Cepheid RVs has been a widely adopted ingredient in infrared-surface-brightness/Baade–Wesselink pipelines and in the treatment of γ-velocities/K-term in Galactic structure work → 4.
