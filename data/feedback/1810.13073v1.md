## [1810.13073v1-general-review] General Review


Let me read the remaining pages.



I've read the full paper. Here is my evaluation.

## Overall assessment

This is a well-written mDPD (many-body dissipative particle dynamics) simulation study of liquid droplets on micropillar-textured hydrophobic surfaces. It pursues a well-motivated question — the "paradox" that, thermodynamically, the low-adhesion Cassie–Baxter state of a superhydrophobic surface is only *metastable* while the sticky Wenzel state is the *stable* minimum, so there must be a mechanism to eject a droplet that has fallen into the Wenzel state. The authors' answer, that the surface energy released when neighboring droplets coalesce can spontaneously drive a Wenzel-to-Cassie transition, is the paper's genuinely novel contribution and is a reasonable resolution of that paradox. The rest of the paper is largely high-quality *validation* of the mDPD machinery against known physics. The work is credible and competently done, but the central new claim is supported qualitatively and in a single configuration, and I think the paper overstates its generality in places.

## Points of strength

- **Clear framing of the paradox (Fig. 1c).** Recasting self-cleaning as a Wenzel-to-Cassie transition problem, distinct from the better-studied Cassie-to-Wenzel direction, is a useful reorganization of the problem and correctly motivates the study.
- **Genuine novelty in §3.3.** Demonstrating a coalescence-powered Wenzel-to-Cassie transition (Figs. 9–10) as a *mechanism that resets droplets out of the sticky state* is the substantive new idea, and it goes beyond the prior literature the authors cite, which has essentially all modeled jumping from an already-Cassie state.
- **Practical integrator correction.** The clarification that the MVV parameter λ is *system-dependent* (λ ≈ 0.55 for mDPD, not the borrowed Groot–Warren 0.65) and its demonstration in Fig. 2 is a small but genuinely useful contribution to the mDPD community.
- **Active-learning parameterization.** Using GPR + an acquisition function to map intrinsic contact angle to A_sl with only four runs (vs. "more than ten" before) is a neat and appropriate computational-efficiency move, with uncertainty bands shown (Fig. 3).
- **Solid validation against experiments.** Apparent contact angle and contact-angle hysteresis match Bhushan & Jung (Fig. 5) well, and the Oh-scaling of jumping velocity agrees with LBM/FVM/experiment (Fig. 8).

## Substantive criticisms

1. **The central claim rests on visual/kinematic evidence, not an energy budget.** The paper's thesis is that released coalescence surface energy (ΔE_s, Eq. 12) overcomes the Wenzel-to-Cassie barrier B₁. But the authors never actually compare ΔE_s to B₁ for the *Wenzel* case. They compute ΔE_s for the Cassie jumping case and note the energy-conversion efficiency η < 0.6% (i.e., >99% of the released energy is dissipated to viscosity, mostly because they are in 0.1 < Oh < 1). If less than 0.6% of the released energy even escapes as macroscopic motion, the argument that it is "sufficient" to push a *far more sticky* Wenzel droplet over B₁ is non-trivial and currently unproven. The transition is asserted from a snapshot sequence (Fig. 9) and a COM trajectory (Fig. 10). An explicit computation of ΔE_s(α, θ₀, R) versus B₁(α, θ₀) — perhaps a parameteric sweep — would convert the paper's headline claim from "plausible" to "demonstrated," and would be the single most valuable addition.

2. **The novel result is a single configuration.** §3.3 uses only P = 8.50 µm, R = 44.67 µm, θ₀ = 120°. The generality claim in the abstract/conclusion ("a possible physical mechanism") is extrapolated from one point. It is not shown how sensitive the transition is to the wetting-state factor α, droplet size, or intrinsic angle — precisely the parameters a designer would tune.

3. **The "spontaneous / without external energy" framing is doing quiet work.** In *both* the jumping and the Wenzel-to-Cassie cases, an artificial horizontal force is applied to force the two droplets into contact before the driving force is released. The transition is powered by coalescence energy only *after* that artificial contact. This is a physically defensible proxy (on a real condensation surface droplets coalesce on their own), but the strong words "spontaneous" and "without external energy inputs" in the abstract overstate what is simulated: the *transition* is spontaneous, the *bring-together* is not.

4. **The model's missing gas phase is central, not peripheral, to the central result.** In the conclusion the authors acknowledge the vapor phase is not a real gas (no air drag after jumping; no motion of trapped air beneath the droplet — they cite Attarzadeh for the latter). But the Wenzel-to-Cassie transition *literally* involves air being pulled out of the pillars from under the droplet (the whole mechanism, Fig. 9). The limitation that the model may not capture trapped-air dynamics therefore directly bears on the paper's main novel finding, not only on the secondary jumping case. It deserves to be foregrounded, not buried.

5. **The α = 1 threshold is used too sharply.** The authors treat α < 1 ⟹ Cassie and α > 1 ⟹ Wenzel as a crisp diagnostic ("generally believed…"). Real wetting-state selection is path- and history-dependent, and even α < 1 does not guarantee Cassie stability for sufficiently high intrinsic angle (the known wetting-on-hydrophilic / metastability subtlety). Fig. 5 already shows some scatter and a finite-width transition. Engaging with the fact that α is a *necessary but not sufficient* condition (rather than a switch) would make the §3.1 discussion more defensible.

6. **The "one order of magnitude" three-droplet claim is a fragile single point.** The headline 0.26 m/s (3 droplets) vs. 0.01 m/s (2 droplets) jumps ~26×, but only for θ_CB = 160°; the authors themselves say the difference is "not significant" for θ_CB = 150°, and the 0.01 m/s denominator sits near the viscous-dominated, low-signal regime. Leading the conclusion with "may increase the jumping velocity by one order of magnitude" overclaims a result that holds in one high-θ case.

7. **Minor: cross-method comparison at mismatched contact angles.** In Fig. 8 the FVM points are at θ = 155° and 170°, LBM at 160°, mDPD at 160°, so the overlaid Oh-curves are being compared at slightly different apparent angles, which weakens the point-by-point "agreement" a bit. The inset η(Oh) likewise overlays datasets at different θ.

8. **Parameterization range.** The active-learning map covers only 100°–130° of intrinsic angle (fine, and justified), while the rough-surface results use *apparent* angles of 150°–160°; this is not an error (apparent ≠ intrinsic), but the paper could state more explicitly that the A_sl values used for the textured runs sit inside the mapped GPR range.

## Bottom line

A solid, well-presented computational study whose one truly new idea — coalescence-release powering a spontaneous Wenzel-to-Cassie transition — is plausible, well-motivated, and potentially useful for biomimetic design, but currently demonstrated with qualitative evidence in a single geometry and resting on a model gas phase that is directly relevant to that phenomenon. An explicit energy-budget analysis (ΔE_s vs. B₁) and a small parameter sweep around the Wenzel-to-Cassie case would turn the paper's central contribution from suggestive to convincing. The jumping velocity and Oh-scaling portions, while competently executed, are essentially validation of known behavior rather than new physics, which makes the paper's overall novelty a tier below what its abstract implies.

## [1810.13073v1-expert-collegue-review] Expert Collegue Review


# Review: Zhang et al., "Self-cleaning of hydrophobic rough surfaces by coalescence-induced wetting transition" (arXiv:1810.13073)

## Summary of what the paper attempts

A Warren-type many-body DPD (mDPD) study of micro-pillar textured hydrophobic/hydrophobic-rough surfaces. Four pieces of work: (1) reproduce the Bhushan & Jung contact-angle and hysteresis data, (2) reproduce and extend the coalescence-induced jumping literature with a v*–Oh scaling across O(0.1–0.35), (3) show an ostensibly new observation that two coalescing Wenzel-state drops spontaneously transition to Cassie with no external stimulus, and (4) show that the initial arrangement of three drops controls the resulting spatial distribution of liquid tracers.

## What works well

- **Validation is unusually thorough for an mDPD paper.** Comparison to Bhushan & Jung (2007) for both 110°/120°/130° substrates with a clean W→C split near α≈1 (Fig. 5) plus a body-force sliding-angle proxy for hysteresis (Δθ_CAH ≈ 3.44° vs 49.87°) is strong. Comparing v*–Oh against LBM (Peng et al.) and FVM (Wasserfall et al.) and noting both the drop below Oh≈0.1 and the viscous-dominant Oh>1 behavior (Fig. 8) is the right comparison surface.
- **The active-learning GPR workflow is a genuine methodological contribution.** Using ~4 mDPD runs with a 1% acceptance tolerance to map θ₀↔A_sl, with the GPR prediction uncertainty as the acquisition function, is a reasonable and reproducible recipe that the mesoscale community needs more of.
- **The λ system-dependence observation (λ=0.55 vs 0.65 for MVV)** is small but the community has silently imported 0.65 from Groot & Warren for over a decade without checking, and the temperature-drift vs Δt diagnostic (Fig. 2) is the right way to settle it. Even if it's a minor point, it'll save people real wall-clock.
- **The energy-accounting honesty.** Admitting η<0.6% and citing Paulsen et al. (2012) to argue the process is viscous-dissipation-limited for 0.1<Oh<1 is the sort of self-criticism that makes a paper credible.

## What I'd push back on

1. **The "novel mechanism" is under-characterized by a single parameter point.** The W→C demonstration (Sec. 3.3, Fig. 9–10) is run at θ₀=120°, R=44.67 µm, P=8.50 µm (α=0.20→0.16). That's exactly one data point on an otherwise unmapped threshold. The paper would be much stronger with a ΔE_s vs α (or vs θ₀) sweep identifying a minimum released surface energy to break B₁, with a criterion of the form ΔE_s ≳ ΔF̃(W)−ΔF̃(C)+E_diss. As presented, the "paradox" framing in §1 over-promises: the reader is led to expect the paper resolves the W→C puzzle generally, but it shows one instance. The free-energy landscape of Fig. 1(c) is never numerically evaluated for these parameters — B₁ is only sketched. A string-of-states / nudged-elastic-band minimum free-energy path for a coalescing pair would have closed that gap.

2. **No experimental validation of the headline claim.** All the "novel" content is simulation. Coalescence-induced W→C transitions have been hinted at in other contexts (Liang 2016, Xie 2018, Zhao 2018 referenced in the bibliography); the paper should at least mention how this specific observation differs or aligns with those, or commit to a proposed minimal experiment (e.g., two-pinned Wenzel drops of defined size on a PDMS replica, high-speed imaging of the W→C moment). As-is, the W→C result is unverified by an external observer.

3. **Statistical uncertainty is mostly hidden.** The standard-deviation bars in Fig. 8 are a good start, but for the jumping velocity V_j=9.9 cm/s (Sec. 3.2) and for the W→C transition time (~162.5 µs, Sec. 3.3), the paper doesn't report the spread across independent runs. Five seeds at minimum, with the timing of the W→C moment (detected via pinned anchor release in Fig. 9 — what exactly is the criterion?) reported, would let a skeptical reader decide how robust the transition is.

4. **The "one order of magnitude" claim in §3.4 is self-undermined.** V_j=0.26 m/s (3 droplets) vs 0.01 m/s (2 droplets) for θ_CB=150° is 26×, but the authors immediately note the difference isn't significant for θ_CB=160°. Two droplets at θ=150° sits in the viscous-limited tail where the inertial-capillary law already says V_j should be small. The cleaner statement is "three-drop coalescence extends the inertial regime to larger Oh because more energy is released" — that's a more honest and more useful observation than "an order of magnitude." Also, V_j in §3.2 was 9.9 cm/s; in the four-droplet comparison in Fig. 12 the 2-drop curve at θ=160° peaks around 0.25 m/s, which is 2.5× higher — the comparison is being done at different θ and probably different R; the figure should make the (θ, R, P) tuple explicit.

5. **Vapor phase limitations, beyond what the authors already admit.** The authors honestly flag that the "vapor" is evaporated mDPD liquid, misses air friction on the jumping drop and the trapped-air motion under the drop (Boreyko & Chen 2009, Attarzadeh & Dolaatabadi 2017 cited). But the impact on the W→C transition specifically is more serious than on the jumping case: air trapped in the pillar gaps during W→C de-wetting is part of the dynamics. The single-pillar-gap geometry and the no-slip frozen-particle boundary method (Li et al. 2018) means the de-wetting of the liquid film under the drop is essentially a 2D problem; a comment on how the W→C timing would change if the vapor phase were given explicit pressure (or if the boundary were no-shear) would be useful.

6. **Eq. (12) for ΔE_s.** The prefactor 2πσ_l v and the bracket are not derivable from the standard Wenzel/Cassie ΔF formulation on first inspection; for a circular domain (1−ϕ−ϕ cos θ₀) sin²θ appears, but the 2−… term and R² outside look off by a factor. Please re-derive in a supplement; if it's a typo, the η numbers inherit that error.

7. **Multi-drop tracer distribution (Sec. 3.4).** The colored snapshots in Fig. 11 are a nice visualization, but the practical claim "non-contact method to manipulate liquids in small droplets" is aspirational. The "spatial distribution" of tracers is sensitive to the exact initial Δ₁₂, Δ₁₃ and β, which is also sensitive to numerics (the 2-step coalescence in Fig. 11(d) is essentially an ordering effect). A figure showing how the final component centroids shift as β sweeps from 170° to 190° would let someone decide whether the effect is robust to the experimental tolerances (a 1–2° error in drop placement) that any wet-lab implementation would have.

8. Minor: Fig. 4(b) is a static snapshot — the equilibrium shape after "short mDPD simulation to relax" with frozen vs frozen-pillar base is not quantified (contact line position, wetted fraction). A contact-angle-vs-α table for the three θ₀ at α∈{0.2, 1.0, 1.52} would make Fig. 5 easier to audit. And Eq. (11) for α is defined with √2·P − D, i.e. P measured center-to-center; the schematic in Fig. 4(a) labels P as center-to-center consistently, but other papers (e.g. Bhushan & Jung) label it as gap; the body text should state the convention unambiguously.

## Big picture

Coalescence-induced jumping is a well-trodden path by 2018 (Wang 2011, Lv 2013, Boreyko 2009, Waterfall 2017, Peng 2013, Attarzadeh 2017, Xie 2018, Liang 2015). This paper's distinct contributions are (i) the mDPD+GPR parameter workflow, (ii) the λ system-dependence observation, and (iii) a single demonstration that coalescence energy suffices to drive a W→C transition. (iii) is the right thing to study but it would be a stronger result with a threshold criterion, multiple parameter points, and some experimental corroboration or a more direct comparison to Liang 2016 / Xie 2018. (i) and (ii) are useful-to-the-community even if not field-moving. None of the three is "field-shaping." For a peer review, this reads as a solid journal article (PNAS/PRFlow/Langmuir quality) that's incremental in the larger picture; the validation story carries it.

## [1810.13073v1-critique] Critique


# Critique: "Self-cleaning of hydrophobic rough surfaces by coalescence-induced wetting transition" (Zhang, Li, Maxey, Chen, Karniadakis, arXiv:1810.13073v1)

## Summary

The paper uses many-body dissipative particle dynamics (mDPD) to study (i) wetting states (Cassie-baxter vs. Wenzel) of droplets on pillar-textured hydrophobic substrates, (ii) coalescence-induced jumping, and (iii) a coalescence-induced spontaneous Wenzel-to-Cassie (W2C) wetting transition, proposed as a natural self-cleaning mechanism that resolves the "stuck Wenzel droplet" paradox.

## Strengths

1. **Validation strategy.** Comparing apparent contact angle and contact-angle hysteresis against Bhushan & Jung's experiments (Fig. 5) for three substrate energies is the right level of validation for a mesoscopic model, and the quantitative agreement (e.g., Δθ_CAH ≈ 3.4° in Cassie vs. ≈ 4° experimentally) is credible.
2. **Methodological contributions worth their length.** Two items will be useful beyond this application: (a) the demonstration that the MVV λ is *system-dependent* (Fig. 2) — λ = 0.65, the "standard" value, drifts badly for mDPD here, and λ = 0.55 is preferable; this corrects a genuine over-generalization in the DPD literature; (b) the GPR-based active-learning parameterization (θ₀ vs. A_sl) that reduces required calibration runs from "more than ten" to four, with an uncertainty estimate carried in Fig. 3.
3. **Physical analysis is careful and consistent.** The Oh-number scaling of the dimensionless jumping velocity (Fig. 8) matches LBM and FVM data, the energy-conversion efficiency is quantified (η < 0.6%, consistent with the viscous-dominated coalescence regime of Paulsen et al.), gravity's irrelevance is shown (R ≪ ℓ_c), and the four-stage decomposition of the coalescence dynamics is clear.
4. The W2C mechanism itself is physically reasonable: a Wenzel droplet pinned in the gaps is released when growth by coalescence reduces its wetting-state factor below the gap-pinning limit (R = 44.7 μm, α = 0.20 → coalesced R̄ = 56.3 μm, ᾱ = 0.16).

## Weaknesses

1. **The headline claim is not experimentally supported.** The paper's central novel result — a *spontaneous* W2C transition driven by the surface energy released on coalescence (Sec. 3.3, Figs. 9–10) — rests on simulation alone. Worse, the two droplets are brought together by *externally imposed* horizontal driving forces (removed only after contact). The "spontaneous" qualifier applies only to the post-contact dynamics, yet the paper's framing ("without external energy inputs") does not adequately flag that the coalescence itself is artificially triggered. For a paper whose stated aim is to explain a real physical mechanism in nature, one would expect high-speed imaging of coalescing Wenzel-state condensate, or at least a parametric study of the threshold: how much release in ΔE_s is actually required, what is the critical coalesced size, and how sensitive is the transition to asymmetry in droplet sizes? None of this is quantified.

2. **No energy budget for the barrier.** The core claim is that the released surface energy "may be sufficient to cause a jumping motion" / to break the B₁ barrier. But B₁ is only sketched schematically (Fig. 1c); the released energy ΔE_s is never compared against the computed barrier height in this exact geometry. As it stands, the mechanism is observed (the droplet lifts and dewets the gaps) but not *explained in energy terms*, which is what the introduction promises.

3. **Novelty is undermined by a near-duplicate prior study that is under-played.** Reference [73] (Zhao, Chen & Liu, "Spontaneous wetting transition of droplet coalescence on immersed micropillared surfaces," *Appl. Math. Model.* 63:390–404, 2018) is essentially the same phenomenon — coalescence-induced spontaneous wetting transition on micropillars, in mDPD. It is cited in the paper only incidentally (for a contact-angle computation procedure, page 9), not as the closest related work whose difference (immersed vs. vapor-surrounding, immersed vs. air-exposed) must be made. The paper's "we demonstrate a novel self-cleaning mechanism" framing needs qualification: what this paper adds over [73] is the vapor-phase setting, the pillar-separation/α framework, and the connection to self-cleaning, not the transition itself.

4. **The surrounding gas phase is a proxy.** The vapor phase is mDPD fluid evaporated from the liquid, so it does not have the pressure, viscosity, or density of air. The authors acknowledge this, but it is not a minor point: it (a) under-represents air drag on the jumping droplet (the authors themselves note the observed post-jump velocity decay is missing), and (b) makes the W2C dewetting dynamics unreliable in detail, since the pull-out of liquid from the gaps depends on the counter-flow of gas beneath the droplet. Both of the flagship results are exposed to this modeling gap.

5. **The multi-droplet "order of magnitude" claim is over-stated.** The abstract says "case adding one more droplet to coalesce may increase the jumping velocity by one order of magnitude," which applies only to the θ_CB = 150° concentrated three-droplet case (0.26 vs. 0.01 m/s; V_COM peaks 0.22 vs. 0.26 m/s in Fig. 12 for 150°). For θ_CB = 160° the authors concede the difference is "not significant." The finding is real but a single geometry/angle corner of a small parameter space, and the paper never explores whether the enhancement depends on the specific β = 60° / 90° / 180° configurations tested.

6. **Limited statistical treatment of the event-driven results.** Fig. 8 carries error bars (five independent mDPD runs), but the coalescence-jumping (Sec. 3.2) and W2C-transition (Sec. 3.3) dynamics — the actual main findings — are presented as single realizations (Figs. 9, 10), with no statement of ensemble size, reproducibility of the pinned-anchor behavior at t = 60.9 μs, or variability of the transition time.

7. **Geometry and scale are narrow.** Monodisperse vertical pillars only; the conclusions section rightly admits hierarchical and soft-coating substrates are unexplored, but a Wenzel-to-Cassie mechanism on a *monodisperse* array, where α simply falls below the gap limit upon doubling of volume, risks being a geometrically inevitable outcome rather than a robust effect of the mDPD method. Testing the transition on a second (e.g., square) pillar array, or varying D/H rather than only P, would distinguish model/geometry artifacts from mechanism.

8. **Minor issues.** (a) The hysteresis "tilted plate" proxy applies a body force rather than an actual tilt — the resulting Δθ_CAH values agree with experiments, but the proxy's fidelity is not analyzed. (b) The λ = 0.55 choice is justified only by energy drift, not by an accuracy check (e.g., EOS values or interfacial tension at both λ). (c) Jumping *velocity* is compared to literature curves in dimensionless form; an absolute-value comparison against measured V_j (e.g., Boreyko & Chen) for their geometry would strengthen Sec. 3.2. (d) Fig. 3 uses four training points with 1% tolerance — fine for a one-parameter mapping, but the 95% confidence band over A_sl = −15 extrapolates past the last training point (−20), which is where the θ₀ = 150°–160° substrate of much of the paper would be used; the extrapolated region's validity is untouched.

## Verdict

This is a well-executed mDPD study whose incremental methodological pieces (λ clarification, GPR active learning) and standard validations (wetting state and hysteresis vs. experiment; v* vs. Oh scaling) are solid. The central Wenzel-to-Cassie claim is interesting and plausible, but it is under-demonstrated (single runs, no energy budget against B₁, artificially triggered coalescence, proxy vapor phase) and under-differentiated from the near-identical prior result of Zhao, Chen & Liu (ref. [73]). As submitted, it is a good journal-level simulation contribution to an active niche topic rather than a demonstration of a new mechanism — it would be substantially stronger with (i) an explicit ΔE_s vs. B₁ energy comparison, (ii) a threshold/criticality study of the transition, (iii) ensemble statistics, and ideally (iv) one experimental snapshot of coalescing Wenzel droplets.

## [1810.13073v1-novelty] Novelty of the contribution


## Stated contribution (in my own words)

The paper develops and uses a many-body dissipative particle dynamics (mDPD) model to study how liquid droplets wet and coalesce on micro-pillared hydrophobic surfaces. Its three deliverables are:

1. **Wetting-state characterization.** They map droplet wettability (Wenzel vs. Cassie-Baxter) to a dimensionless "wetting state factor" α, quantify contact-angle hysteresis in each state, and validate both against Bhushan & Jung's experiments. They also use a GPR + active-learning scheme to set the intrinsic contact angle with only ~4 simulation runs.

2. **Coalescence-induced jumping (Cassie).** Two equal droplets in the Cassie state coalesce and jump off; the dimensionless jumping velocity v* decreases with increasing Ohn, reproducing prior LBM/FVM/experiment trends.

3. **The headline result — spontaneous coalescence-induced Wenzel-to-Cassie transition.** Two droplets initialized stuck in the high-adhesion Wenzel state coalesce, and the surface energy released on coalescence is shown to be sufficient to pull the droplet out of the pillar gaps into the low-adhesion Cassie state with **no external energy input**. They propose this as a mechanism explaining why droplets on rough surfaces can self-clean. A related multi-droplet study shows the internal liquid-component distribution can be steered by droplet arrangement, and that a third droplet can raise jumping velocity by ~an order of magnitude.

## Novelty judgment vs. the cited prior work

**What is actually new.**

- The **coalescence-powered Wenzel→Cassie transition** is the one genuinely new result. The authors correctly note they had considered spontaneous W→C impossible [27, 30], and all prior wetting-transition work in their reference list is Cassie→Wenzel powered by *external* inputs (electrostatic, vibration, acoustic, pulse heating — [23–26]), or classical energy-barrier analysis [19–22]. Re-purposing the coalescence-released surface energy to *unstick* a Wenzel droplet into Cassie (rather than launch it into the air) is a previously unconsidered effect and a legitimately interesting observation.
- The multi-droplet **control of the internal spatial distribution of liquid components** (Fig. 11) is a small but fresh practical finding aimed at non-contact liquid manipulation.

## [1810.13073v1-methodology] Methodology reproducibility


## Paper

Zhang, Li, Maxey, Chen, Karniadakis, "Self-cleaning of hydrophobic rough surfaces by coalescence-induced wetting transition" (arXiv:1810.13073v1, 31 Oct 2018). A many-body DPD (mDPD) study claiming that surface energy released when two droplets coalesce is enough to drive a *spontaneous* Wenzel→Cassie wetting transition on a pillar-textured superhydrophobic substrate — offering a mechanism for why stuck Wenzel droplets can self-clean without external energy.

# 1. Methodology at the level of detail needed to reproduce the central experiment

## 1.1 mDPD model

- **Formulation (Warren 2003 mDPD).** Unit-mass particles, d²rᵢ/dt² = Fᵢ, with three pair forces. The density-dependent conservative force is
  Fᶜᵢⱼ = A·w_c(rᵢⱼ)·eᵢⱼ + B(ρᵢ+ρⱼ)·w_d(rᵢⱼ)·eᵢⱼ,
  with w_c(r)=1−r/r_c (attractive, A<0, cutoff r_c), w_d(r)=1−r/r_d (repulsive, B>0, cutoff r_d). Dissipative Fᴰᵢⱼ = −γw_D(rᵢⱼ)(eᵢⱼ·vᵢⱼ)eᵢⱼ and random Fᴿᵢⱼ·dt = δw_R(rᵢⱼ)dWᵢⱼ eᵢⱼ with fluctuation–dissipation δ² = 2γk_B T, w_D = w_R². Local densities use the 3D Lucy kernel w_ρ(r) = (105/16πr_cp³)(1+3r/r_cp)(1−r/r_cp)³. EOS: ρ = ρ_k T + α_MF(Aρ² + 2B r_d ρ³), which contains a van der Waals loop so vapor–liquid coexistence exists.
- **Fixed parameters (used in all runs):** A_sl = A_ll = −40.0, B = 25.0, γ = 6.0, δ = 18.0, r_c = 1.0, r_d = r_cp = 0.75, k_B T = 1.0. The liquid–solid attraction A_sl alone is varied to tune wettability.
- **Time integration:** modified velocity-Verlet ("MVV", Groot–Warren) with the tunable λ. The paper's first contribution is showing λ is system-dependent: with this EOS, the canonical λ = 0.65 lets k_B T drift with Δt (Fig. 2), while **λ = 0.55** is stable to larger time steps. (The actual Δt of production runs is *not* stated — a gap for reproduction.)
- **Physical mapping (Table 1):** fluid ≈ 60–65% glycerol/water at 20 °C; [m] = 2.29×10⁻¹⁵ kg, [L] = 2.37×10⁻⁶ m, [t] = 5.58×10⁻⁷ s, giving ρ = 1.16×10³ kg/m³, σ_LV = 6.85×10⁻² N/m, ν = 1.17×10⁻⁵ m²/s (μ = 13.6 cP). In DPD units ρ = 6.74, σ = 9.32, ν = 1.16 (each with ±0.0x uncertainties reported, presumably from the GPR runs).

## 1.2 Parameterization of wettability (active learning)

Instead of a full parameter sweep, the authors map the single tunable parameter A_sl to the intrinsic contact angle θ₀ (Young's law on a flat wall, θ₀ ∈ 100°–130° region of interest) using a **Gaussian-process-regression active-learning loop** (their prior work, Zhao et al. 2018, ref. [59]). Start with two mDPD runs (A_sl = −30, −20); the next run is placed at the maximum of the GPR standard deviation ε(A_sl); iterate until ε < acceptance tolerance (stated as ε₀ = 1%, i.e. δ_col = 0.01 in Fig. 3b). The final θ₀(A_sl) map over 100°–130° is built from **only 4 training runs**, each a flat-wall droplet (A_sl = −40, B = 25 etc., A_sl varied), θ₀ read at equilibrium. Three substrate materials correspond to θ₀ = 110°, 120°, 130°.

## 1.3 Substrate construction and central-protocol geometry

- **Texture:** square lattice of mono-sized cylinders, D = 3.79 µm, H = 7.58 µm, tunable center-to-center separation P. Built by cutting regions out of a thermal-equilibrium mDPD configuration; particle spacing δr = 1.25 µm, density 6.74. The base is a frozen plate, thickness 3.0 µm; no-slip boundaries via the arbitrary-geometry boundary method of ref. [66]. Quantified particle counts given: 237 µm × 237 µm substrate with 85,328 base (frozen) particles + 17,346 pillar particles (400 pillars, i.e. ~43 particles per pillar).
- **Wetting state factor:** α = (√2 P − D)²/(R H). α < 1 → Cassie favored, α > 1 → Wenzel favored (their Eq. 11).
- **Droplet:** R = 44.67 µm sphere (189,040 particles per droplet; 378,080 for the pair), released at the substrate center; periodic BC in x–y, no-slip at the wall. Equilibration: 100 time units to relax + 100 for statistics (single droplet); "a short simulation" for the two-droplet pair (duration unspecified). Gravity is dropped because R = 44.67 µm ≪ l_c = √(σ/ρg) ≈ 2.45 mm.

## 1.4 The central experiment (coalescence-induced Wenzel→Cassie transition, §3.3)

1. Choose P = 8.50 µm so that the two separated droplets (R = 44.67 µm) sit at α = 0.20, while the coalesced drop (R̄ = R·2^⅓ = 56.28 µm) sits at α = 0.16 — the Cassie state is energetically favored but the droplets start in/relax to the (metastable for this α? — actually the *stable, low-energy* sticky) **Wenzel** state.
2. Use an external body force on each droplet (following LaFuma–Quéré's switching protocol, ref. [18]) to push the system through a Cassie-Baxter excursion, then remove the force, then relax to equilibrium — the pair ends up in the Wenzel state (Fig. 9a snapshot at t = 0 shows the wetted grooves).
3. **Coalescence trigger:** apply "a pair of horizontal forces with small magnitude" in x–y to make the droplets approach; the force is switched off the instant the droplets contact and a liquid bridge forms.
4. **Observation:** the coalesced droplet accelerates upward (stage II, bottom-pressure effect), the liquid in the grooves is pulled out from between pillars, two pinned anchor points are seen at t = 60.9 µs, and by t = 162.5 µs the entire droplet is out of the gaps: a Wenzel→Cassie transition with no ongoing external energy. COM displacement dZ_COM and V_COM(t) (Fig. 10) are the measured outputs, split into four stages (I: 0–40.2 µs bridge formation; II: 40.2–97.4 µs acceleration; III: 97.4–208.6 µs deceleration under adhesion; IV: >208.6 µs settling into Cassie with oscillations).

## 1.5 Companion experiment used as the energetic benchmark (two-droplet jumping, §3.2)

Same setup but P = 11.85 µm (α = 0.50, θ₀ = 120°, apparent θ_CB = 160°). After the identical force-to-contact protocol, the coalesced drop **jumps off** the surface with V_j = 9.9 cm/s (contact at 86.5 µs, detachment at 190.5 µs). The same Ohnesorge-number sweep (R = 44.67 / 34.37 / 27.68 / 24.17 µm → Oh = 0.23 / 0.26 / 0.29 / 0.31, keeping α = 0.50 so P = 11.85 / 10.75 / 9.92 / 9.45 µm) compares dimensionless V* = V_j/√(σ/ρR̄) against 3D LBM (Wang et al.), FVM (Wasserfall et al.) and experiment; **5 independent mDPD runs per point**, error bars = std dev. Released surface energy from their Eq. (12); conversion efficiency η = 2(v*)²/(3A₀) < 0.6% (inset Fig. 8). A three-droplet study (β = 60°/90°/180°, concentrated vs spaced arrangements) follows with the same morphology and reports V_j = 0.26 m/s vs 0.01 m/s.

# 2. Assumptions and things the methodology papers over

**Acknowledged by the authors (stated, not fixed):**
1. The "vapor" phase is mDPD vapor (evaporated from the liquid), not air — wrong pressure, wrong viscosity; aerodynamic drag on a jumping drop and motion of entrained trapped air are both missed. They state this but quantify neither effect.
2. Only mono-sized, square-lattice cylindrical pillars; no hierarchical (lotus-like) structure or soft coatings.

**Papered over (implicit, unvalidated, or simply missing):**

3. **The force magnitude is never given.** The whole study hinges on "a pair of horizontal forces with small magnitude," applied until contact + bridge formation, then removed. No value, no time history, no sensitivity to the magnitude or to the release criterion. A reproducer cannot know how much kinetic energy/linear momentum the incoming droplets carry — which directly contaminates any attribution of take-off energy to "released surface energy."
4. **No energy budget for the central result.** The paper never computes the Wenzel-to-Cassie free-energy barrier B₁ (Fig. 1c is a hand-drawn sketch), and in the Wenzel transition case it does not evaluate ΔE_s at all (Eq. 12 is only used for the Cassie jumping case). The claim that the transition is "powered by the surface energy released upon coalescence" is therefore qualitative. Nor does the analysis separate the genuine energy effect from the mechanical pumping — the upward acceleration of the coalesced lump (visibly in Fig. 9–10) pulls the liquid out of the grooves regardless of the thermodynamic barrier.
5. **Δt of production runs is never stated.** λ = 0.55 was chosen from an isothermal (k_B T drift) test over Δt ∈ [10⁻³, ~10⁻²]; energy conservation, pressure/σ accuracy, and any drift at the actual Δt are not reported. 9. **Rough discretization of the pillars.** ~43 solid particles per pillar (diameter ≈ 3–4 particle cutoffs), walls built by "cutting" an equilibrated liquid; skin thickness, effective wall location, and whether the no-slip condition reproduces dynamic contact angles on such a coarse curvature are inherited from ref. 10. **Metastability protocol bias.** The Wenzel test cases require forcing a Cassie excursion and then relaxing to the equilibrium Wenzel state; any trial that did not settle into Wenzel (or that jumped during the relaxation) would simply be discarded — no success rate is reported.
11. **Hysteresis measurement details elided.** Δθ_CAH is obtained by pushing with a body force "until it just starts to move" — force value omitted; advancing/receding angles are extracted from local droplet curvature per refs. [57, 73] without method description.
12. **Minor but real inconsistencies:** Table 1 caption says "60% glycerol/water" while the text says "the 65% glycerol/water solution at 20 °C" (the quoted ρ, σ, ν are then ambiguous as to which standard dataset they must match); Fig. 7 caption puts contact at 86.5 µs and detachment at 190.5 µs while the stage boundaries in the text are 88.4 µs and 181.4 µs.
13. **The "one order of magnitude" claim is loaded.** 0.26 m/s (3 drops, θ_CB = 150°) vs 0.01 m/s (2 drops, θ_CB = 150°); at θ_CB = 160° the ratio is only ~2.6×, so the headline factor is chosen from the substrate where the two-drop baseline is viscously damped to near zero. Cross-comparisons with LBM/FVM also mix different θ and area fractions ϕ (0.08–0.126), so the Oh-slope agreement does not isolate the model prediction.
14. **No controls.** No force-free/flat-surface control to attribute the take-off momentum to coalescence energy; no direct simulation of the B₁ barrier; no check that the vapor remains saturated (or that evaporation over the ~1 ms Wenzel-transition run doesn't distort the drop).

# 3. Assessment

The study is a competent mDPD implementation: parameters are laid out fully (Eqs. 2–7, Table 1, unit mappings, geometry, particle counts), λ = 0.55 is a genuinely useful correction for density-dependent DPD, the GPR active-learning calibration is a clean efficiency trick, and results are validated decently against Bhushan–Jung experiments (θ and Δθ_CAH vs α) and against LBM/FVM for jumping velocity. But the *central* claim — spontaneous, energy-driven Wenzel→Cassie transition by coalescence — is presented without the two things that would make it quantitative: a computed transition barrier and an energy balance (released surface energy vs dissipation vs momentum contributed by the forcing protocol), plus no error bars on the key events. The missing force magnitude and Δt mean the central experiment cannot, as written, be closed-loop reproduced, and the vapor-phase and pillar-discretization approximations are inherited rather than checked. Solid incremental computational work whose headline mechanism remains plausible-but-unquantified, with the "order of magnitude" self-cleaning design insight resting on an unfavorable baseline comparison.

## My understanding of the paper's argument

The paper builds one claim from a chain, each link supposed to support the next:

1. **The paradox (motivation, p.2, Fig 1c).** Two wetting hypotheses for a rough hydrophobic solid: Wenzel (liquid fills the grooves, high adhesion, high hysteresis) and Cassie‑Baxter (droplet sits partly on trapped air, low adhesion). In the free‑energy landscape the Wenzel state is the *global minimum* with the Cassie state metastable, and the Wenzel→Cassie barrier `B1` exceeds the Cassie→Wenzel barrier `B2`. But a self‑cleaning surface *needs* the non‑sticking Cassie state, even though the landscape says the sticky Wenzel state is more stable. The authors flag this as a paradox: there must be a mechanism that ejects a droplet from Wenzel out to Cassie, and it cannot be thermally activated (that transition is effectively irreversible).

2. **The candidate mechanism (Sec 1).** Coalescence of neighboring droplets releases surface energy. On *super*hydrophobic surfaces that released energy is known to be enough to make a coalesced droplet jump. The new proposal: if two droplets are instead *stuck in the Wenzel state*, the same coalescence‑released surface energy may be enough to push the coalesced droplet over `B1`, flipping it to Cassie. That would be a genuine *self‑cleaning* pathway — no external energy (field/vibration/acoustic/heating), which all the earlier Wenzel→Cassie methods require.

3. **The tool (Sec 2).** Many‑body DPD with the Warren/Pagonabarraga‑Frenkel repulsive term, giving a van‑der‑Waals loop → vapor‑liquid coexistence + free surfaces + tunable contact angle. 5. **The two coalescence results (Sec 3.2–3.3).**

## Feedback on the logical argument

The narrative is coherent and the motivating paradox is a genuine, well‑chosen hook; that is the paper's real strength. But the argument has four load‑bearing gaps, three of them exactly at the central claim:

The "spontaneous" in the headline is doing more work than the simulations show.** The transition is not initiated spontaneously: in Sec 3.3 the droplets are "forced" together by imposed horizontal body forces, which are then removed at contact. ## Evaluation

**Paper:** Zhang et al., "Self-cleaning of hydrophobic rough surfaces by coalescence-induced wetting transition" (arXiv:1810.13073, 2018) — many-body DPD (mDPD) simulations of droplet wetting and coalescence on pillar-textured hydrophobic surfaces.

## [1810.13073v1-weak-point] Weakest link in the argument


**The weakest link: the causal attribution in §3.3 — upgrading "a Wenzel→Cassie transition was observed *during* coalescence" (Figs. 9–10) to "the transition is *powered by* the surface energy released upon coalescence," and from there to the headline "novel self-cleaning mechanism."**

The chain snaps at the Wenzel→Cassie step, for three reasons.

**1. There is no energy accounting in this case.** The paper's own framing (Fig. 1(c)) says a Wenzel-trapped droplet can only escape if something supplies free energy ≥ barrier B₁. But B₁ is never computed, the released coalescence energy ΔEₛ (Eq. 12 gives the tool) is never evaluated for this case, and the energetic cost of pulling liquid out of the grooves is never estimated. Contrast this with the companion jumping case, where they do quantify the conversion efficiency η (Eq. 13; <0.6% of ΔEₛ survives as translational kinetic energy, the rest is viscously dissipated). The Wenzel→Cassie claim therefore rests on a single snapshot sequence and a COM trajectory showing *temporal correlation* — the liquid is seen to be dragged out of the gaps in Stage III, *in step with the droplet's upward momentum*, i.e., a kinematic/viscous consequence of the coalescence impulse. Nothing in the figures separates "global energy release overcame B₁" from "local coalescence flow perturbed a metastable contact line until it de-pinned."

**2. There is no negative control, and the paper's own setup contradicts its "stable Wenzel" premise.** A Wenzel-locked droplet without coalescence is never simulated, so the assertion that it "cannot change to the Cassie-Baxter state without energy inputs" rests only on the generic schematic of Fig. 1(c). Worse: §3.3 reveals that at P = 8.50 µm (α = 0.20) the droplets had to be **force-pushed from Cassie *into* Wenzel** to prepare the initial condition. That is only possible if Wenzel is *metastable* (Cassie the minimum) in this geometry — consistent with their own Fig. 5 (α < 1 relaxes to Cassie) but flatly inconsistent with the "Wenzel is the more favorable and stable state" narrative used to motivate the paradox (which actually describes the α > 1 regime). Given a metastable Wenzel droplet, de-trapping on the µs–hundreds-of-µs scale by a vigorous local reconfiguration is the *expected* failure mode; demonstrating it would not entail the specific energy-release mechanism claimed, and the "novel mechanism" claim (coalescence *must* drive self-cleaning, rather than any adequate perturbation) is untested.

**3. The "self-cleaning" conclusion adds yet another untested step.** In Fig. 10(a) the de-trapped droplet never leaves the surface — dZ_COM settles at ≈13 µm, the resting height. No contaminant is ever present; "cleaning" is inferred solely from Fig. 5(b)'s hysteresis values (3.44° vs 49.87°), extrapolated to the transition product but never demonstrated.

(Secondary, but worth flagging: the "one order of magnitude" velocity boost from a third droplet is real at θ_CB = 150° (0.26 vs 0.01 m/s) yet declared "not significant" at 160° while Fig. 12 shows a difference there; and the non-physical vapor phase is an acknowledged, self-limited caveat.)

The data support the *phenomenon* (coalescence de-trapped droplets); they do not support the *mechanistic attribution* on which the novelty and the self-cleaning claim rest.

**Assessment.** This is a competent, well-validated mesoscale simulation study (GPR-informed parameterization, active-learning calibration, multiple cross-methods benchmarks). The central observation is genuinely new and plausibly correct — prior work focused on coalescence jumping *from* Cassie; trapping-escape from Wenzel is a fresh angle. But the load-bearing energetic claim of the abstract/conclusions is asserted, not established: no barrier height, no released-energy budget, no control run, and a self-contradictory stability narrative in the very regime demonstrated. That gap is fixable (compute B₁ and ΔEₛ, run a no-coalescence control and a fixed-energy perturbation control), which is why the paper lands at "strong but not excellent."

## Summary

The paper uses many-body dissipative particle dynamics (mDPD) to study droplets on micro-pillar textured hydrophobic surfaces. Four contributions are made: (i) GPR-based active learning to reduce mDPD calibration runs from >10 to 4; (ii) a clarification that λ = 0.55 — not the conventional 0.65 — is the optimal MVV time-integration parameter for mDPD systems; (iii) quantitative validation of apparent contact angle and hysteresis against Bhushan & Jung experiments across the wetting-state factor α; and (iv) the central result — a *spontaneous* Wenzel-to-Cassie transition driven by the surface energy released during two-droplet coalescence, with both droplets initially in the Wenzel (stable) state.

---

## Strengths

**The central physical insight is genuinely novel.** Every prior study of coalescence-induced droplet jumping (Wang et al. 2011, Lv et al. 2013, Wisdom et al. 2013, Peng et al. 2013, Artaezadeh & Dolatabadi 2017) assumed the droplets started in the Cassie-Baxter state. This paper shows that the surface energy released by coalescence (Eq. 12) is apparently sufficient to push a droplet *out* of the sticky, stable Wenzel state over the barrier B₁, ending it in the low-adhesion Cassie-Baxter state. This directly addresses the "paradox" raised in §1: why droplets on self-cleaning surfaces don't remain permanently stuck in the Wenzel state. If the mechanism is robust, it is a new design principle for superhydrophobic surfaces.

**Multi-method cross-validation of the jumping velocity (Fig. 8) is strong.** The mDPD results for dimensionless jumping velocity v* vs. Ohnesorge number Oh agree with both 3D multiphase LBM (Wang et al. 2013) and FVM (Wasserfall et al.), and are consistent with the experiments of Mulroe et al. (2017) and Wisdom et al. (2013). Error bars (std. dev. of 5 independent runs) are shown.

**The MVV λ clarification is a small but real technical contribution.** Many DPD/mDPD practitioners use λ = 0.65 by rote (the Groot & Warren default) without re-justifying it for their specific EOS. Figure 2 demonstrates empirically that for the mDPD EOS (Eq. 3), λ = 0.55 gives superior accuracy and stability, and this finding will be useful to the mesoscopic simulation community.

**The GPR active-learning scheme is a neat efficiency gain.** Replacing >10 mDPD calibration runs with 4, using an acquisition function based on GPR prediction uncertainty, is a well-motivated and cleanly executed strategy (Fig. 3, ε_A_sl = 0.01). The approach generalizes to other mesoscopic model calibration tasks.

**The energy-conversion efficiency analysis (Eq. 13, inset of Fig. 8)** is a nice quantitative touch: showing η < 0.6% confirms that the process is in the inertially-limited viscous regime for 0.1 < Oh < 1, consistent with Paulsen et al. (2016). This anchors the physics beyond just "it jumps."

**Stage-by-stage trajectory analysis** (stages I–IV in Figs. 7, 10) is clear and pedagogically useful. The COM displacement/velocity decomposition makes the physical stages (approach/bridge formation, pressure build-up, maximum velocity, adhesion-driven deceleration/detachment) transparent.

---

## Major Concerns

### 1. Generality of the Wenzel-to-Cassie transition is not established

This is the headline result, yet it is demonstrated for exactly one set of parameters: cylindrical pillars D = 3.79 µm, H = 7.58 µm, P = 8.50 µm, R = 44.67 µm, θ₀ = 120°. The paper should vary at least one of:
- **Pillar geometry** (vary H, D, P independently rather than just P)
- **Intrinsic contact angle** (θ₀ = 110°, 130° alongside 120°)
- **Droplet size** (R = 34, 45, 56 µm)

A quantitative criterion for when the transition occurs would substantially strengthen the paper. The physical argument that ΔE_s > B₁ is the necessary condition is stated (Fig. 1c) but is never made quantitative: B₁ is never estimated in mDPD energy units for this geometry, and ΔE_s (Eq. 12) is never actually computed numerically and compared to it. The reader is left with the qualitative "plausible" answer.

### 2. Single run for the Wenzel-to-Cassie transition

Fig. 9 and 10 show only one deterministic trajectory. No ensemble statistics are provided. Reproducibility with different random initial particle configurations should be checked and reported as error bars, especially since the mDPD particle positions within the substrate are randomized (the paper states "each cylindrical pillar is made of pre-equilibrated random mDPD particles"), which introduces stochasticity into the initial Wenzel state.

### 3. Vapor-phase representation

The authors acknowledge (last paragraph of Conclusions) that the "vapor" is not a physically accurate gas phase — it is just mDPD particles that evaporated, and has the wrong pressure, density, and viscosity. This means:
- The free-energy difference between the Wenzel and Cassie states is not exactly accurate, because σ_lv is matched but the vapor EOS is not.
- There is no aerodynamic drag on the jumping droplet, so absolute V_j values are slightly high.
- The energy barrier B₁ in the Wenzel state is not directly affected (it is a liquid-solid energy), but the ΔE_s released in the Cassie→Wenzel direction involves the liquid-vapor interface energy, which is the one quantity the mDPD gets right.

This is a known and accepted limitation of the mDPD model (Warren 2003). However, in §3.3 specifically, where the claim is that ΔE_s exactly drives B₁, the fact that the vapor does not have the correct compressibility could in principle affect the energy bookkeeping. The limitation is stated in the Conclusions but deserves a paragraph in §3.3 where the Wenzel-to-Cassie result is presented.

### 4. Potential discrepancy in Fig. 5(a) at large α

In the Wenzel regime (α > 1), the experimental data (blue diamonds) at α ≈ 1.5–2.5 sit at θ ≈ 60–70°, while the mDPD data (squares and circles) plateau near 115–120°. This is a discrepancy of roughly 50°. The paper attributes this implicitly to the fact that in the Wenzel state the apparent angle is the "measured" angle rather than the equilibrium Young angle, but does not discuss the large absolute offset. If the experimental system at large α has a different intrinsic chemistry or a different wetting state than what mDPD captures, the validation should be limited to the Cassie-Baxter regime, or the Wenzel-state discrepancy should be explicitly discussed.

### 5. Three-droplet jumping: mechanism unexplained

The claim that "adding one more droplet to coalescence may increase jumping velocity by one order of magnitude" (V₃ = 0.26 m/s vs. V₂ = 0.01 m/s for θ_CB = 150°, §3.4) is striking, but the paper does not *explain* why the equilateral-triangle three-droplet arrangement releases so much more kinetic energy for θ_CB = 150° while for θ_CB = 160° the difference is negligible. A plausible hypothesis is that for θ_CB = 150° the three-droplet geometry leads to a more symmetric, near-simultaneous coalescence that avoids the directional cancellation seen in two-droplet cases, but this is merely speculated, not analysed. A comparison between the three-droplet energy-release rate (dU/dt during stage II) and the two-droplet rate would clarify this.

### 6. The "non-contact manipulation" claim is not quantitatively supported

The abstract and Conclusions say multiple-droplet coalescence offers "new non-contact methods to manipulate liquids inside small droplets." This is an interesting conclusion from Fig. 11, but the evidence is qualitative: four snapshots showing different colored-component distributions for four configurations. A quantitative metric — center-of-mass offset of component 1 vs. 2, or a mixing index (e.g., the variance of local composition in a sub-region) — plotted as a function of β and Δ₁₃/Δ₁₂ would make this claim much more defensible and reproducible.

## Minor Issues

- **The wetting-state factor α (Eq. 11)** uses √2 P, which is specific to a square (orthogonal) lattice. For hexagonal packing, the maximum inter-pillar distance is different. The generalization should be noted.
- **Figure 2** shows the λ scan at one single parameter setting. It would be ideal to show that λ = 0.55 remains optimal across the full A_sl ∈ [−30, −20] range, not just at one point.
- **The GPR prior** (mean and covariance function) used in §2.3 is not specified. A reader wanting to reproduce the result would need this.
- **The acceptance tolerance ε_A_sl = 0.01** (stated as relative in the text: "ε_tol = 1%") is applied to the *relative* value of A_sl, which is in the range [−35, −20]. Since |A_sl| ≈ 25, ε = 0.01 corresponds to ~0.04% in absolute value, which is tighter than the stated 1%. The text should clarify this.
- **Units in Table 1**: the kinematic viscosity ν = 1.16 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s is listed for a "65% glycerol/water solution at 20 °C." Glycerol-water at 65% typically has ν ≈ 1.3–1.5 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s at 20 °C (per Zhang et al. and other references). This should be double-checked against the source (ref. 62).
- **The "89 µm droplet diameter"** stated in the Abstract is the *diameter* of R = 44.67 µm. This is fine, but the abstract uses "diameter 89 µm" and §3.3 uses R = 44.67 µm; both are consistent but a reader skimming might confuse this with the 89 µm being a radius.

## Summary of Feedback to the Student

You have something genuinely interesting here. The spontaneous Wenzel-to-Cassie transition result — if it is robust across the parameter space you claim — is a real physical insight that nobody else has documented. That is the part that would get this paper cited in a Nature Materials perspective. But right now, the paper presents one beautiful single trajectory for the most important result, without either (a) scanning parameters to show the transition is generic, or (b) making the energetics quantitative (computing B₁ and ΔE_s in mDPD energy units and showing ΔE_s > B₁ explicitly). Do both of these before submission.

The GPR calibration work and the MVV λ correction are solid but small — keep them, but don't let them distract from the central result. The three-droplet manipulation section is a teaser that currently has more claim than data. Either expand it with quantitative metrics or tone down the language in the abstract.

The vapor-phase caveat needs to appear in §3.3, not only in the Conclusions — the reader evaluating the Wenzel-to-Cassie claim needs to see it there. And the Fig. 5a discrepancy in the Wenzel regime at large α should be explicitly addressed, even if the explanation is "out of the experimental range of the substrate used."

Overall: this is a good piece of work with a strong central contribution that is not yet sufficiently robustly demonstrated. With the additional parameter scan and energy-barrier quantization, this becomes a publishable and citable result.
